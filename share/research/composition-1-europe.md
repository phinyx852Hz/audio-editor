# Composition / Europe and the Americas

## Organum

Organum is the earliest European polyphony: a plainchant with at least one voice added to it.
At first it was improvised: one singer read the chant (the vox principalis) while another added
a voice by ear (the vox organalis), usually the same melody a fourth or fifth below, starting
and ending on a unison with the chant. The first treatise to describe it and give rules for it
is *Musica enchiriadis* (c. 895); its companion *Scolica enchiriadis* treats it in more
detail and accepts octave doubling, which happens anyway when men and boys sing together. It
was sung to make the high feasts more splendid. Over the 11th and 12th centuries the added
voice grew independent of the chant, and at Saint-Martial in Limoges it sang melismas over held
chant notes.

The Notre Dame school, the composers working at or near the cathedral in Paris from about 1160
to 1250, while it was being built (1163–1238), gave
organum measured rhythm through the six rhythmic modes, repeating patterns of long and short
notes. Léonin compiled the *Magnus liber organi*, two-voice organa (organa dupla) on the
graduals, Alleluias and responsories of the church year, the first large collection credited to
one composer. In it three styles alternate: organum purum, where the chant is syllabic and the
tenor holds each note under a florid upper voice; discant, where the chant is melismatic and
both voices move in a rhythmic mode, the tenor always in the fifth mode, in long notes; and
copula, which Johannes de Garlandia placed "between organum and discant", a short cadential
link. Pérotin, a generation later, wrote replacement discant sections (clausulae) with new
tenor rhythms, and organa in three voices, where every part is modal, and four: *Viderunt omnes*
and *Sederunt principes*, sung at Notre Dame on New Year's Day 1198 and on St Stephen's Day 1199,
when a decree of Bishop Odon de Sully allowed organa in three or four parts. He also wrote
conductus on new texts, many by Philip the Chancellor. Most of what is known of both men comes
from Anonymous IV, an English student writing around 1275, who called Pérotin the best composer
of discant. Giving new words to the upper voice of a clausula made the motellus, the ancestor
of the motet. The repertory survives in three manuscripts: W1 (St Andrews, now at
Wolfenbüttel), F (Florence, Pluteo 29.1, the largest, with 462 clausulae) and W2 (Wolfenbüttel,
the latest, with the most motets); none is older than the 1230s, though the music was sung from
the last quarter of the 12th century. How strictly modal rhythm governed organum purum is still
debated, because the theorists who describe it, Garlandia, Franco of Cologne and Anonymous IV,
wrote two generations after it was composed: Willi Apel and William Waite read all of it as
modal, a view most scholars no longer hold. Not everyone liked the new style. John of
Salisbury, who taught in Paris in Léonin's time, complained in his *Policraticus* (1159) that the
singers' high and low parts, running scales and broken notes made worship sound like a concert
of sirens, though he allowed that in moderation it lifted the soul.

*In PHI*: Compose > Europe and the Americas > Organum holds a chant tenor in long notes under two
or three voices in the rhythmic modes, consonant with it on each long, then a discant clausula
with the tenor in the fifth mode; Pérotin's voices exchange their phrases.

## Isorhythm

Isorhythm ("same rhythm") builds a voice from a repeating rhythm, the talea, laid over a
repeating series of pitches, the color, of the same or a different length. It grew out of the
rhythmic modes and first appears in French motets of the 13th century, such as those of the
Montpellier Codex; the motets in the illuminated *Roman de Fauvel* (early 14th century) are among
the first built around it, and it became the main organising principle of the 14th-century
motet. The tenor, usually a fragment of chant, carries it: in the Kyrie of Machaut's *Messe de
Nostre Dame* (c. 1360) a color of 28 pitches runs through a talea of four durations seven times.
When the two lengths differ, they meet again only after several statements; a color of nine
notes against a talea of five realigns after five colors. Philippe de Vitry, whose treatise *Ars
nova* (c. 1322) named the new style, and Guillaume de Machaut are its masters. Later motets
restate the tenor in diminution, as in Johannes Alanus's *Sub Arturo plebs*, whose tenor runs
three times at the proportions 9:6:4, and extend isorhythm to every voice (panisorhythm), as
Dunstable, Ciconia and Du Fay did; a mass by Pycard in the English Old Hall manuscript sets its
upper and lower parts at 4:9 and contracts the lower through 12:9:8:6 until they align again.
The terms talea and color are medieval; the word isorhythm was coined by Friedrich Ludwig in
1904 for 13th-century music and later extended to Machaut. As an analytical idea it has been
applied to Peyote songs of some North American peoples and to music of India and Africa.

*In PHI*: the tenor's talea and color of different lengths, its second half in diminution, and
upper voices that repeat taleae of their own, with hockets at the joins.

## Species counterpoint

Counterpoint is the art of writing independent voices that sound well together. Species
counterpoint teaches it in five steps against a given melody, the cantus firmus: note against
note (first species), two notes against one (second), four against one (third), syncopations
across the beat, where dissonance is prepared and resolved (fourth), and a free mixture of
them all (fifth, florid). Johann Joseph Fux codified the method in *Gradus ad Parnassum*
(Vienna, 1725), a Latin dialogue between a master, Aloysius (for Palestrina), and his pupil
Josephus, and it has taught composers ever since, Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven among them.
Knud Jeppesen's *Counterpoint* (1931) refined it on the evidence of Palestrina's own music.

*In PHI*: Compose > Species Counterpoint writes against the MIDI clip shown in the mixer, or a
cantus firmus written by Fux's rules, each species in turn, in the Mode menu's mode.

## Fugue

A fugue is a contrapuntal piece built on one theme, the subject, which each voice enters with in
turn. In the exposition the subject alternates with its answer, the subject transposed to the
dominant (exactly, a real answer, or adjusted to stay in the key, a tonal answer), while the
voice that has finished goes on in counterpoint, often a regular countersubject. Episodes,
usually sequences on fragments of the subject, lead to middle entries in related keys; a
stretto overlaps the entries, and a pedal on the dominant or tonic can close it. J. S. Bach's
*The Well-Tempered Clavier* (1722 and 1742) and *The Art of Fugue* are the models; the
textbook "school fugue" of the Paris Conservatoire is set out in André Gédalge's *Traité de la
fugue* (1901).

*In PHI*: Compose > Fugue takes the MIDI clip shown in the mixer as its subject, or writes one,
then an exposition, episodes in sequence, middle entries, a stretto and a tonic pedal.

## Twelve-tone rows

Twelve-tone technique orders all twelve pitch classes in a row, and builds a piece from that row
and its transformations: the prime, its inversion (intervals turned upside down), its
retrograde (backwards) and the retrograde inversion, each on any of the twelve pitches, 48
forms laid out in a 12 by 12 matrix. Arnold Schoenberg formulated it in the early 1920s (the
Suite for Piano, Op. 25, 1921–23, is the first wholly twelve-tone work), and his pupils Alban
Berg and Anton Webern took it in different directions. Josef Matthias Hauer worked out a
twelve-tone method of his own at the same time. Milton Babbitt extended it in the United
States: combinatoriality (a row whose halves combine with a transformation's halves to make all
twelve notes), and time points, which serialise rhythm by the same numbers. After 1945 Pierre
Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen and others serialised duration, dynamics and timbre too
(integral serialism).

*In PHI*: Compose > Europe and the Americas > 12-Tone Row shows a row and its matrix and writes a
piece in one to four voices by Schoenberg's and Babbitt's methods.

## Stochastic music and sieves

Stochastic music is Iannis Xenakis's name for music composed with the laws of probability: masses
of sound (clouds of pizzicati, glissandi, grains) whose overall shape is chosen and whose single
events follow a distribution, as gas molecules follow the kinetic theory. Sieves are his later
tool for building scales and rhythms from residue classes, numbers that leave the same remainder.

Xenakis (1922–2001), an engineer and architect who worked with Le Corbusier, reacted against the
serialism of the 1950s, whose density he said the ear heard only as statistics. *Metastaseis*
(1953–54) and *Pithoprakta* (1955–56) were the first stochastic works; *Achorripsis* (1956–57)
used Poisson's law throughout, a matrix of seven timbres by 28 sections in which the number of
events in each cell follows the law, and from 1962 the ST pieces were calculated on an IBM 7090.
He set out the theory in *Musiques formelles* (1963), translated as *Formalized Music* (1971);
sieves came in *Nomos Alpha* (1965–66), which also orders its sections by the rotations of a
cube, and his article "Sieves" (1990). He founded EMAMu (1966, later CEMAMu) and built the UPIC
drawing system (1977) and the GENDY programs of dynamic stochastic synthesis (1991).

*In PHI*: Compose > Europe and the Americas > Stochastic Matrix fills an *Achorripsis*-style
matrix by Poisson's law, each cell a cloud of notes; Stochasticize the Clip adds a new line
beside the MIDI clip in the mixer by clouds, sieves and Markov screens; and Cubify arranges a
clip or the launcher's scene by the cube rotations of *Nomos Alpha* and Messiaen's modes.

## Chance and aleatory

Aleatory music (from Latin *alea*, dice) leaves some of its choices to chance, either when it is
composed or when it is played. John Cage composed by chance operations from 1951: in *Music of
Changes* for piano every sound, duration and dynamic was chosen by tossing coins to consult
charts of 64 cells, after the I Ching, and its sections follow square-root proportions. In
*4′33″* (1952) and the indeterminate scores that followed, chance moved into performance.
In Europe, Pierre Boulez ("Alea", 1957) and Karlheinz Stockhausen (*Klavierstück XI*, 1956) let
performers choose the order of fixed fragments. Witold Lutosławski heard Cage's Piano Concerto
on the radio in 1960 and developed limited aleatory, or aleatory counterpoint: in *Jeux
vénitiens* (1960–61) each player repeats a written figure at their own speed, out of time
with the others, until a cue from the conductor ends the section.

*In PHI*: Cage's chance charts cast by the I Ching in square-root sections, then Lutosławski's
ad libitum, every part looping its own figure out of time until a cue.

## Process music and minimalism

Minimal music builds pieces from repetition and slow, audible change. La Monte Young's long
drones and Terry Riley's *In C* (1964), 53 figures each player repeats at will over a pulse,
began it. Steve Reich found phasing in his tape pieces *It's Gonna Rain* (1965) and *Come Out*
(1966), two loops of the same recording drifting apart, and carried it to live players in
*Piano Phase* (1967); his essay "Music as a Gradual Process" (1968) asked for processes slow
enough to hear happening. *Drumming* (1971) builds patterns up beat by beat. Philip Glass
grew figures by additive and subtractive process, adding and removing notes in repeated
cells, in *Music in Fifths* and *Music in Twelve Parts* (1971–74) and the opera *Einstein on
the Beach* (1976).

*In PHI*: Compose > Europe and the Americas > Process Music runs phasing and build-up, additive
and subtractive process and a pulse on a cell; Minimalize a Scene arranges the launcher's scene
at the cursor by the same processes.

## Tintinnabuli

Tintinnabuli (Latin, "little bells") is the style Arvo Pärt arrived at in 1976, after years of
near silence, with the piano piece *Für Alina*. It pairs two voices: an M-voice (melody) moving
mostly by step around a central pitch, and a T-voice (tintinnabuli) that sounds only the notes
of the tonic triad, each time the triad note nearest to the melody note above or below it by a
fixed rule. Phrase lengths often follow the syllables of a text or grow by one note each time.
*Cantus in Memory of Benjamin Britten* (1977) is a mensuration canon, each voice at half the
speed of the one above and lower; *Fratres* and *Tabula rasa* (1977) and *Spiegel im Spiegel*
(1978) followed.

*In PHI*: an M-voice around a centre, phrases growing by a note, its T-voice, their mirror, a
bell and a mensuration canon, each voice slower and lower.

## Spectral music

Spectral music takes its harmony and form from the analysis of sound itself, its spectrum of
partials, as computers and the sonogram made it visible. Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail, with
Michaël Levinas and Roger Tessier, founded the group L'Itinéraire in Paris in 1973. Grisey's
*Partiels* (1975), part of the cycle *Les Espaces acoustiques*, orchestrates the spectrum of a
trombone's low E, each instrument a partial, and breathes between harmonic and inharmonic
sound; Murail's *Gondwana* (1980) builds its chords by frequency modulation. The partials are
not tempered, so the parts play in quarter tones and finer. Hugues Dufourt named the movement
in 1979; Kaija Saariaho and Magnus Lindberg carried it on, and IRCAM's tools gave it analysis.

*In PHI*: the harmonic series of a low fundamental as the harmony, each part a partial in its own
cents, each cycle an attack and its resonance, breathing from harmonic to inharmonic and back.
