# Composition / Africa

## Layered ostinato (Ewe drumming)

The dance-drumming of the Ewe people of southeastern Ghana and Togo, in pieces such as Gahu,
Adzrowo and Atsiagbekɔ (Agbekor), is built from layers of ostinato over a timeline. The iron bell
(gankogui) plays the timeline, an asymmetric pattern of seven strokes in twelve pulses, against
which every other part is heard; the rattle (axatse) doubles it or marks the beat. Supporting
drums enter one at a time, the kaganu, kidi and sogo, each repeating a short figure placed
against the bell, so that groupings of three and two pulses sound at once. The high kaganu keeps
time with bell and rattle, while kidi and sogo are response drums. The master drummer leads, on
the atsimevu in Agbekor or the boba in Gahu, and the lead drum's calls change what the response
drums answer and guide the dancers from section to section.

Gahu shows the form. It is social music, with no religious ties or set season, which Emmanuel
Agbeli of Kopeyia traces to Kokosawa, a Yoruba style from Nigeria that the Ewe played at more than
twice its tempo. The present Kopeyia arrangement opens with a short slow Kokosawa, then works like
a rondo: the "free movement" (A), a light, easy dance, is followed each time by the more intense
"serious movement" (B), which leads into a new variation, C, D, E and on to H, in turn (ABA,
ABCABC, ABDABD and so on), each variation cued by the lead drum. Adzrowo (Adzro), about 150 years old by Agbeli's account and often
heard at funerals, which in Ghana are largely joyful, uses the same timekeepers but gives the lead
to the atsimevu, with a small hand-played boba joining kidi and sogo as a response drum; its
call-and-response dialogues give it a similar theme-and-variations shape, but its dancers keep a
much simpler step, largely independent of the drumming, and older Ewe prize it for its
sophistication. Atsiagbekɔ, a warrior dance and one of the oldest in the Ewe canon,
retells in drumming, dance, song and chant, as Agbeli tells it, a small village's victory over a
far larger army some five centuries ago. Its supporting drums are kaganu, kidi, kroboto and
totodzi, and none leaves its ostinato: here the call and response runs almost wholly between
the atsimevu and the dancers.

The Ga of the coast around Accra built the same kind of texture into Kpanlogo, a young people's
recreational style of the late 1950s, the years of independence, which borrowed figures from
older Ga pieces such as Gome, Kolomashie and Oge and from highlife. The elder Nii Taki recalls
its first name, Gbajo, "storytelling": a story became a song and the song a dance. Its dancing
was thought so risqué that a more spaced-out version was made, which Kwame Nkrumah approved.
Bell (ngongo), rattle (ashakashaka), finger castanet (ododompo) and one or two square frame drums
(tamalin) keep time under three kpanlogo drums, the lead tuned highest. There is no set order of
variations; the music starts slow and quickens, and in a traditional setting the lead drummer
watches the dancers and varies to fit them while the support drums respond. A cantor and chorus
sing over it in call and response. David Locke's studies and those of A. M. Jones made the
style the textbook case of African polyrhythm; Kofi Agawu has warned against hearing it only as
rhythm, apart from dance, language and meaning.

C. K. Ladzekpo, an Anlo-Ewe master drummer who taught at Berkeley, explains how the textures
are built. A period of twelve pulses holds a main beat scheme of four beats, each three pulses
long, the beats the dancer's feet keep; set against it, a secondary scheme of six two-pulse
beats makes the basic six-against-four cross rhythm (three against two at the half period). Its
half-period motif starts where the two schemes coincide, a moment of resolution, and moves into
conflict; shifting the six-beat scheme one pulse later gives a variant that starts in conflict
and resolves on the second and fourth main beats. Children learn these by singing them, a
syllable for coincidence (kpla), for main beats (tu) and for secondary beats (ka), and in a
game two children sing the basic pattern and its variant at once until one loses the thread.
Composers overlap such textures and set different motifs one after another within a period:
the basic motif followed by its variant, with one note split in two for a smooth join, is the
source of the familiar seven-stroke bell timeline; a pickup on the third main beat gives a
second seven-stroke form, and leaving out a note gives the five-stroke line common in sacred
dance-drumming. Learners tap the four main beats with feet and one hand while the other hand
plays the timeline. Eugene Novotney's thesis argues more broadly that the 3:2 relationship
underlies the timelines of much West African music and, as clave, of the music of the African
diaspora, and that these rhythms are best understood as integrated wholes rather than sums of
groupings; he also sorts out the often confused terms polyrhythm, cross-rhythm, beat and pulse.

*In PHI*: the bell's twelve-pulse timeline, then rattle, kaganu, kidi and sogo entering one by
one, three against two, the lead drum's calls changing their responses, with cantor and chorus.

## Hocket and interlocking

In hocket a melody is shared out among several players, each sounding only some of its notes,
so that it exists only when their parts interlock. Among the Banda-Linda of the Central African
Republic, ensembles of side-blown horns, each made to sound one pitch, play cyclic melodies this
way, each player repeating a figure of his own; the Aka (BaAka) forest people sing interlocking
yodelled polyphony. Simha Arom transcribed these ensembles by recording each part on its own
track while the musicians heard the rest, and showed in *African Polyphony and Polyrhythm*
(1985; English 1991) how each figure varies within the cycle while the whole stays the same.
Gerhard Kubik named the "inherent patterns" that listeners hear from the interlocking that no
single player plays. The same technique appears in medieval European hocket, in the Shona
mbira, where two players interlock, and in Balinese kotekan.

Elsewhere in Africa, Wikipedia names hocketing among the Ba-Benzélé (whose hindewhu whistle-and-
voice figure Herbie Hancock borrowed for the 1973 "Watermelon Man"), the Mbuti, the Basarwa
(San), the Gumuz of the Blue Nile region of Sudan, and the Gogo of Tanzania, and points to the
Bigwala gourd trumpets of Busoga in Uganda. Its article carries a request for more citations, so
those names are a starting point rather than a settled list.

Colin Turnbull's notes to his Ituri forest recordings of the 1950s, written in 1957–61, describe
how the Mbuti do it. Each singer takes one or two notes and hoots them at the right moment, so the
melody passes from voice to voice, note by note, and builds a harmony as it goes. Turnbull says
this is used mainly in hunting and honey-gathering songs, sung on vowels, since the sound matters
more than the words. In the elephant-hunting song a soloist gives the story and the chorus answers
in this way, then the solo singer drops out and the hocket carries on. He describes the melodies
as resting almost entirely on the dominant, with an occasional seventh, falling in shape, over a
single chord, so the skill lies in improvising within narrow limits. The Mbuti also sing rounds
(*rondisa*), stretching or shortening the phrase to suit the number of singers as it passes
anticlockwise round the circle. They keep no drums of their own, a burden for people on the move,
and borrow them from villagers; the beat comes from sticks struck together, split sticks called
ngbengbe, and clapping. In her 1991 introduction to the reissue, Michelle Kisliuk notes that Alan
Lomax used Mbuti hocketing as evidence that an egalitarian society makes an interlocking music,
and that Charles Keil questioned the comparison. She also notes that hocketing and yodelling are
shared across many forest peoples, while their repertoires differ a good deal.

The name is French: *hoquet*, a hiccup or sudden catch, latinised as *hoquetus* or *ochetus*.
Sanders, writing in *New Grove*, sets aside an older idea that the word came from Arabic. In
Europe the technique belonged to the Notre Dame school of the 13th and early 14th centuries,
first in sacred music and later in secular songs, where two upper voices traded notes and rests,
usually on a vowel, as in the textless *In seculum* pieces. Later examples include Louis
Andriessen's *Hoketus*, split parts in drum corps, Andean siku panpipe pairs, Ukrainian
and Russian kuvytsi and Lithuanian skudučiai panpipes, handbell choirs, Haitian rara and
Dominican gagá bands, Javanese imbal, and guitar pairs that split one line between them.

*In PHI*: a cyclic melody shared out note by note among up to eight horns, each with one pitch,
their figures interlocking, then each figure varying within the cycle while the melody stays.

## Congolese rumba and the sebene

Congolese rumba (also called African rumba or rumba lingala) grew up in the twin cities of
Kinshasa and Brazzaville from the late 1930s. Its roots are in maringa, a Bakongo couple dance
from the old Loango kingdom, played on the likembe (thumb piano), a bottle struck with a rod
and a small frame drum, the patenge; accordions and acoustic guitars replaced the likembe in
the 1920s. In the 1940s, Cuban son records from EMI's G.V. series (Sexteto Habanero, Trio
Matamoros) were heard on the radio, often labelled "rumba", and the name stuck. Musicians moved
piano, trumpet and drum parts onto guitars and saxophones. Kazadi wa Mukuna argues that the
music stayed maringa underneath, with Latin forms used mainly as models for learning the new
instruments. Franco Luambo made the same point: the horns sing vocal lines, the melody follows
the tones of Lingala, and the guitars are African. UNESCO inscribed the genre in 2021.

A song typically opens slowly with the singers, in 4/4 at a moderate tempo, then breaks into the
sebene, a faster instrumental section of repeated, interlocking guitar figures over the cavacha
drum pattern (credited to Zaïko Langa Langa's drummer Meridjo Belobi around 1970). Here the
singers turn to short chanted phrases, the atalaku shout cues to the dancers, and the guitars
take over, usually cycling the I–IV–V–IV progression in a major key. The guitars have set roles:

- **Accompagnement** (rhythm) holds the song's basic cyclic pattern, low in the ensemble.
- **Mi-solo** ("half solo") sits between, often in arpeggios and fingerpicked figures.
- **Solo** (lead) plays fast, high lines near the top of the neck, answering the singers in the
  slow part and carrying the melody in the sebene.
- **Bass** locks to the clave-like beat and outlines the harmony.

Sources differ on where the mi-solo came from. One credits Faugus Izeidi; another links the term
to the three-guitar line-ups of the late 1960s, such as Docteur Nico, Dechaud and De La France in
African Fiesta Sukisa. Credit for the sebene itself is also argued: it goes variously to West
African migrant bands in the 1930s, Antoine Kasongo's orchestra in the late 1940s, Wendo, Henri
Bowane or Manuaku Waku. Franco's OK Jazz made the sebene the heart of the song rather than a
break between choruses, and soukous later dropped the sung story altogether for the dance.

Other features: vocal harmony mostly in thirds; call and response between singer and chorus,
singer and instrument, and between instrument sections; horns that punctuate rather than lead;
improvisation built on motifs, often in thirds and sixths. Early guitarists of the late 1940s
and 1950s often used an open tuning (D-G-D-G-B-D) with a capo, prized for its buzz.
