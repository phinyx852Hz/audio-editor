# Frame Drums

## The frame drum

A frame drum is a drum whose head is wider than its shell is deep: a skin stretched over a
shallow hoop of bent wood, so thin that the shell does not resonate and the sound is the
skin's own. It is among the oldest instruments of which there is a record. The head is usually
rawhide (goat, calf, fish, lizard, reindeer or caribou), tacked, glued, laced or sewn to the
frame, and on many modern drums it is plastic and tuned with screws. Some frames carry jingles,
rings, chains or snares; some are square, octagonal or oval; some have a handle, a crossbar or a
thumb hole.

Because the shell does not resonate, every sound comes from where and how the skin is touched.
A stroke near the centre gives a low, open tone; a stroke at the edge a high, dry one; a finger
on the inside of the skin raises or bends the pitch; a snap of the fingers against the rim
gives the sharp accents of the daf and the riq. The same hoop of skin is a bass drum, a snare
and a rattle at once, which is why the frame drum is played in nearly every tradition with
the hands alone.

Three ways of holding it are now distinguished. Held upright in one hand and played with the
fingers of both, as the tar, bendir, daf and riq are played; held flat or on its edge and struck
with a beater, as the bodhrán and most shamans' drums are; and laid on the lap, the "lap style"
of the modern frame drum movement, which frees both hands to play as on a tabla.

## The ancient frame drum

The frame drum appears in the art and texts of the earliest cities. Small frame drums were
played in Mesopotamia, Egypt and Israel, and then in Greece and Rome, where the Greek drum was
the *tympanon* and the Latin the *tympanum*; they belonged above all to the cults of mother
goddesses, Inanna and Ishtar, Astarte, Isis and Cybele, and to the rites of Dionysus. The
Hebrew Bible's *tof* is the drum Miriam takes up to sing at the crossing of the Red Sea
(Exodus 15:20), the drum of the women who meet David returning from battle, and of the
prophets' ecstatic processions. In Sumer large frame drums were played in the temple rites. In
the Middle East the frame drum has stayed largely a women's instrument, and it accompanies
recitation of the Qurʾān. Crusaders brought it to Europe in the 13th century, where as the
timbrel or tabret it too was played mainly by women, to accompany song and dance.

Most of what is known comes from images: reliefs, painted vases, figurines of women holding a
round drum flat against the chest. The skins and frames themselves rarely survive. A small
Ptolemaic-era frame drum skin painted with a woman playing before Isis is among the few that do.

## When the Drummers Were Women

Layne Redmond (1952–2013), a drummer and student of Glen Velez, spent some fifteen years
gathering the images of frame drummers in the religious and healing rites of the ancient
Mediterranean world, and published them in *When the Drummers Were Women: A Spiritual History of
Rhythm* (1997). Nearly all the drummers in the images she collected are women; she argued that
the frame drum was the principal percussion instrument of the region from about 3000 BC to
AD 500, that it was the instrument of priestesses, and that its loss to women followed the decline
of the goddess cults and the rise of the churches that forbade them.

The book made the history of the frame drum widely known and brought many women to the drum.
Its larger claims, about a time when women led the religious life of the ancient world, are
Redmond's interpretation and are not settled scholarship; its value is the images, which are
real and which had been little noticed. Remo's first Signature Series drum was a frame drum
designed by Redmond. She was inducted posthumously into the Percussive Arts Society Hall of Fame
in 2021.
