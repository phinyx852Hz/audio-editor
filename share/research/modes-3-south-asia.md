# Modes / South Asia

## Ancient India (Nāṭyaśāstra)

The earliest Indian system of melody: two parent scales (grāmas), ṣaḍja-grāma and
madhyama-grāma, of seven svaras measured in 22 śrutis to the octave, rotated into seven
mūrchanās each, from which the jātis, melody types, were made.

The *Nāṭyaśāstra*, credited to Bharata (between about 200 BCE and 200 CE), is a treatise on
drama with music in its chapters 28 to 33. Matanga's *Bṛhaddeśī* (c. 9th century) first used
the word rāga for a melody type; the grāma system gave way to rāga by the thirteenth century,
when Śārṅgadeva's *Saṅgītaratnākara* summed up the old theory.

- [Natya Shastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natya_Shastra) and
  [Shruti (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shruti_(music)), Wikipedia.
- Lewis Rowell, *Music and Musical Thought in Early India* (University of Chicago Press, 1992).

## Hindustani (North India)

The classical music of North India and Pakistan: rāgas grouped into ten thāṭs, sung or played
over a drone, in forms such as dhrupad, khayal, ṭhumrī and instrumental ālāp-jor-jhālā, with the
tāl cycles of the tabla or pakhāvaj.

It grew from the music of the temples and of the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal courts, where Persian
and Indian music met: Amīr Khusrau (13th–14th centuries), Tānsen at Akbar's court. The
gharānās, schools of style, formed in the courts of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries;
Bhatkhande and Paluskar brought the music to public schools and concerts in the twentieth.

- [Hindustani classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_classical_music), Wikipedia.
- Bonnie C. Wade, *Khyāl: Creativity within North India's Classical Music Tradition* (Cambridge
  University Press, 1984).
- Joep Bor (ed.), *The Raga Guide* (Nimbus, 1999).

## Karnatak (South India)

The classical music of South India: rāgams ordered under the 72 melakartas, compositions (kṛtis)
by the composers of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the gamakas (ornaments) that make
each rāgam, and the tāḷam cycles of the mṛdaṅgam.

Purandara Dāsa (1484–1564) laid out its teaching; Venkaṭamakhin's 72 melakartas (1620) ordered
its scales; the Trinity of Tyāgarāja, Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar and Śyāma Śāstri (late 18th–19th
centuries) wrote its core repertoire. The Madras Music Academy (1928) and its December season
made Chennai its centre.

- [Carnatic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnatic_music), Wikipedia.
- T. Viswanathan and Matthew Harp Allen, *Music in South India* (Oxford University Press, 2004).

## Hanumat mata

The rāga-rāginī system: six male rāgas (Bhairava, Kauśika, Hindola, Dīpaka, Śrī and Megha), each
with five or six wives, the rāginīs, and sons, a family of melodies tied to seasons, times of
day and moods. The Hanumat mata (school of Hanuman) is one of four such classifications.

The systems appear in treatises from the fourteenth century and are best known through
rāgamālā paintings, series of miniatures that portray each rāga as a scene, made in the Rajput,
Deccani and Mughal courts from the sixteenth century. Bhatkhande's thāṭs replaced them in
teaching.

- [Ragamala paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragamala_paintings), Wikipedia.
- Klaus Ebeling, *Ragamala Painting* (Ravi Kumar, 1973).

## Bharata

The sage to whom the *Nāṭyaśāstra* is credited, the oldest Indian treatise on theatre, dance and
music, compiled between about 200 BCE and 200 CE. Its music chapters define the 22 śrutis, the
grāmas and mūrchanās, the jātis, the instruments and the tāla of its time.

The text was commented on by Abhinavagupta (c. 1000), whose *Abhinavabhāratī* is the main key
to it. Its theory of rasa, the emotional flavour of art, still shapes Indian aesthetics.

- [Bharata Muni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharata_Muni), Wikipedia.
- *The Nāṭyaśāstra*, trans. Manomohan Ghosh, 2 vols (1950–61).

## Venkaṭamakhin

A South Indian scholar of the seventeenth century at the Thanjavur court. His
*Caturdaṇḍīprakāśikā* (1620) showed that 72 seven-note scales, the melakartas, could be made from
the twelve notes by fixed rules, and so ordered every rāgam.

Govindācārya in the eighteenth century named the 72 and made each a complete scale; it is the
system Karnatak musicians use today.

- [Melakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melakarta), Wikipedia.

## V. N. Bhatkhande

Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande (1860–1936), a lawyer from Bombay, travelled across India to collect
compositions from the gharānās and published them in notation in *Kramik Pustak Mālikā*
(1913–37). He grouped the Hindustani rāgas under ten thāṭs (parent scales), still the standard
classification.

He held the first All-India Music Conferences (from 1916) and founded music colleges in
Gwalior, Baroda and Lucknow (Marris College, 1926, now Bhatkhande University).

- [Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu_Narayan_Bhatkhande) and
  [Thaat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaat), Wikipedia.
