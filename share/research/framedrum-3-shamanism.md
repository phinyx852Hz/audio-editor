# Frame Drums / Shamanism

## The shaman's drum

Across the northern world, from Sápmi through Siberia and Mongolia to the Inuit Arctic and the
Americas, the shaman's instrument is a frame drum. Shamanism, a word taken from the Evenki
*šaman* of Siberia, names the practices in which a specialist enters an altered state to travel
among the spirits, to heal, to divine and to guide the souls of the dead; and in almost every
northern tradition it is the drum that carries the shaman there. The drum is called the
shaman's horse, or reindeer, or boat; the beater is the whip; its sound is the hoofbeat of the
journey, and its steady pulse what draws the shaman out of ordinary consciousness and brings
him or her back.

Mircea Eliade's *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (1951; English 1964), the founding
comparative study, put the drum at the centre: made from the wood of the world tree, it lets the
shaman fly to the centre of the world, where the tree joins earth, sky and underworld. Later
scholars have pulled back from his sweeping claims. Åke Hultkrantz, in "The Drum in Shamanism"
(1991), called Eliade's view that the drum is indispensable to shamanism hard to accept, since
many shamans have worked without one, while still calling him the foremost scholar of the
subject. The Finnish folklorist Lauri Honko put the opposite view in a phrase (1964): "from
Altai to Lappland the drum is the liturgical handbook of shamanism".

Across the north the drum is nearly always a frame drum: in Alaska, Greenland, Sápmi and
Siberia alike, a sliver of wood bent into an oval, egg-shaped, round or angular hoop, with a
skin over it and a handle behind. The drums of Greenland, Alaska and the Chukchi have a handle
that sticks out from the frame; the Altai peoples carved theirs as a human figure hung with
strips of cloth. Among the Chukchi, the Russian ethnographer Waldemar Bogoras found that almost
every adult had a drum and beat it for pleasure at home, and distinguished the "family
shamanism" that every household performed by day from the shaman's own séance, at night and in
the dark.

## Siberia and Central Asia

The Siberian drum is a broad, shallow frame of bent wood, round or oval, with a single head of
reindeer, elk or horse hide, held from behind by a handle or crossbars and hung with iron
pendants, bells and rattles that sound with every stroke. Its skin is often painted with the
three worlds, upper, middle and lower, and the spirits who live in them, and its handle carved
with the face of an ancestor or helping spirit. A new drum is not an instrument until it has been
brought to life: in a ceremony of "enlivening" the drum's spirit is woken, after which no one else
may touch it. Among the Tuvans, the Buryats, the Altai and the Mongols the drum is the horse on
which the shaman rides to the spirit world. Under Soviet rule drums were confiscated and burned
and shamans persecuted; since the 1990s shamanism has been openly practised again in Tuva,
Buryatia and Mongolia.

The Khakas drums of the Minusinsk valley on the upper Yenisei show how much a single drum
holds. Grigory Potanin sorted Siberian drums by their handles into an Altai-Sayan, a Tungus and
a Samoyed type; the Khakas drums are of the first, large, up to 75 cm high, with a wooden
handle behind. Two drums the Finnish geologist J. J. Sederholm brought home from Siberia in
1917, studied by Victoria Soyan Peemot a century later, are Kača Khakas drums of the early 20th
century. The larger, the "Painted Drum", is 60 cm high on a frame of aspen 12 to 14 cm deep.
Six knobs under the skin are its "horns" or "nipples", which feed the shaman and the spirits.
An iron bar through the handle, the *kipic* or "bowstring", hung with bells, rattles and cloth,
and six iron blades on the frame, the "sabres", are the shaman's weapons. A zigzag border
around the skin is the "sky-belt", read by different shamans as the Milky Way, the shaman's road
across the sky, a bridge or a rainbow, and lines across it divide the skin into the upper,
middle and lower worlds, peopled with sun and moon, trees, birds, animals and riders on
horses who are the shaman's guardians. The Soviet scholar Sergei Ivanov counted about 70 known
Khakas drums.

## The Sámi drum

The Sámi of northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia had two kinds of drum, both oval and
both headed with reindeer hide: a bowl drum, its skin strapped over a hollowed burl, called *goavddis* in
Northern Sámi, and a frame drum of thin bentwood, called *gievrie* in Southern Sámi. The *noaidi*
used it to enter trance, when his free spirit left the body to visit the spirit world, and to
divine: a small pointer of brass or horn, the *vuorbi*, was set on the head, and where it stopped
among the figures painted there, of gods, animals, people and the sun, the future was read.
Louise Bäckman has noted that divining with a pointer on the drumhead seems to be known only
among the Sámi; other peoples divined with the drumstick or a staff. Cords hung with metal
rings and charms, tied to the frame, rattled as the noaidi beat the skin with a hammer.

The figures were painted in red, with a dye boiled from alder bark. On the South Sámi frame
drums they ran in one band around the edge with the sun, *biejjie*, in the centre; on the bowl
drums of the north they were set in rows, in fields that stood for the worlds the noaidi
travelled between: the sky above, the earth below it, and sometimes an underworld beneath.

The oldest mention is in the *Historia Norvegiae* of the late 12th century, which describes a
drum painted with whales, a boat, reindeer and snowshoes. In the 17th and 18th centuries
Lutheran missionaries confiscated and burned the drums, and noaidi were tried for witchcraft;
between 70 and 80 drums survive, most in museums, the largest collection in the Nordic Museum
in Stockholm. Some have come home. The drum of Anders Paulsen (Poulsen), who played it before
the witchcraft court at Vadsø in 1692 and explained its figures, passed in January 2022 from
the National Museum of Denmark to the Sámi museum in Karasjok, where it had been on loan since
1979: the first Sámi object returned to Sápmi from abroad. The drum is today a symbol of the
Sámi people.

Some drums were never found by the missionaries. Three South Sámi frame drums have turned up in
the Norwegian mountains of Helgeland and Nord-Trøndelag, each hidden under a rock. The
Henriksdalen drum, found in 1969 by Kristine Andersen Vesterfjell, belonged to her grandfather
Nils Johan Johannessen Vesterfjell, who died in 1871. The Røyrtjønna drum was found by three men
on a coffee break in 1993. The Visten drum was found by two brothers out hunting in 1997, with
its hammer, a brass pointer, a small white stone that probably served as a second pointer,
copper buttons given to the drum as gifts, and scraps of cloth. Its pinewood frame, worn by long
use and mended with copper, has been radiocarbon dated to after 1530 and its hammer to after
1685: a drum perhaps a century or two old when it was hidden, and the more trusted for its age.

Sámi makers now build drums again, drawing on the forms and figures of the 17th- and
18th-century drums. Francis Joy interviewed two of them. Elle-Maaret Helander, from Utsjoki in
Finnish Lapland, came to drum making through a course in core shamanism in 1997 and a drum
building course at the Sámi education centre in Inari; she paints figures still tied to the old
worldview and does not make drums as souvenirs. Ovlla Gaup of Kautokeino in Norway made a
replica of Anders Paulsen's drum, a bowl drum, after a handicraft course at the Sámi duodji
school in Jokkmokk in 2001.

## The Arctic and the Americas

The **qilaut** of the Inuit, a broad hoop of wood with a handle and a head of caribou skin, is
struck on the rim, not the skin, with a short stick as the drum is turned in the hand; its name
means "that by means of which the spirits are called up". The shaman, the *angakkuq*, used it to
call the spirits; the drum dance it now accompanies, with song, is the centre of Inuit
festivity. Further south, the hand drums of many Native American peoples carry song, dance and
healing, and are treated as living beings with a voice of their own.

## Drumming and trance

Does the drum itself cause trance? In 1962 the psychologist Andrew Neher argued that it does.
A flickering light near the brain's alpha rhythm, 8 to 13 cycles a second, was known to build up
the brain's electrical waves and pull them to its own rate. A drumbeat, Neher reasoned, holds
many frequencies at once, so it reaches more of the brain than a pure tone, and mostly low ones,
which the ear can take loud without pain, so it carries more energy. In his laboratory,
drumming changed the electrical activity of the brain's auditory region, and his subjects
reported unusual perceptions; some twitched. He predicted that trance ceremonies would drum at
just under 8 to 13 beats a second, and found support in A. M. Jones's analyses of African dance
drumming, at 7 to 9 a second, and in Haitian Vodou drumming at 8 or 9. Fatigue, hunger and
overbreathing, he added, would make dancers more susceptible. The idea was taken up widely and
is still repeated. The ethnomusicologist Gilbert Rouget answered it at length in *La musique et
la transe* (1980; *Music and Trance*, 1985). Neher's
laboratory drumming was steady, Rouget observed, while the drumming of real ceremonies changes
constantly; those who enter trance learn to, in a setting that expects it, while others hearing
the same drums do not; and "if Neher were right, half of Africa would be in a trance from the
beginning of the year to the end". For Rouget music is the trigger of trance only within a
culture's expectations, and the relation between music and trance takes as many forms as there
are cultures.

Jörg Fachner's review (2011) weighs both. Neher's laboratory drums beat at 3, 4, 6 and 8 a
second, in the brain's theta range rather than the alpha range of flicker; 8 to 13 beats a second
is hard for one drummer, though a group can reach it by interlocking their strokes, and
sequencers can now hold it indefinitely, which is how the idea came back in rave culture, to
explain trance on the techno dance floor. Brain waves do follow outside rhythms, but Neher read
his result by eye from the EEG trace rather than by measurement, and no one has replicated it; a
2006 Stanford symposium on brainwave entrainment presented no replication. What is better
established is the body's part: in long drumming and dancing, movement, breath and heartbeat fall
into step, and the pressure swings this sets up in the blood lower cortical arousal and raise
theta activity.

## Core shamanism and the modern drum

The anthropologist Michael Harner, who studied with the Shuar and Conibo of South America, drew
from many traditions what he called "core shamanism", the practices he believed they share, and
taught them in *The Way of the Shaman* (1980) and through the Foundation for Shamanic Studies,
founded in 1985. Its central practice is the shamanic journey to a steady drumbeat on a
single-headed frame drum, at about five beats a second; Harner made the first journey drumming
tape so that his readers could journey without a drummer. Core shamanism and the neo-shamanism
around it brought the frame drum to many Westerners, alongside the frame drum revival of Glen
Velez and Layne Redmond. Anthropologists and many Indigenous people have criticized it for
lifting practices out of the cultures that made them.
