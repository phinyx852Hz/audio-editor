# Acoustic Ecology / Anthropology and science

## Steven Feld

Steven Feld (born 1949) is an American anthropologist, ethnomusicologist, recordist and
musician, who studied with the linguistic anthropologists of his generation and the
ethnographic filmmaker Jean Rouch, and taught at the Universities of Pennsylvania and Texas,
New York and Columbia Universities and the University of New Mexico. From 1976 he worked
among the Kaluli people of Bosavi, in the rainforest of Papua New Guinea, and his study of
them is the founding work of the anthropology of sound.

He coined acoustemology (acoustic epistemology) for knowing the world through sound: not
sound as a sign of something else, but listening as a way of being in and understanding a
place. His later work turned to the bells of Europe (*The Time of Bells*, five CDs,
2004–06), and to jazz in Ghana, with the musicians of Accra (*Jazz Cosmopolitanism in Accra*,
2012), on his label VoxLox.

- [Steven Feld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Feld), Wikipedia.
- [Steven Feld](https://www.stevenfeld.net), his own site, with his recordings and writings.
- Steven Feld, "Waterfalls of Song: An Acoustemology of Place Resounding in Bosavi, Papua New
  Guinea", in Steven Feld and Keith Basso (eds), *Senses of Place* (School of American Research
  Press, 1996).

## The Kaluli and Sound and Sentiment

The Kaluli (Bosavi people) live on the slopes of Mount Bosavi, a volcano in the Southern
Highlands of Papua New Guinea, in longhouse communities in dense rainforest where one hears
far more than one sees. Feld's *Sound and Sentiment: Birds, Weeping, Poetics, and Song in Kaluli
Expression* (1982; 2nd ed. 1990; 3rd ed. 2012) showed how their song, weeping and poetry are
built from the sounds of the forest, above all its birds, which the Kaluli hear as the
voices of the dead.

Its key is the myth of "the boy who became a muni bird": a boy refused food by his older
sister turns into a muni bird (the beautiful fruit dove) and cries a falling, descending
call, the model of Kaluli sung-texted weeping and of their songs. In the gisalo ceremony,
studied before Feld by the anthropologist Edward L. Schieffelin (*The Sorrow of the Lonely
and the Burning of the Dancers*, 1976), a dancer sings of the places of the forest until
the listeners weep, and in grief burn him with torches. Feld's analysis joined the sounds of
place, the names of places and the structure of song into one system.

- [Kaluli people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaluli_people) and
  [Mount Bosavi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Bosavi), Wikipedia.
- Steven Feld, *Sound and Sentiment* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 1982; Duke University
  Press, 3rd ed. 2012).
- Edward L. Schieffelin, *The Sorrow of the Lonely and the Burning of the Dancers* (St. Martin's
  Press, 1976).

## Lift-up-over sounding

Lift-up-over sounding (Kaluli *dulugu ganalan*) is the Kaluli aesthetic of sound that Feld set
out in 1988: voices, instruments, birds, insects and water overlapping and interlocking, none
starting or ending together, each in and out of phase with the others, never in unison. The
forest itself sounds this way, and Kaluli song and work-song sound with it, so that music and
environment are made in the same grain. Feld compared it to the "groove" of African-American
music, and it became a model for thinking of a soundscape as a texture without a single
centre.

- Steven Feld, "Aesthetics as Iconicity of Style, or 'Lift-up-over Sounding': Getting into the
  Kaluli Groove", *Yearbook for Traditional Music* 20 (1988).
- [Kaluli people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaluli_people), Wikipedia.

## Voices of the Rainforest and the Bosavi recordings

Feld's recordings carried the Kaluli soundscape to listeners outside anthropology.
*Voices of the Rainforest* (Rykodisc, 1991), produced by Mickey Hart of the Grateful Dead in
his *World* series, composes a day in Bosavi, from dawn to night, in an hour, the
forest's birds, insects and water with the Kaluli's songs, work and play, as a soundscape
composition rather than a catalogue; its royalties went to a fund for the Bosavi people.
*Rainforest Soundwalks: Ambiences of Bosavi, Papua New Guinea* (EarthEar, 2001) gives four
unedited soundwalks; and *Bosavi: Rainforest Music from Papua New Guinea* (Smithsonian
Folkways, 2001, three CDs) gathers the music Feld recorded from 1976 to 2000.

- [Steven Feld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Feld), Wikipedia.
- [*Bosavi: Rainforest Music from Papua New Guinea*](https://folkways.si.edu/bosavi-rainforest-music-from-papua-new-guinea/world/album/smithsonian),
  Smithsonian Folkways.

## Anthropology of sound

After Feld, many ethnographers studied how a people's music and sense of place come from the
sounds around them. Anthony Seeger's *Why Suyá Sing* (1987) followed the songs of the Kĩsêdjê
(Suyá) of Brazil; Marina Roseman's *Healing Sounds from the Malaysian Rainforest* (1991), the
Temiar, whose songs are given by spirits of the forest in dreams; Tina K. Ramnarine and Feld's
students carried the work on. In history, Emily Thompson's *The Soundscape of Modernity*
(2002) and Alain Corbin's *Village Bells* (1994) are histories of soundscapes in Schafer's
sense, and sound studies, with Jonathan Sterne's *The Audible Past* (2003), grew in part from
debate with Schafer's view of recording as loss.

- [Ethnomusicology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnomusicology) and
  [Sound studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_studies), Wikipedia.
- Anthony Seeger, *Why Suyá Sing: A Musical Anthropology of an Amazonian People* (Cambridge
  University Press, 1987).
- Marina Roseman, *Healing Sounds from the Malaysian Rainforest: Temiar Music and Medicine*
  (University of California Press, 1991).
- Emily Thompson, *The Soundscape of Modernity* (MIT Press, 2002).

## Bernie Krause and biophony

Bernie Krause (born 1938), an American musician (a member of the Weavers and, with Paul
Beaver, of the synthesiser duo Beaver & Krause) turned natural soundscape recordist, divided
the soundscape by its sources: **biophony**, the sounds of living things; **geophony**, wind,
water, earth; and **anthropophony**, the sounds of humans. His niche hypothesis (from the
1980s) holds that in a healthy habitat each species calls in its own band of frequency and
time, so that together they fill the spectrum like the sections of an orchestra, and that a
damaged habitat shows as gaps in it. His archive Wild Sanctuary holds recordings of thousands
of habitats, many now gone, which his *The Great Animal Orchestra* (2012) describes; the
Fondation Cartier in Paris made an installation of it (2016).

- [Bernie Krause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernie_Krause) and
  [Biophony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophony), Wikipedia.
- Bernie Krause, *The Great Animal Orchestra: Finding the Origins of Music in the World's Wild
  Places* (Little, Brown, 2012).

## Soundscape ecology and ecoacoustics

Soundscape ecology made acoustic ecology a field science. Bryan Pijanowski, Almo Farina and
colleagues named it in "Soundscape Ecology: The Science of Sound in the Landscape" (*BioScience*,
2011): the study of the sounds of a landscape, biological, geophysical and human, as a measure
of its ecological state. Ecoacoustics (from about 2014) gathers the methods: passive acoustic
monitoring, recorders left in a habitat for months, and acoustic indices computed from
spectrograms (the acoustic complexity index, the bioacoustic index, acoustic entropy), which
estimate how many voices a place holds without naming each species. Coral reefs, rainforests
and cities are now surveyed this way.

- [Soundscape ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soundscape_ecology) and
  [Ecoacoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecoacoustics), Wikipedia.
- Bryan C. Pijanowski and others, "Soundscape Ecology: The Science of Sound in the Landscape",
  *BioScience* 61/3 (2011).
- Almo Farina, *Soundscape Ecology: Principles, Patterns, Methods and Applications* (Springer,
  2014).

## Natural quiet

The protection of quiet is acoustic ecology's politics. Gordon Hempton, an American acoustic
ecologist, founded One Square Inch of Silence (2005) in the Hoh Rain Forest of Olympic National
Park, Washington, a marked spot he argued should be kept free of human noise, and wrote *One
Square Inch of Silence* (2009) with John Grossmann; he later co-founded Quiet Parks
International (2019), which certifies quiet places. The US National Park Service's Natural
Sounds and Night Skies Division measures and manages park soundscapes, and noise is studied as
a pollutant of human and animal health.

- [Gordon Hempton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Hempton),
  [One Square Inch of Silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Square_Inch_of_Silence) and
  [Noise pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_pollution), Wikipedia.
- Gordon Hempton and John Grossmann, *One Square Inch of Silence* (Free Press, 2009).

## Listening as ethics

From Schafer onward, acoustic ecology has held that how we listen is an ethical matter: that
attending to a place, its creatures and its people is a form of care, and that noise is not
only a nuisance but the sound of who is allowed to be heard. Westerkamp and Oliveros made
listening itself the practice; Feld asked who records whom, and whose voices a recording
keeps, which is why the royalties of his Bosavi recordings went to the Kaluli; and recent
writers turn the question to the voices of species and lands that cannot speak for
themselves in human courts.
