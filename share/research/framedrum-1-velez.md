# Frame Drums / Glen Velez

## Glen Velez

Glen Velez (born 1949, Dallas, Texas) is the American percussionist, singer and composer more
than anyone responsible for the frame drum's return to the West. Of Mexican American family, he
grew up in Texas, played jazz drums, studied classical percussion at the Manhattan School
of Music and with Fred Hinger, served as a percussionist in the U.S. Army's 1st Armored Division
Band (1968–70) and settled in New York, where he toured and recorded with Steve Reich and Musicians from 1973 to 1993
(he is on the first recording of *Music for 18 Musicians*, 1978) and with the Paul Winter
Consort from 1983 to 1998. His recordings with the Paul Winter Consort brought him several
Grammy awards.

From the 1970s he turned to the hand drums of the world and above all to the frame drum, seeking
out teachers from one tradition after another. The bodhrán, pandeiro, riq, bendir, tar, kanjira,
tamburello and the Azerbaijani ghaval are his instruments; he also sings overtones and South
Indian konnakol, and composes for cross-cultural ensembles, often in several meters at once. His
first album, *Handdance: Frame Drum Music* (Nomad, 1984), was recorded with Layne Redmond; others
include *Internal Combustion* (1985), *Seven Heaven* (1987), *Assyrian Rose* (1989), *Doctrine of
Signatures* (1994), *Rhythms of the Chakras* (1998) and *Breathing Rhythms* (2000). John Cage
wrote *Composed Improvisation No. 3 for One-sided Drums With or Without Jangles* (1989) for him. He has taught
at the Mannes School of Music and The Juilliard School, and the Percussive Arts Society inducted
him into its Hall of Fame in 2014.

## Velez's teachers and traditions

Velez learned each drum from players of its own tradition, and kept each one's technique
distinct: as he has said of the kanjira and the riq, each is played in its own way. The
Percussive Arts Society names among his teachers:

- **Ramnad Raghavan**, the South Indian mridangam master, then artist in residence at Wesleyan
  University, for the **kanjira** and the drumming of Carnatic music; and later **Trichy
  Sankaran** and **T. H. Subash Chandran**, also masters of South Indian rhythm.
- **Hanna Mirhige**, for the Arab **riq**; Velez first played alongside him in New York's belly
  dance classes. And **Michel Merhej Baklouk**, the Lebanese riq master.
- **Dom Um Romão** and **Erasto Vasconcelos**, for the Brazilian **pandeiro**.
- **Zevulon Avshalomov**.
- **Alessandra Belloni**, for the southern Italian **tamburello** and **tammorra**.
- **Ephat Mujuru**, the Zimbabwean mbira player; **Kepa Junkera**, the Basque trikitixa player;
  and **Ed Harrison**.

He studied the instruments' history as well as their playing, writing on the frame drums of
ancient Western Asia from 1980.

## A unified technique: Handance

Velez's contribution was not only to learn the traditions but to join them. He took the strokes
of each, such as the South Indian split-hand technique of the kanjira, the finger snaps and rolls
of the riq and daf, and the strokes of the tar and bendir, and joined them, with his classical
training in Western percussion, into a single
technical vocabulary that could be used on any frame drum, held in the hand, laid on the lap or
played freehand. The Percussive Arts Society calls it "a unified technical vocabulary"; the
articles of the 1990s called it "a unified approach to the frame drum". Much of the modern lap
style, both hands playing a drum laid flat across the knee, comes from it, and from the
kanjira's split-hand technique on which it draws. He also brought new techniques of his own,
such as playing the bodhrán with brushes.

He named his teaching the **Handance Method**. In it the voice and the body are part of the
playing: the student steps in place, plays and vocalizes at once, often each in a different
rhythmic cycle, so that rhythm is felt in the feet, heard in the voice and played in the hands
together. Its exercises join voice warm-ups and walking to the drum. Velez published it in instructional videos, *The Fantastic
World of Frame Drums* (1990) and *Handance Method 1* and *2* (1996), in *Handance Method with
Cueing and Performance Guide* (2002) and *Handance Duets for Frame Drums* (2001), and in manuals
for the bodhrán, tar and shakers (2004). Cooperman makes a series of tars and bendirs to his
design.

## Students and the frame drum movement

Velez's students and the players he influenced made the frame drum an instrument of its own in
Western music. **Layne Redmond** (1952–2013) studied with him from 1981 and recorded with
him, beginning with *Handdance* (1984), before writing *When the Drummers Were Women* (1997)
and teaching her own method. **Yousif Sheronick**, a Lebanese American percussionist,
teaches online at the Frame Drum School, joining South Indian vocalizations with Velez's Handance
of stepping, playing and vocalizing at once. Many of today's frame drum teachers learned the lap
style and the unified technique from him or from his students, and the instrument now has its
own festivals, such as Tamburi Mundi in Freiburg.
