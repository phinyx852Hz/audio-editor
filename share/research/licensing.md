# Licensing / Software

## About this section

What follows is a general introduction to software licenses, copyright and Creative Commons:
what each is, where it came from, and how they fit together. It is education, not legal advice.
The law differs from country to country and changes with new statutes and court decisions, and a
license means what its own text says, not what a summary says. For a real question about your
own work (releasing an album, sampling a record, shipping a program), read the license itself
and ask a lawyer who practices in the country concerned.

The list of open source software PHI is built on, or learned from, is in the manual.

  [Creative Commons license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Commons_license), Wikipedia: the overviews of the three submenus.

## Proprietary software

Software whose owner keeps the exclusive rights that copyright gives (see
[Copyright](research:Copyright)) and grants users only a narrow license to run it, usually through
an end-user license agreement (EULA) accepted when the program is installed. The source code is
kept secret; copying, sharing, studying by disassembly and changing the program are forbidden or
limited. Most commercial audio software is proprietary: Adobe Audition, Sound Forge and WaveLab,
whose best features PHI brings together, are all sold this way.

In the 1950s and 60s software came with the computer, often as source code that users' groups
such as SHARE (1955) passed around freely. IBM's decision of 1969 to price its software apart
from its hardware ("unbundling", under antitrust pressure) helped make software a product of its
own. Bill Gates's "Open Letter to Hobbyists" (1976) argued that copying Altair BASIC was theft
and that programmers had to be paid; by the 1980s shrink-wrap licenses were the norm. Proprietary
does not mean paid: **freeware** is proprietary software given away, and **shareware** (named in
the early 1980s) may be tried for free and paid for if kept.

- [Proprietary software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proprietary_software) and
  [End-user license agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-user_license_agreement), Wikipedia.
- [Freeware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeware), [Shareware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareware) and [History of software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_software), Wikipedia.
- Bill Gates, ["An Open Letter to Hobbyists"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Open_Letter_to_Hobbyists),
  *Homebrew Computer Club Newsletter* (1976).

## Free software

Software that respects its users' freedom, defined by the Free Software Foundation as four
freedoms: to run the program for any purpose (freedom 0), to study how it works and change it
(1), to give away copies (2), and to give away changed versions (3). Freedoms 1 and 3 need the
source code. "Free" is as in free speech, not free beer: free software may be sold.

Richard Stallman, a programmer at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, saw its sharing
culture end as its programs became proprietary in the early 1980s. He announced the GNU project
("GNU's Not Unix"), a whole free operating system, in September 1983, published the *GNU
Manifesto* in 1985 and founded the Free Software Foundation that October. GNU wrote most of a
Unix-like system (the GCC compiler, the Emacs editor, the shell and its tools); Linus Torvalds's
Linux kernel (1991), released under the GNU GPL in 1992, completed it, and the result is the
GNU/Linux of today.

- [Free software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_software) and
  [GNU Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Project), Wikipedia.
- [Richard Stallman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Stallman), [Free Software Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Software_Foundation), [The Free Software Definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Free_Software_Definition),
  [GNU Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Manifesto) and [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux), Wikipedia.
- Richard Stallman, [*The GNU Manifesto*](https://www.gnu.org/gnu/manifesto.html) (1985).

## Open source

Software whose source code is published under a license that lets anyone use, study, change and
share it. The Open Source Initiative judges licenses against its Open Source Definition, ten
criteria: free redistribution, source code, derived works allowed, the integrity of the author's
source, no discrimination against persons or groups or fields of use, and so on.

The term was coined by Christine Peterson in February 1998, after Netscape announced it would
release the source of its browser (the start of Mozilla), and it was taken up to present free
software to business without the ambiguity of "free". Eric S. Raymond, author of *The Cathedral
and the Bazaar* (1997), and Bruce Perens founded the Open Source Initiative that year; the
definition is Perens's Debian Free Software Guidelines (1997) with Debian's name taken out.
The two movements describe nearly the same set of licenses but different values: the free
software movement speaks of users' freedom as an ethical matter, the open source movement of a
better way to develop software. "FOSS" and "FLOSS" (free/libre and open source software) name
both at once.

  [OSI-approved licenses](https://opensource.org/licenses), Open Source Initiative.
- [Open source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_software), [Open Source Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Source_Initiative),
  [The Open Source Definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Open_Source_Definition), [Debian Free Software Guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debian_Free_Software_Guidelines),
  [The Cathedral and the Bazaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cathedral_and_the_Bazaar) and
  [Alternative terms for free software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_terms_for_free_software), Wikipedia.
- Eric S. Raymond, [*The Cathedral and the Bazaar*](http://www.catb.org/~esr/writings/cathedral-bazaar/)
  (1997; O'Reilly, 1999).
- Richard Stallman, ["Why Open Source Misses the Point of Free Software"](https://www.gnu.org/philosophy/open-source-misses-the-point.html).

## Source-available software

Software whose code can be read, but under terms the Open Source Definition does not accept:
no commercial use, no use by competitors, no use as a hosted service, or a time limit before the
code becomes open. Examples are the Server Side Public License (MongoDB, 2018), the Business
Source License (MariaDB, 2016), whose code turns open after a set number of years, and the
"Commons Clause" (2018) added to otherwise open licenses. Several database and infrastructure
companies moved to such licenses in the late 2010s and early 2020s to stop cloud providers from
selling their software as a service, and several were forked by their communities in return
(Elasticsearch as OpenSearch, Terraform as OpenTofu, Redis as Valkey).

Shared source is not open source: being able to see the code is not the same as being free to
use, change and share it.

- [Source-available software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source-available_software),
  [Server Side Public License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_Side_Public_License), [Business Source License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_Source_License), [Commons Clause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commons_Clause) and
  [Shared source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_source), Wikipedia.

## Copyleft and permissive licenses

The two families of open source license. A **permissive** license (MIT, BSD, Apache) lets anyone
do almost anything with the code, including build it into proprietary software, as long as the
copyright notice and license text are kept. A **copyleft** license (GPL, LGPL, AGPL, MPL) also
gives everyone those freedoms, but requires that copies and changed versions be passed on under
the same license, with their source: the freedom travels with the code and cannot be taken away
downstream. Strong copyleft (GPL, AGPL) covers the whole program a work is part of; weak copyleft
(LGPL, MPL) covers only the library or the files themselves.

"Copyleft" was a joke on "copyright" (a sticker Don Hopkins sent Stallman read "Copyleft (L), All
Rights Reversed"); Stallman used it for the GNU licenses from 1985 on. It works by copyright
itself: the author keeps the copyright and licenses it on condition of sharing alike, which is why
copyleft cannot be enforced against a work in the public domain. Creative Commons's ShareAlike
(see [The four license elements](research:The%20four%20license%20elements)) is the same idea for
culture.

- [Copyleft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyleft) and
  [Permissive software license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permissive_software_license), Wikipedia.
- [What is Copyleft?](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/copyleft.html), Free Software Foundation.

## GNU General Public License (GPL)

The strong copyleft license of the GNU project, and the most widely used free software license.
Anyone may run, study, change and share the program; whoever distributes it, changed or not, must
give the recipients the complete corresponding source under the same GPL, with no further
restrictions. A program that includes GPL code, linked into one executable, is a work based on it
and must be GPL as a whole when distributed. Running the program privately, or changing it for
yourself, carries no obligation.

Stallman wrote GPL version 1 in February 1989, unifying the licenses of GNU Emacs, GCC and the
debugger. Version 2 (June 1991) added the "liberty or death" clause: if a court order or patent
license prevents you from meeting the license's terms, you may not distribute at all. Version 3
(29 June 2007), drafted in public over eighteen months, added an explicit patent license,
protection against "tivoization" (hardware that runs only the maker's signed builds), clauses on
digital restrictions management laws, and compatibility with the Apache License 2.0. The Linux
kernel stayed on GPL version 2 only. Most GNU software is "version 3 or any later version", which
lets the FSF's future versions apply. The GPL has been upheld in court, from Germany
(*Welte v. Sitecom*, 2004) to the United States (*Jacobsen v. Katzer*, 2008, on the related
Artistic License, and the Software Freedom Conservancy's BusyBox suits).

- [GNU General Public License](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-3.0.html), version 3, and the
  [GPL FAQ](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html), Free Software Foundation.
- [GNU General Public License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_General_Public_License),
  [Tivoization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tivoization), [Jacobsen v. Katzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobsen_v._Katzer) and [Linux kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux_kernel), Wikipedia.

## GNU Lesser General Public License (LGPL)

A weak copyleft license for libraries. The library itself stays free (changes to it must be
shared under the LGPL), but programs of any license, proprietary ones included, may link to it,
as long as the user can replace the library with a changed version: in practice, by linking to it
dynamically or supplying the object files to relink.

It began as the GNU *Library* General Public License with GPL version 2 (1991), so that the GNU C
library could be used by every program on a system. Stallman renamed it the *Lesser* GPL with
version 2.1 (1999), to say it gives less protection than the GPL and should not be the first
choice for every library. Version 3 (2007) is written as a set of extra permissions on top of GPL
version 3. Qt, the toolkit PHI's interface is built with, and FFmpeg's core libraries are among
the best-known LGPL software.

- [GNU Lesser General Public License](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/lgpl-3.0.html), Free Software
  Foundation, and ["Why you shouldn't use the Lesser GPL for your next library"](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/why-not-lgpl.html).
- [GNU Lesser General Public License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Lesser_General_Public_License),
  [Qt (software)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qt_(software)) and [FFmpeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FFmpeg), Wikipedia.

## GNU Affero General Public License (AGPL)

The GPL with one more condition: if you change the program and let people use it over a network,
as a web service, you must offer those users its source. It closes the "service loophole" of the
GPL, whose obligations begin only when copies are distributed, which a program that runs on a
server never is.

Henry Poole's company Affero and the FSF wrote the first Affero GPL in 2002, as GPL version 2
with that clause. The FSF published the GNU Affero GPL version 3 on 19 November 2007, alongside
GPL version 3 and compatible with it. PHI itself is licensed under the GNU AGPL version 3 only
(its LICENSE file); JUCE, the audio framework, moved to the AGPL with a commercial alternative
in 2024. Many companies' policies forbid AGPL code, and some of the source-available licenses
(see [Source-available software](research:Source-available%20software)) began as stricter
variants of it.

- [GNU Affero General Public License](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl-3.0.html), Free Software
  Foundation.
- [GNU Affero General Public License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Affero_General_Public_License)
  and [JUCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JUCE), Wikipedia.

## MIT License

The shortest of the widely used licenses: a few lines that let anyone use, copy, change, merge,
publish, distribute, sublicense and sell the software, on the one condition that the copyright
notice and the license text go with all copies or substantial portions, and a disclaimer that the
software comes "as is", with no warranty.

It comes from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the 1980s; the X Window System
(1984–87) is the best-known early use, and MIT has used several slightly different texts, which
is why the OSI and SPDX call the common one the "Expat" or simply MIT license. It is now the most
common license on GitHub, used by Node.js, jQuery, Ruby on Rails and .NET among many others.

- [The MIT License](https://opensource.org/license/mit), Open Source Initiative.
- [MIT License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_License) and [X Window System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_Window_System), Wikipedia.

## BSD licenses

Permissive licenses from the Berkeley Software Distribution, the University of California's
version of Unix. All require keeping the copyright notice, the conditions and the disclaimer;
they differ in their clauses:

- **4-clause** (the original, 1990): also requires every advertisement for software using it to
  credit the University; with many contributors, the lists of credits grew impractical, and it is
  incompatible with the GPL.
- **3-clause** ("new" or "modified" BSD): the advertising clause removed, by the University's
  decision of 22 July 1999. A remaining clause forbids using the authors' names to endorse
  derived products.
- **2-clause** ("simplified" or FreeBSD license): the endorsement clause removed too, leaving a
  license nearly equal to MIT.
- **0-clause** (0BSD): no conditions at all, close to the public domain.

BSD Unix was the work of Berkeley's Computer Systems Research Group from 1977; after the lawsuit
*USL v. BSDi* (1992–94) it was freed of AT&T code, and FreeBSD, NetBSD and OpenBSD continue it.
Its code, under its license, is inside many proprietary systems, among them Apple's macOS.

- [BSD licenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSD_licenses),
  [Berkeley Software Distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Software_Distribution) and [UNIX System Laboratories, Inc. v. Berkeley Software Design, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNIX_System_Laboratories,_Inc._v._Berkeley_Software_Design,_Inc.),
  Wikipedia.
- [The 3-Clause BSD License](https://opensource.org/license/bsd-3-clause), Open Source Initiative.

## Apache License 2.0

A permissive license, longer and more precise than MIT or BSD. It also gives users an explicit
license to any patents the contributors hold on their contributions, and ends that license for
anyone who sues claiming the software infringes a patent. Changed files must say they were
changed, and a NOTICE file of attributions, if the project has one, must be passed on.

The Apache Software Foundation wrote version 1.0 (1995) for the Apache web server, version 1.1
in 2000, and version 2.0 in January 2004. It is compatible with GPL version 3, but not version 2
(whose terms forbid the patent clause's extra conditions). Android, Kubernetes, Swift and much of
the Java and machine-learning worlds are under it.

- [Apache License, Version 2.0](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0), Apache Software
  Foundation.
- [Apache License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apache_License) and [The Apache Software Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Apache_Software_Foundation), Wikipedia.

## Mozilla Public License 2.0

A weak copyleft license that works file by file: changes to MPL files must be shared under the
MPL, but they may be combined with files under any other license, proprietary ones included, in
a larger work. It sits between the permissive licenses and the LGPL.

Mitchell Baker wrote version 1.0 (1998) for the Netscape source code that became Mozilla, with
version 1.1 in 1999. Version 2.0 (January 2012) was rewritten to be shorter and clearer, added a
patent clause like Apache's, and is compatible with the GNU licenses unless the author marks the
code "Incompatible With Secondary Licenses". Firefox, Thunderbird and LibreOffice are among its uses.

- [Mozilla Public License 2.0](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/) and its
  [FAQ](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/FAQ/), Mozilla.
- [Mozilla Public License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozilla_Public_License) and [Mitchell Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_Baker), Wikipedia.

- **zlib**: the license of the zlib compression library (1995) and many game libraries:

- **Boost Software License** (2003): permissive, without the notice in compiled programs;

- **Artistic License**: Perl's license (1991; version 2.0, 2006), usually dual-licensed with the

## License compatibility

Whether code under two licenses can be combined into one program and distributed. Permissive
licenses fit almost anywhere, because their only demand (keep the notice) can be met inside any
other license. Copyleft licenses each require the whole to be under their own terms, so two
different copylefts usually cannot be combined unless one allows the other: GPL version 2 only and
GPL version 3 cannot, while "version 2 or later" can be taken as version 3. Code generally flows
from permissive to copyleft, not back.

Some well-known cases: Apache 2.0 code can go into a GPLv3 program but not a GPLv2-only one;
MPL 2.0 code can go into GPL, LGPL and AGPL programs; GPLv3 code can be combined with AGPLv3 code
(sections 13 of each provide for it); CC BY-SA 4.0 content can be adapted under GPLv3, one way
only. Licenses that are not open source, and the non-commercial and no-derivatives Creative
Commons licenses, are incompatible with all the copyleft licenses.

- [License compatibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License_compatibility), Wikipedia, with
  David A. Wheeler's diagram of the common licenses, and [Relicensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relicensing).
- [Various licenses and comments about them](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/license-list.html), Free
  Software Foundation, which lists each license's compatibility with the GPL.

## Dual licensing

Offering the same code under two licenses at once, the user choosing either. The commonest form
is a copyleft license for free use beside a commercial license for those who want to keep their
own changes private, sold by the owner of the copyright. It needs a single owner (or contributors
who sign over or license their rights in a contributor license agreement) so the code can be
licensed both ways.

MySQL (GPL and commercial, from 2000) made the model famous; Trolltech offered Qt under its own
free license and the GPL besides commercial terms, and Nokia added the LGPL in 2009; JUCE offers
the AGPL and commercial licenses; Steinberg's VST 3 SDK, once proprietary and GPLv3, became MIT
licensed in 2025. Other projects are dual-licensed to be compatible with more licenses, such as
"MIT or Apache 2.0" across the Rust language's libraries.

- [Multi-licensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-licensing) and
  [Contributor license agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contributor_License_Agreement),
  [MySQL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MySQL) and [Virtual Studio Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_Studio_Technology), Wikipedia.

## SPDX license identifiers

Short standard names for licenses, such as `MIT`, `Apache-2.0`, `GPL-3.0-or-later` and
`AGPL-3.0-only`, written in a comment at the head of each source file:
`SPDX-License-Identifier: AGPL-3.0-only`, as in each of PHI's own files. Expressions combine
them: `MIT OR Apache-2.0`, `GPL-2.0-or-later WITH Classpath-exception-2.0`.

The Software Package Data Exchange began in 2010 as a project of the Linux Foundation, to let
companies describe the licenses in the software they use and ship (a "software bill of
materials"). Its list names over six hundred licenses; the specification became the
international standard ISO/IEC 5962 in 2021. The Linux kernel converted to SPDX headers from 2017.
The REUSE project of the Free Software Foundation Europe builds on it.

- [Software Package Data Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_Package_Data_Exchange) and [Software bill of materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_bill_of_materials), Wikipedia.
- [SPDX License List](https://spdx.org/licenses/), SPDX.
- [REUSE](https://reuse.software), Free Software Foundation Europe.

# Licensing / Copyright

## Copyright

The exclusive right of the author of an original work to copy it, to distribute copies, to adapt
it (translations, arrangements, remixes and other derivative works), and to perform and display
it in public, and to license others to do these things. It protects the expression, not the
idea: a melody as written, a recording as made, a program's source code, but not a style, a
chord progression in general, an algorithm or a method of operation. The work must be original
(in the United States, since *Feist v. Rural* in 1991, a minimal degree of creativity, not mere
effort) and, in many countries, fixed in some tangible form.

Copyright begins automatically, the moment the work is made: no registration or © notice is
required under the Berne Convention, though in the United States registration is needed to sue
for infringement and brings statutory damages. It lasts, in most countries, for the author's life
plus 70 years (the European Union from 1993 to 1995, the United States from 1998); the Berne
minimum is life plus 50. Works made by employees in their job, and in the US certain commissioned
"works made for hire", belong to the employer.

The Statute of Anne (1710), in Britain, was the first copyright law to give the right to authors
rather than printers, for 14 years renewable once. The US Constitution (1787) empowered Congress
"to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts", and the first US Copyright Act followed in
1790. The Berne Convention (1886), led by Victor Hugo's literary association, made authors'
rights international; it has over 180 members, the United States joining only in 1989. Computer
programs are protected as literary works, in the US from the CONTU report (1978) and *Apple v.
Franklin* (1983), and worldwide by the TRIPS agreement (1994) and the WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996).

## Licenses and assignment

Copyright is property that can be transferred or shared out. An **assignment** sells or gives the
copyright itself to someone else (a publisher, a record label, an employer); it usually has to be
in writing and signed. A **license** keeps the copyright with its owner and gives permission to
use the work in stated ways: exclusive or not, for a territory or a period, for money or free.
Every software license, Creative Commons license and sync or sample license is a license in this
sense; using a work beyond what the license allows is infringement of the copyright.

An open source or Creative Commons license is a public license, offered to everyone at once,
accepted by using the work under its conditions. Most cannot be revoked for copies already
licensed, and most end automatically for someone who breaks their conditions (GPL version 3 and
the CC 4.0 licenses give 30 days to put it right and have the license restored).

- [Copyright transfer agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_transfer_agreement) and
  [License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License) and [Public copyright license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_copyright_license), Wikipedia.

## Exceptions: fair use and fair dealing

Every copyright law lets some uses of a work happen without permission. In the **United States**,
fair use (Copyright Act, section 107) weighs four factors: the purpose and character of the use
(above all whether it is transformative, adding new expression or meaning), the nature of the
work, the amount used, and the effect on the market for the original. It is decided case by case:
there is no number of seconds or bars that is always fair. In *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* (1994),
2 Live Crew's parody of "Oh, Pretty Woman" could be fair use though commercial; in *Google v.
Oracle* (2021), copying the declarations of Java's programming interface was fair use.

**Fair dealing** in the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, India and elsewhere is narrower: a use
must fit one of the purposes the law lists (research and private study, criticism and review,
quotation, news reporting, parody and pastiche, teaching) and be fair. The **European Union**'s
countries have lists of exceptions under the InfoSoc Directive (2001) and the Digital Single
Market Directive (2019), including quotation, parody and text and data mining.

## The public domain

Works no one owns, which anyone may use for any purpose without permission: works whose copyright
has expired, works that were never protected (facts, ideas, in the US the works of the federal
government), and works whose authors gave up their rights. Most music written before about 1900
is in the public domain as a composition, but a recent recording or edition of it has its own
copyright.

In the United States, works published before 1929 are in the public domain as of 2025, each
January 1 adding the works of another year (Public Domain Day), since the Sonny Bono Copyright
Term Extension Act of 1998 froze the date for twenty years; *Eldred v. Ashcroft* (2003) upheld
that act, and the case led directly to Creative Commons. Sound recordings made before 1972 were
brought under federal law by the Music Modernization Act (2018) and leave copyright on a schedule
of their own. In countries counting life plus 70, an author's works enter the public domain on
the January 1 after the seventieth anniversary of their death.

## Copyright in music

A recorded song has at least two copyrights: the **musical work** (the composition: melody,
harmony as written, lyrics), usually owned by the songwriters and their publishers, and the
**sound recording** (the master), usually owned by the artist or the label, marked ℗ instead of
©. Each is licensed separately: a cover version needs the song but not the master; a sample needs
both.

The rights to use them have their own names. **Mechanical** rights, to reproduce the song on
records and in streams, date from the US Copyright Act of 1909, which made them a compulsory
license at a set rate after *White-Smith v. Apollo* (1908) held piano rolls were not copies.
**Performance** rights, to play the music in public and on radio, are collected by performing
rights organizations: SACEM in France (1851, the first), ASCAP (1914) and BMI (1939) in the
United States, PRS in the United Kingdom, GEMA in Germany. **Synchronization** licenses put music
with pictures, in films, games and advertising; **neighbouring rights** pay performers and
producers when recordings are broadcast. In the United States, the Music Modernization Act of
2018 created the Mechanical Licensing Collective for streaming.

**Sampling** has been the hardest case. *Grand Upright v. Warner* (1991) held Biz Markie's
uncleared sample of Gilbert O'Sullivan an infringement; *Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films*
(Sixth Circuit, 2005) said of recordings "get a license or do not sample", while *VMG Salsoul v.
Ciccone* (Ninth Circuit, 2016) allowed a trivial ("de minimis") use; the European Court of Justice
in *Pelham v. Hütter* (2019), over two seconds of Kraftwerk, held that a sample needs permission
unless it is changed beyond recognition. *Williams v. Gaye* (2018, "Blurred Lines") and *Skidmore
v. Led Zeppelin* (2020, "Stairway to Heaven") turned on how far a song's feel and notes can be
owned. Sample packs and sound libraries come with their own licenses, usually "royalty-free": the
sounds may be used in your music, but not resold as samples.

## Moral rights

Rights of the author that protect their personal connection to the work rather than its money:
to be named as its author (**attribution** or paternity), and to object to distortion or
mutilation that harms their honour or reputation (**integrity**); in some countries also to decide
when it is first published, and to withdraw it. They stay with the author even when the copyright
is sold, and in France they never expire.

They come from the French and German traditions of *droit d'auteur*, authors' rights as a right
of the person, as against the Anglo-American tradition of copyright as an economic right. The
Berne Convention added them in article 6bis (1928). Common-law countries give them grudgingly:
the United Kingdom from 1988, where they must be asserted and can be waived, and the United
States only for some works of visual art (the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990). This is why
Creative Commons licenses, in their 4.0 versions, waive moral rights only as far as needed to
use the licensed rights.

  [Visual Artists Rights Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_Artists_Rights_Act), Wikipedia.

## Patents, trademarks and trade secrets

Three other kinds of "intellectual property", often confused with copyright:

- **Patents** protect inventions, including technical processes, for about 20 years, in exchange
  for publishing how they work; they must be applied for and granted, and they cover the idea,
  however it is written. Software patents are allowed in the United States within the limits of
  *Alice v. CLS Bank* (2014), but excluded "as such" in Europe (European Patent Convention,
  article 52). Audio formats have been the classic case: the MP3 patents of Fraunhofer and others
  kept free encoders out of many distributions until they expired (Technicolor ended its licensing
  in April 2017); AAC is still licensed through a patent pool, while FLAC, Vorbis and Opus were
  designed to be free of royalties. The Apache 2.0, GPLv3 and MPL 2.0 licenses include a patent
  license for this reason.
- **Trademarks** protect names, logos and other signs that tell buyers whose goods they are, for
  as long as they are used. They are why a free program can be forked but not sold under its
  original's name (Debian's "Iceweasel" of 2006–16), and why PHI, which brings together the best of
  Adobe Audition, Sound Forge and WaveLab, does not use their names or logos as its own.
- **Trade secrets** protect valuable information kept confidential, such as unpublished source
  code or a mastering engineer's chain, for as long as it stays secret. They do not stop anyone
  who discovers the same thing independently.

## Digital rights management

Technical locks on copies (copy protection, encrypted streams, license servers) and the laws that
forbid getting around them. The WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996) asked its members to protect such
measures; the United States did so in the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (1998, section 1201),
and the European Union in the InfoSoc Directive (2001). Breaking a lock can be unlawful even when
the use would otherwise be allowed, though the US Copyright Office grants exemptions every three
years. The DMCA also created the "notice and takedown" system by which hosting sites remove
material on a rights holder's complaint.

Music was the first great battle: Sony BMG's rootkit on audio CDs (2005), and Apple's iTunes
store, which sold songs locked with its FairPlay system from 2003 until it went DRM-free in 2009.
GPL version 3 answers these laws by saying a covered program is not an effective technological
measure.

# Licensing / Creative Commons

## Creative Commons

A nonprofit organization, and the set of free public licenses it publishes, that lets authors of
any kind of work (writing, images, music, film, data, teaching materials) give the public
permission in advance to share and build on their work, on conditions they choose: "some rights
reserved" in place of "all rights reserved". The licenses are made for culture and knowledge, not
for software, for which Creative Commons recommends the software licenses (see
[Copyleft and permissive licenses](research:Copyleft%20and%20permissive%20licenses)); CC0 is used
for both.

Creative Commons was founded in 2001 by Lawrence Lessig, Hal Abelson and Eric Eldred, with the
support of the Center for the Public Domain, at the time *Eldred v. Ashcroft* was challenging the
extension of US copyright terms; the case was lost in 2003, and the licenses became the way for
authors to open their works themselves. The first licenses appeared on 16 December 2002, inspired
by the GNU GPL. Version 2.0 (2004) made attribution part of
every license; 3.0 (2007) was adapted ("ported") to dozens of national laws; 4.0 (November 2013)
is one international license for all countries, covers the European database right, and is the
version to use today. There are over two billion works under CC licenses, among them Wikipedia,
Flickr's and Wikimedia Commons's photographs, MIT OpenCourseWare and much of open-access science.

Every license comes in three layers: the **legal code**, which a court reads; the **deed**, a
short human-readable summary; and the **machine-readable** metadata, so search engines can find
works by their license. The licenses cannot be revoked: once a work is out under one, those who
received it may go on using it on its terms, even if the author stops offering it.

## The four license elements

Each Creative Commons license is built from four conditions, each with a symbol and an
abbreviation:

- **BY (Attribution)**: you must credit the author, give the license, link to it, and say if you
  made changes, in a way that does not suggest the author endorses you or your use. Every CC
  license has it.
- **SA (ShareAlike)**: if you remix, transform or build on the work, you must license what you
  make under the same license (or a compatible one). It is the copyleft of
  [Copyleft and permissive licenses](research:Copyleft%20and%20permissive%20licenses), for culture.
- **NC (NonCommercial)**: you may not use the work "primarily intended for or directed toward
  commercial advantage or monetary compensation". What counts is decided by the use, not the
  user; a commercial use can still be licensed separately from the author.
- **ND (NoDerivatives)**: you may share the work only whole and unchanged; adaptations
  (remixes, edits, translations, a song put to film) may not be shared. Under 4.0 you may still
  make them for yourself.

SA and ND cannot be combined, since one is about sharing adaptations and the other forbids it.
That leaves six licenses (see [The six licenses](research:The%20six%20licenses)).

## The six licenses

From the most open to the most restrictive:

| License | Short name | Share | Adapt | Commercial use | Adaptations must be |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attribution | CC BY | yes | yes | yes | anything, with credit |
| Attribution-ShareAlike | CC BY-SA | yes | yes | yes | CC BY-SA |
| Attribution-NoDerivatives | CC BY-ND | yes | no | yes | not shared |
| Attribution-NonCommercial | CC BY-NC | yes | yes | no | anything non-commercial, with credit |
| Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike | CC BY-NC-SA | yes | yes | no | CC BY-NC-SA |
| Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives | CC BY-NC-ND | yes | no | no | not shared |

Only CC BY and CC BY-SA, with CC0, are "approved for free cultural works" (Freedom Defined, 2008):
the ones that meet the same freedoms as free software, and the only ones Wikipedia and Wikimedia
Commons accept. CC BY-SA is Wikipedia's license (since 2009, version 4.0 since 2023), and since
2015 CC BY-SA 4.0 works can be adapted into GPLv3 ones. The NC and ND licenses keep commercial or
adaptation rights for the author to sell separately; they cannot be mixed into BY-SA or free
software works.

The first set of 2002 had eleven licenses, including ones without attribution; nearly everyone
chose BY, and from 2004 the rest were retired. Also retired: the Sampling, Sampling+ and
NonCommercial Sampling+ licenses (2003–2011), written with the artist Negativland and the
musician Gilberto Gil for remixing and sampling music, and the Developing Nations license
(2004–2007).

## CC0 and the Public Domain Mark

**CC0** ("CC Zero", 2009) is not a license but a dedication: the author gives up all their
copyright and related rights in the work, as far as the law allows, putting it as near to the
public domain as they can. Where the law does not let an author waive copyright, it falls back to
an unconditional license, and where that fails too, to a promise not to enforce. No attribution is
required, though it is still courteous. It is used for data (Europeana's metadata, many
scientific databases), images (the collections of many museums, such as the Metropolitan Museum's
Open Access, 2017), software, and sounds.

The **Public Domain Mark** (2010) is a label, not a legal tool: it marks a work already in the
public domain, such as a scan of an 18th-century score, so others can find it as free. Only use it
for works that are free of copyright worldwide, and never on your own work, for which CC0 is the
tool.

## Using Creative Commons works

To credit a CC work properly, give its **TASL**: the Title, the Author (as they want to be named),
the Source (a link to where it is), and the License (its name and a link to its deed), and say if
you changed it ("cut and pitched down", "remixed from"). For example: "*Rain on a Tin Roof* by
J. Doe, from freesound.org, CC BY 4.0, trimmed". Under 4.0 the credit may be given in any
reasonable way for the medium: in a description, the liner notes or the credits at the end. If
you combine works under different licenses, each part keeps its own; a ShareAlike part sets the
license of the adaptation as a whole.

In music and sound, Creative Commons has been widely used: ccMixter (2004), a remix community run
by Creative Commons itself at first; Jamendo (2005) and the Free Music Archive (2009), with whole
albums; Freesound (2005, from the Music Technology Group of Pompeu Fabra University in Barcelona),
with hundreds of thousands of sounds under CC0, CC BY and CC BY-NC. Nine Inch Nails released
*Ghosts I–IV* (2008) under CC BY-NC-SA and it was the year's best-selling MP3 album on Amazon.
Before using a sound in work you will sell, check that its license does not have NC, and keep a
record of where every sound came from and under what terms.
