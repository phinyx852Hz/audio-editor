# Modes / Europe

## Ancient Greece

Greek music theory gave Europe its first names for modes: the harmoniai (Dorian, Phrygian,
Lydian, Mixolydian and the rest), later the tonoi, built from tetrachords, four notes spanning a
fourth whose two inner notes moved to make three genera: diatonic, chromatic and enharmonic.
Joined tetrachords made the Greater Perfect System of two octaves.

Plato and Aristotle argued over the moral character of each harmonia; Aristoxenus measured
intervals by ear and Ptolemy by ratio. Only about sixty fragments of notated music survive, the
Seikilos epitaph (1st century CE) the oldest complete song. Medieval theorists borrowed the
Greek names for the church modes but attached them to different scales, which is why Dorian
means one thing in Plato and another in Gregorian chant.

- [Musical system of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_system_of_ancient_Greece), Wikipedia.
- Martin L. West, *Ancient Greek Music* (Oxford University Press, 1992).
- Andrew Barker, *Greek Musical Writings*, 2 vols (Cambridge University Press, 1984–89).

## Byzantine and Greek Orthodox chant

The chant of the Eastern Church is ordered by the Octoechos, eight modes (echoi), four
authentic and four plagal, each with its own scale, final, cadences and melodic formulas, and
with intervals that are not those of the piano.

Tradition credits John of Damascus (8th century) with the Octoechos cycle of hymns. Byzantine
neumes were reformed in 1814 by Chrysanthos of Madytos, Gregorios the Protopsaltes and
Chourmouzios the Archivist; Chrysanthos's *Theōrētikon mega* (1832) divided the octave into 68
moria, and the Patriarchal Music Committee of 1881 into 72, the system still taught.

- [Byzantine music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_music) and
  [Octoechos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octoechos), Wikipedia.
- Egon Wellesz, *A History of Byzantine Music and Hymnography* (Oxford, 2nd ed. 1961).

## Western Church (Gregorian chant)

Plainchant is sung in eight modes, four authentic (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, on
the finals D, E, F and G) each paired with a plagal mode a fourth lower (Hypodorian and so on).
A mode is known by its final, its range and its reciting tone.

The eight-mode system came to the West from the Byzantine Octoechos in the Carolingian era,
around 800, when Frankish singers sorted chant into tonaries. *Musica enchiriadis* (9th
century), Hucbald and Guido of Arezzo gave it its theory; the Greek names were added by
mistake from Boethius. The modes ruled European music until major and minor took over in the
seventeenth century, and came back with Solesmes's chant revival in the nineteenth.

- [Gregorian chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_chant) and
  [Gregorian mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_mode), Wikipedia.
- David Hiley, *Western Plainchant: A Handbook* (Oxford University Press, 1993).

## Russian Orthodox (znamenny chant)

The unison chant of the Russian Church, named for its neumes (znamya, "sign"). Its melodies
move within the obikhod scale, built of trichords that repeat at the fourth rather than the
octave, and are grouped into eight glasy (tones), after the Byzantine Octoechos.

It came with Christianity in 988 and grew into a tradition of its own from the twelfth century.
Polyphony and western harmony replaced it in the cathedrals from the seventeenth century; the
Old Believers, who broke with the church in the 1660s, still sing it, and Rachmaninoff's
*All-Night Vigil* (1915) sets znamenny melodies.

- [Znamenny chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Znamenny_chant), Wikipedia.
- Johann von Gardner, *Russian Church Singing*, 2 vols (St Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1980–2000).

## Ashkenazi Jewish liturgy (nusach)

Nusach is the traditional way of chanting each prayer of the Ashkenazi service, set in prayer
modes (steiger) named after prayers that use them: Adonai Malakh (major with a lowered seventh),
Magen Avot (minor) and Ahavah Rabbah (with an augmented second, the freygish of klezmer).

The modes grew in the synagogues of Central and Eastern Europe from the Middle Ages, handed down
by cantors (hazzanim) by ear. The term steiger dates from the nineteenth century; Abraham Zvi
Idelsohn described the system in the twentieth.

- [Nusach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nusach_(Jewish_music)), Wikipedia.
- Abraham Z. Idelsohn, *Jewish Music in its Historical Development* (1929).

## Tonal Western music

Two modes, major and minor, with harmony that moves by functions (tonic, subdominant,
dominant) and keys that can change by modulation, on the twelve notes of equal or near-equal
temperament.

Major and minor emerged from the church modes during the seventeenth century, with the triad as
the unit of harmony (Lippius, 1612). Jean-Philippe Rameau's *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722) made
the theory; Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* (1722, 1742) played in all keys. Equal temperament
became the norm in the nineteenth century, and the system was stretched by Wagner and set aside
by atonality in the twentieth, while it went on in popular music.

- [Tonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonality) and
  [Major and minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_and_minor), Wikipedia.
- Jean-Philippe Rameau, *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722).
- Brian Hyer, "Tonality", *Grove Music Online*.

## British and Irish folk

Folk song in the British Isles is modal: Ionian, Mixolydian, Dorian and Aeolian are the most
common, and many tunes are gapped, using five or six notes of the scale (pentatonic and
hexatonic), which can be heard in more than one mode.

Edward Bunting wrote down the tunes of the last harpers at the Belfast Harp Festival (1792);
the Folk-Song Society (1898) and collectors such as Lucy Broadwood, Anne Gilchrist and Cecil
Sharp gathered thousands of songs before 1920. Their analyses of the modes fed the English
pastoral school of Vaughan Williams and Holst, and the folk revivals of the 1950s and 60s.

- [English folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_folk_music) and
  [Irish traditional music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_traditional_music), Wikipedia.
- Cecil Sharp, *English Folk Song: Some Conclusions* (1907).

## Eastern European folk

The village and klezmer music of Ukraine, Romania, Poland and the Balkans uses modes that mix
the church modes with the augmented second: the Ukrainian Dorian (Dorian with a raised fourth),
the Romanian minor, and the freygish shared with the synagogue.

These modes were carried by Romani, Jewish and village musicians across the region. Collectors
from Oskar Kolberg in Poland (from 1857) to Bartók in Romania (1909–18) wrote them down; klezmer
musicians took them to America, and the klezmer revival of the 1970s brought them back.

- [Ukrainian Dorian scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Dorian_scale) and
  [Klezmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klezmer), Wikipedia.
- Béla Bartók, *Rumanian Folk Music*, ed. Benjamin Suchoff, 5 vols (1967–75).

## Hungary

Two traditions: the music of the Romani bands of the cities, with the "Hungarian" or "gypsy"
minor (minor with a raised fourth and two augmented seconds), and the old peasant song,
pentatonic and often descending, that Bartók and Kodály found in the villages.

The verbunkos style of the Romani bands (late eighteenth century) became the sound of
"Hungarian" music for Liszt and Brahms. In 1905–06 Bartók and Kodály began recording peasant
song with a phonograph and argued that it, not the city bands' music, was the older layer;
Kodály's *A magyar népzene* (1937) set out its pentatonic basis.

- [Hungarian minor scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_minor_scale) and
  [Verbunkos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbunkos), Wikipedia.
- Zoltán Kodály, *Folk Music of Hungary* (1937; English ed. 1960).

## Spain (Andalusia, flamenco)

Flamenco and much Andalusian music turn on the "mode of E" (modo de Mi): Phrygian, often with a
raised third, falling to its final through the Andalusian cadence (A minor, G, F, E). Its
rhythms (compás) are cycles of twelve beats with shifting accents in soleá, bulerías and
alegrías.

The style grew among the Romani (gitanos) of Andalusia, drawing on Moorish, Jewish, Castilian
and Romani music, and took its modern form in the cafés cantantes from the 1860s. Manuel de
Falla and Federico García Lorca held the Concurso de Cante Jondo in Granada in 1922 to save the
old deep song.

- [Flamenco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flamenco) and
  [Phrygian dominant scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygian_dominant_scale), Wikipedia.
- Peter Manuel, "Flamenco Harmony: Historical Development, Structure and Theory", *Ethnomusicology*
  30/1 (1986).

## Pythagoras (school of)

The Pythagoreans found that the consonances are simple ratios of string lengths: the octave
2:1, the fifth 3:2, the fourth 4:3. Pythagorean tuning stacks pure fifths, which makes a
whole tone of 9:8 and a small semitone, the limma, of 256:243.

Pythagoras (6th century BCE) left no writings; the story of his discovery (with hammers in a
smithy) comes from Nicomachus (2nd century CE) and Boethius's *De institutione musica* (c. 500),
which carried Pythagorean tuning into the Middle Ages, where it stayed the theory until the
Renaissance.

- [Pythagorean tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_tuning), Wikipedia.
- Boethius, *Fundamentals of Music*, trans. Calvin M. Bower (Yale University Press, 1989).

## Archytas

A Pythagorean philosopher, mathematician and general of Tarentum (c. 428–347 BCE), friend of
Plato. He gave ratios for all three genera of the tetrachord, among them 28:27 and 5:4, bringing
the pure major third and the seventh harmonic into Greek theory.

His tunings are known from Ptolemy's *Harmonics*, which quotes them. He also proved that no
whole number lies halfway between n and n + 1 in ratio, so the tone cannot be split into two
equal rational halves.

- [Archytas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archytas), Wikipedia.
- Carl A. Huffman, *Archytas of Tarentum* (Cambridge University Press, 2005).

## Aristoxenus

A pupil of Aristotle (4th century BCE) who held that the ear, not ratios, should judge
intervals. His *Elementa harmonica* measures them in tones and fractions of a tone (a quarter
tone for the enharmonic), and describes the shades of each genus.

His approach, against the Pythagoreans', is the first description of music by equal steps,
and anticipates equal temperament. His *Elementa rhythmica* is the first theory of rhythm.

- [Aristoxenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristoxenus), Wikipedia.
- Andrew Barker, *Greek Musical Writings*, vol. 2 (1989), with a translation.

## Ptolemy

The Alexandrian astronomer (c. 100–170) wrote the *Harmonics*, which compares the tunings of the
Pythagoreans, Aristoxenus and Archytas, and gives his own: among them the tense diatonic,
16:15, 9:8, 10:9, the scale of just intonation.

Ptolemy tested his ratios on a monochord and on the helikon, a many-stringed instrument of his
own. The *Harmonics* was the most complete ancient work on tuning; Boethius drew on it, and
Kepler's *Harmonices mundi* (1619) answered it.

- [Ptolemy's intense diatonic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy%27s_intense_diatonic_scale), Wikipedia.
- Jon Solomon, *Ptolemy, Harmonics: Translation and Commentary* (Brill, 2000).

## Guido of Arezzo

A Benedictine monk (c. 991–1033) who taught singers to read new chant at sight. He named the
six notes of the hexachord ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la from the first syllables of the hymn *Ut
queant laxis*, and set neumes on lines a third apart, the ancestor of the staff.

His *Micrologus* (c. 1026) is the most copied medieval treatise on music after Boethius. The
hexachord system, with mutation between hexachords on C, F and G, was how singers understood
the modes until the seventeenth century; ut became do.

- [Guido of Arezzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo), Wikipedia.
- Dolores Pesce, *Guido d'Arezzo's Regule rithmice, Prologus in antiphonarium, and Epistola ad
  Michahelem* (Institute of Mediaeval Music, 1999).

## Bartolomé Ramos de Pareja

A Spanish theorist (c. 1440–1522) who taught at Salamanca and Bologna. His *Musica practica*
(Bologna, 1482) divided the monochord so that the thirds came out pure (5:4 and 6:5), against
the Pythagorean thirds of the theory of his time.

The book set off a long quarrel with the followers of Boethius, and opened the way to the just
intonation that Zarlino later made a system.

- [Bartolomé Ramos de Pareja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolom%C3%A9_Ramos_de_Pareja), Wikipedia.

## Heinrich Glarean

A Swiss humanist (1488–1563) whose *Dodecachordon* (Basel, 1547) added four modes to the
eight of chant: Aeolian and Hypoaeolian on A, Ionian and Hypoionian on C. The names were new,
taken from the Greek; the scales were already sung.

He showed each mode in polyphony by Josquin and his contemporaries. Ionian and Aeolian are
today's major and minor, so the book stands at the turn from modes to keys.

- [Heinrich Glarean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Glarean) and
  [Dodecachordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodecachordon), Wikipedia.
- Heinrich Glarean, *Dodecachordon*, trans. Clement A. Miller (American Institute of Musicology,
  1965).

## Nicola Vicentino

An Italian composer and theorist (1511–1576) who tried to bring back the chromatic and
enharmonic genera of the Greeks. His *L'antica musica ridotta alla moderna prattica* (1555)
described the archicembalo, a harpsichord with 31 keys to the octave.

He lost a public debate on the genera to Vicente Lusitano in Rome in 1551, and wrote the book
in reply. His 31 notes are almost those of 31-tone equal temperament, which Huygens worked out
a century later.

- [Nicola Vicentino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicola_Vicentino) and
  [Archicembalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archicembalo), Wikipedia.
- Nicola Vicentino, *Ancient Music Adapted to Modern Practice*, trans. Maria Rika Maniates
  (Yale University Press, 1996).

## Gioseffo Zarlino

The Venetian theorist and chapel master of St Mark's (1517–1590). *Le istitutioni harmoniche*
(1558) founded harmony on the senario, the numbers 1 to 6, which give the octave, fifth,
fourth and both thirds as pure ratios: just intonation as a system.

He named the major and minor triads by their thirds and, in the 1573 edition, renumbered the
twelve modes to begin on C. His pupil Vincenzo Galilei attacked him for ignoring how
instruments were really tuned.

- [Gioseffo Zarlino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gioseffo_Zarlino), Wikipedia.
- Gioseffo Zarlino, *On the Modes*, trans. Vered Cohen (Yale University Press, 1983).

## Claude Le Jeune

A French composer (c. 1530–1600). His *Dodecacorde* (1598) sets twelve psalms, one in each of
Glarean's twelve modes, and his *Le Printemps* uses musique mesurée, rhythm set by the long and
short syllables of verse in the ancient manner.

He worked with Jean-Antoine de Baïf's Académie de Poésie et de Musique (1570), which tried to
bring back the effects of ancient music; he also wrote in the chromatic genus.

- [Claude Le Jeune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Le_Jeune) and
  [Musique mesurée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musique_mesur%C3%A9e), Wikipedia.

## Johannes Lippius and Johann Crüger

Lippius (1585–1612), a Lutheran theologian, named the harmonic triad (*trias harmonica*) in his
*Synopsis musicae novae* (1612) and treated it as the root of all harmony, an image of the
Trinity. Crüger (1598–1662), cantor of St Nicholas in Berlin, spread the idea in his teaching
books (*Synopsis musica*, 1630).

Thinking in triads rather than in lines is what turned the twelve modes into two, major and
minor. Crüger is remembered also for the chorales of *Praxis pietatis melica* (1647), among them
"Nun danket alle Gott".

- [Johannes Lippius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Lippius) and
  [Johann Crüger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Cr%C3%BCger), Wikipedia.

## Christiaan Huygens

The Dutch physicist (1629–1695) of the pendulum clock and the wave theory of light. In *Novus
cyclus harmonicus* (1691) he showed that dividing the octave into 31 equal steps gives almost
exactly the meantone tuning of his day, with pure-sounding thirds, and every key.

He also worked on the vibration of strings and the pitch of sounds. Adriaan Fokker revived his
31-tone system in the twentieth century.

- [31 equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/31_equal_temperament), Wikipedia.
- [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens), Wikipedia.

## Andreas Werckmeister

A German organist and theorist (1645–1706). *Musicalische Temperatur* (1691) gave well
temperaments, ways of tuning that let every key be played while each kept its own colour,
sharing out the Pythagorean comma unequally among the fifths.

Werckmeister III is still used for Baroque music. Whether Bach's "well-tempered" meant one of
his temperaments is still debated.

- [Werckmeister temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werckmeister_temperament) and
  [Well temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_temperament), Wikipedia.
- Andreas Werckmeister, *Musicalische Temperatur* (Quedlinburg, 1691).

## Johann Philipp Kirnberger

A pupil of Bach (1721–1783) and music master to Princess Anna Amalia of Prussia. *Die Kunst des
reinen Satzes in der Musik* (1771–79) set out Bach's teaching of composition, and he gave
temperaments of his own, Kirnberger II and III, with pure fifths and thirds where they matter
most.

He kept Bach's manuscripts in the princess's library, one of the main sources of Bach's music
today.

- [Kirnberger temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirnberger_temperament), Wikipedia.
- Johann Philipp Kirnberger, *The Art of Strict Musical Composition*, trans. David Beach and
  Jürgen Thym (Yale University Press, 1982).

## Ludwig van Beethoven

In the slow movement of his String Quartet in A minor, Op. 132 (1825), Beethoven wrote a
"Holy song of thanksgiving of a convalescent to the Deity, in the Lydian mode", harmonized
without a B flat, the church mode on F.

He had studied the church modes in the old treatises while writing the *Missa solemnis*; the
movement is one of the first returns to the modes in the age of major and minor.

- [String Quartet No. 15 (Beethoven)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_Quartet_No._15_(Beethoven)), Wikipedia.

## Mikhail Glinka

The founder of Russian art music (1804–1857). In *Ruslan and Lyudmila* (1842) a descending
whole-tone scale stands for the sorcerer Chernomor: one of the first uses of the whole-tone
scale, which became a Russian way of painting the supernatural.

His folk-based melody and his use of the scale passed to the Mighty Handful (Balakirev,
Musorgsky, Rimsky-Korsakov and others) and through them to Debussy.

- [Mikhail Glinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Glinka) and
  [Whole-tone scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole-tone_scale), Wikipedia.

## Frédéric Chopin

Chopin's mazurkas (1825–49) bring the modes of Polish village music into the salon: the raised
fourth of the Lydian, drones, and the rhythms of the mazur, kujawiak and oberek, with the accent
on the second or third beat.

He heard the music in the Mazovian countryside as a boy. Later Polish composers, Szymanowski
above all, built on the same sources.

- [Mazurkas (Chopin)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazurkas_(Chopin)), Wikipedia.

## Franz Liszt

Liszt (1811–1886) used the Hungarian (gypsy) minor and the music of the Romani bands in his
Hungarian Rhapsodies (from 1846), and in his late works the whole-tone scale, augmented triads
and harmony without a key.

His book *Des Bohémiens et de leur musique en Hongrie* (1859) claimed the Hungarian style for
the Romani, a view Bartók later answered. Late pieces such as *Nuages gris* (1881) look ahead
to Debussy.

- [Franz Liszt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Liszt) and
  [Hungarian Rhapsodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Rhapsodies_(Liszt)), Wikipedia.

## Giuseppe Verdi

Verdi's *Ave Maria* (1889), the first of the *Quattro pezzi sacri*, harmonizes a puzzle scale
published in the *Gazzetta musicale di Milano*, the scala enigmatica: C, D flat, E, F sharp, G
sharp, A sharp, B, with F natural on the way down.

The challenge was to set it in four parts; Verdi did so four times, once with the scale in each
voice.

- [Enigmatic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enigmatic_scale), Wikipedia.

## Camille Saint-Saëns

Saint-Saëns (1835–1921) travelled often in North Africa and wrote its modes into his music:
the *Suite algérienne* (1880), the Bacchanale of *Samson et Dalila* (1877) with its augmented
seconds, and the Egyptian Piano Concerto No. 5 (1896).

He also studied the music of ancient Greece and wrote on the old modes, part of a French
interest in modality that reached Fauré and Debussy.

- [Camille Saint-Saëns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camille_Saint-Sa%C3%ABns), Wikipedia.

## Modest Musorgsky

Musorgsky (1839–1881) took his melody from Russian speech and folk song, with the modes of
znamenny chant and the village. *Boris Godunov* (1869, revised 1872) opens its coronation with
two chords a tritone apart, like bells, outside any key.

His raw harmonies were smoothed by Rimsky-Korsakov after his death; Debussy and Ravel learned
from the original.

- [Modest Mussorgsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modest_Mussorgsky), Wikipedia.

## Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

Rimsky-Korsakov (1844–1908) used the octatonic scale, alternating half and whole steps, from his
tone poem *Sadko* (1867) on; in Russia it is still called the Rimsky-Korsakov scale. He also
used the whole-tone scale for the magical, as Glinka had.

He taught Stravinsky, who made the octatonic scale central to *Petrushka* and *The Rite of
Spring*. His *Principles of Orchestration* (1913) is still read.

- [Octatonic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octatonic_scale), Wikipedia.
- Richard Taruskin, "Chernomor to Kashchei: Harmonic Sorcery", *Journal of the American
  Musicological Society* 38/1 (1985).

## Gabriel Fauré

Fauré (1845–1924) studied at the École Niedermeyer, which trained church musicians in
plainchant, and the church modes colour his harmony throughout: the Requiem (1887–90), the
songs, *Pelléas et Mélisande*.

His modal harmony, moving freely between keys, was passed on to his pupils Ravel and Nadia
Boulanger, and through her to much of the twentieth century.

- [Gabriel Fauré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Faur%C3%A9), Wikipedia.

## Leoš Janáček

Janáček (1854–1928) collected Moravian folk song and studied the melody of Czech speech, noting
people's words as tunes. His music uses the modes of Moravian song, the Lydian fourth above all.

He edited the great collection of Moravian folk songs with František Bartoš (1899–1901). His
operas *Jenůfa* (1904) and *Káťa Kabanová* (1921) set the rhythms of spoken Czech.

- [Leoš Janáček](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo%C5%A1_Jan%C3%A1%C4%8Dek), Wikipedia.

## Cecil Sharp

An English collector (1859–1924) who noted thousands of folk songs and dances in England and,
with Maud Karpeles, in the Southern Appalachians (1916–18). His *English Folk Song: Some
Conclusions* (1907) classified the songs by their modes.

He founded the English Folk Dance Society (1911). His work shaped the English revival and the
pastoral composers, though his choices of what counted as folk are now questioned.

- [Cecil Sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Sharp), Wikipedia.
- [Vaughan Williams Memorial Library](https://www.vwml.org), the archive of the English Folk
  Dance and Song Society, with Sharp's manuscripts.

## Gustav Mahler

Mahler (1860–1911) set Chinese poems in *Das Lied von der Erde* (1908–09) with pentatonic
melody, and his symphonies are full of folk modes, the Ländler and the Bohemian village bands of
his childhood.

The pentatonic of *Das Lied* is one of the first European evocations of East Asian music in a
symphonic work.

- [Das Lied von der Erde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Lied_von_der_Erde), Wikipedia.

## Claude Debussy

Debussy (1862–1918) heard a Javanese gamelan at the Paris Exposition of 1889, and his music
uses the whole-tone scale (*Voiles*, 1909), the pentatonic (*Pagodes*, 1903) and the church
modes in place of major and minor.

He took from Musorgsky and the Russians as well as from Java. His freeing of harmony from
function opened the way for much of the century's music.

- [Claude Debussy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Debussy), Wikipedia.
- Roy Howat, *The Art of French Piano Music* (Yale University Press, 2009).

## Annie Gilchrist

Anne Geddes Gilchrist (1863–1954), an English folk-song scholar, wrote on the modes and
history of folk tunes in the *Journal of the Folk-Song Society* and its successor from the
1900s to the 1950s.

She was one of the most careful analysts of the first revival, tracing tunes across England,
Scotland and Ireland and through hymnody.

- [Vaughan Williams Memorial Library](https://www.vwml.org), which holds her papers.

## Ferruccio Busoni

The pianist and composer (1866–1924) wrote *Sketch of a New Esthetic of Music* (1907), which
counted 113 seven-note scales on C and called for dividing the tone into thirds and sixths.

The book inspired Varèse and the electronic music pioneers; Busoni had a third-tone harmonium
built, and his pupils included Kurt Weill.

- [Ferruccio Busoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferruccio_Busoni), Wikipedia.
- Ferruccio Busoni, *Sketch of a New Esthetic of Music* (1907; English trans. 1911).

## Alexander Scriabin

Scriabin (1872–1915) built his late music on one six-note chord, the mystic or Prometheus chord
(C, F sharp, B flat, E, A, D), and the scales made from it, in *Prometheus: The Poem of Fire*
(1910) with its part for a colour organ.

He was moved by Theosophy and planned a *Mysterium* to end the world in music. His harmony sits
between the octatonic and the overtone series.

- [Mystic chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystic_chord), Wikipedia.

## Ralph Vaughan Williams and Gustav Holst

Vaughan Williams (1872–1958) collected folk songs from 1903 and edited *The English Hymnal*
(1906); his *Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis* (1910) is in Tallis's Phrygian mode. Holst
(1874–1934), his closest friend, studied Sanskrit and set the Rig Veda and the opera *Sāvitri*.

Together they made modal folk song the basis of an English national music, and taught a
generation of composers.

- [Ralph Vaughan Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Vaughan_Williams) and
  [Gustav Holst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Holst), Wikipedia.

## Nikolai and Alexander Tcherepnin

Nikolai Tcherepnin (1873–1945) used a nine-note scale built from the augmented triad with a
note on each side of each of its notes; Alexander (1899–1977), his son, systematized it as the
Tcherepnin scale and joined it to a rhythmic idea he called interpoint.

Alexander travelled in China and Japan (1934–37), published young Chinese and Japanese
composers, and married the pianist Lee Hsien Ming.

- [Alexander Tcherepnin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Tcherepnin) and
  [Nikolai Tcherepnin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Tcherepnin), Wikipedia.

## Maurice Ravel

Ravel (1875–1937) wrote in the church modes and in the Spanish Phrygian of his Basque mother's
country (*Rapsodie espagnole*, 1908; *Boléro*, 1928), and drew on gamelan and jazz.

He studied with Fauré and learned modality from him. His *Daphnis et Chloé* (1912) uses
Lydian and Dorian colours in a large orchestra.

- [Maurice Ravel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Ravel), Wikipedia.

## Béla Bartók

Bartók (1881–1945) recorded thousands of peasant songs in Hungary, Slovakia, Romania and as far
as Turkey and Algeria from 1906. From them he built a music of mixed modes: the acoustic scale
(Lydian with a flat seventh), polymodal chromaticism, and the asymmetric "Bulgarian" rhythms.

His *Mikrokosmos* (1926–39) teaches it in 153 piano pieces. He was among the founders of
ethnomusicology, insisting on recording and transcribing exactly.

- [Béla Bartók](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9la_Bart%C3%B3k), Wikipedia.
- Elliott Antokoletz, *The Music of Béla Bartók* (University of California Press, 1984).

## Zoltán Kodály

Kodály (1882–1967) collected Hungarian folk song with Bartók from 1905 and showed that its
oldest layer is pentatonic. He made folk song the basis of music teaching, with solfa and hand
signs: the Kodály method.

His *Folk Music of Hungary* (1937) and the *Corpus Musicae Popularis Hungaricae* (from 1951),
which he directed, are the standard collections.

- [Zoltán Kodály](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zolt%C3%A1n_Kod%C3%A1ly) and
  [Kodály method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kod%C3%A1ly_method), Wikipedia.

## Igor Stravinsky

Stravinsky (1882–1971), Rimsky-Korsakov's pupil, made the octatonic scale central to
*Petrushka* (1911) and *The Rite of Spring* (1913), set against the diatonic modes of Russian
folk song, with rhythms that change metre bar by bar.

Pieter van den Toorn and Richard Taruskin traced the octatonic in his music; he later turned to
neoclassicism and, in the 1950s, to twelve-tone rows.

- [Igor Stravinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Stravinsky), Wikipedia.
- Pieter C. van den Toorn, *The Music of Igor Stravinsky* (Yale University Press, 1983).

## Abraham Zvi Idelsohn

Idelsohn (1882–1938), a cantor and scholar from Latvia who settled in Jerusalem in 1906,
recorded the songs of Jewish communities from Yemen to Persia and published them in the ten
volumes of the *Thesaurus of Hebrew Oriental Melodies* (1914–32).

He described the prayer modes of the synagogue and compared them with Arabic maqām. He arranged
"Hava Nagila" from a Hasidic tune (1918).

- [Abraham Zevi Idelsohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Zevi_Idelsohn), Wikipedia.
- Abraham Z. Idelsohn, *Jewish Music in its Historical Development* (1929).

## Adriaan Fokker

A Dutch physicist (1887–1972), cousin of the aircraft maker, who revived Huygens's 31-tone
equal temperament. The Fokker organ, with 31 keys to the octave, was built for Teylers Museum
in Haarlem (1950), and composers wrote for it.

He also worked on relativity and quantum theory (the Fokker–Planck equation, 1914).

- [Adriaan Fokker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriaan_Fokker), Wikipedia.
- [Huygens-Fokker Foundation](https://www.huygens-fokker.org), with its microtonal library.

## Alois Hába

A Czech composer (1893–1973) of quarter-tone and sixth-tone music. His opera *Matka* (The
Mother, 1927–29) is in quarter tones, and he had quarter-tone pianos, clarinets and trumpets
built.

He taught microtonal composition at the Prague Conservatory from 1923, drawing on the
microtones of Moravian folk song, and wrote *Neue Harmonielehre* (1927).

- [Alois Hába](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alois_H%C3%A1ba), Wikipedia.

## Ivan Wyschnegradsky

A Russian composer in Paris (1893–1979) who wrote almost only in quarter tones and other
microtones, for two pianos tuned a quarter tone apart, and designed spaces of sound he called
ultrachromatic.

His *Manual of Quarter-Tone Harmony* (1932) set out the theory. He worked with Hába and Carrillo
on quarter-tone instruments.

- [Ivan Wyschnegradsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Wyschnegradsky), Wikipedia.

## Olivier Messiaen

Messiaen (1908–1992) set out his modes of limited transposition in *Technique de mon langage
musical* (1944): seven scales that can be transposed only a few times before they repeat,
among them the whole-tone and octatonic scales. He also used Indian deçī-tālas, Greek metres
and birdsong.

He taught at the Paris Conservatoire from 1941; his pupils included Boulez, Stockhausen and
Xenakis. His *Mode de valeurs et d'intensités* (1949) led to total serialism.

- [Mode of limited transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_of_limited_transposition)
  and [Olivier Messiaen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivier_Messiaen), Wikipedia.
- Olivier Messiaen, *The Technique of My Musical Language*, trans. John Satterfield (1956).

## Heinz Bohlen

A German engineer (1935–2016) who in 1972 proposed dividing the tritave (3:1, an octave and a
fifth) into 13 steps: the Bohlen–Pierce scale, built on odd harmonics only, for sounds such as
the clarinet's.

Kees van Prooijen (1978) and John R. Pierce (1984) found it independently. A Bohlen–Pierce
clarinet was made by Stephen Fox (2008), and there have been symposia on the scale since 2010.

- [Bohlen–Pierce scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohlen%E2%80%93Pierce_scale), Wikipedia.

## Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail

Grisey (1946–1998) and Murail (b. 1947), pupils of Messiaen, founded the ensemble L'Itinéraire
(1973) and spectral music: harmony drawn from the overtones of sounds, analysed by computer,
with microtones as the spectrum gives them. Grisey's *Partiels* (1975) orchestrates the
spectrum of a trombone's low E.

Murail's *Gondwana* (1980) and his work at IRCAM made the approach a school; Murail later taught
at Columbia University.

- [Spectral music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_music), Wikipedia.
- Julian Anderson, "A Provisional History of Spectral Music", *Contemporary Music Review* 19/2
  (2000).

## Georg Friedrich Haas

An Austrian composer (b. 1953) of microtonal music, built on the overtone series and on
beating between close pitches. *in vain* (2000), for 24 players, moves between equal
temperament and the pure intervals of the overtones, with the lights turned out.

He taught at Graz, Basel and Columbia University. His string quartets and *limited
approximations* (2010) for six pianos tuned a twelfth of a tone apart extend the spectral line.

- [Georg Friedrich Haas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Friedrich_Haas), Wikipedia.
