# Modes

## Mode in PHI: melody, rhythm and harmony

PHI's Mode menu has three parts, melody (with tuning), rhythm and harmony, because it follows a
generalized theory of musical modality proposed for PHI. In this theory mode no longer refers
mainly to the organization of pitches. It describes the manner in which musical relationships
are organized and realized across several dimensions. That is why the word is taken in its
broad sense of [a method or way](research:Mode).

The theory distinguishes three primary categories:

1. **Melodic mode**: the manner in which pitches, intervals, melodic gestures and phrases are
   organized and realized through time.
2. **Rhythmic mode**: the manner in which durations, accents, temporal groupings, repetitions
   and rhythmic gestures are organized and realized through time.
3. **Harmonic mode**: the manner in which simultaneous pitches, sonorities, harmonic
   relationships and their transformations are organized and realized through time.

![Melodic, rhythmic and harmonic mode around musical modality](modality-diagram.png)

The significant conceptual move is that modality becomes a general principle of musical
organization rather than a property exclusive to pitch systems. It is a potentially productive
framework for comparative musicology, composition and music software design.

### A general definition

> **Musical mode**: a distinguishable manner of organizing and realizing musical relationships,
> characterized by particular structural principles, hierarchies, patterns of movement,
> expressive gestures and conventions of performance within one or more dimensions of musical
> experience.

This definition lets melodic, rhythmic and harmonic modes be treated as related manifestations
of a higher-order concept. It also deliberately distinguishes mode from scale, meter, chord and
rhythmic pattern. Those can be components of a mode, but they need not make up the whole mode.

### Three dimensions of modality

| Characteristic | Melodic mode | Rhythmic mode | Harmonic mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary material | Pitches and intervals | Durations and temporal events | Simultaneous pitches and sonorities |
| Hierarchy | Tonics, finals, prominent tones | Strong and weak beats, temporal anchors | Harmonic centers, stable and unstable sonorities |
| Movement | Contour, direction, melodic pathways | Pulse, grouping, rhythmic flow | Voice leading, harmonic succession |
| Characteristic gestures | Motifs, turns, ornaments | Syncopation, rhythmic cells, accents | Cadences, suspensions, harmonic gestures |
| Temporal organization | Phrasing, melodic development | Meter, cycles, proportional duration | Harmonic rhythm and rate of change |
| Performance realization | Intonation, articulation, ornament | Timing, swing, microtiming | Voicing, tuning, articulation |

The three modes need not depend on one another. A composition could keep its melodic mode while
changing its rhythmic or harmonic mode, and the same rhythmic organization might support several
melodic systems.
This is the principle of modal independence, and why Western theory has not always granted it
is the subject of [Is Western theory harmony-centered?](research:Is%20Western%20theory%20harmony-centered%3F)

### Historical precedents

The three-part framework is a proposed synthesis rather than an established universal
classification, but it has antecedents.

- **Rhythmic mode** already has an explicit history. The Notre-Dame school of medieval
  polyphony used six [rhythmic modes](research:Medieval%20rhythmic%20modes%20%28ars%20antiqua%29), patterns
  of long and short durations. Mode has described temporal organization, not only pitch, for
  eight centuries. Luigi Antonio Irlandini's study "Expanded Modal Rhythm" (2017) extends
  rhythmic modality to ametric textures, polyrhythms, cross-rhythms and different kinds of
  rhythmic flow.
- **Melodic mode** has especially strong precedents in [rāga](research:Rāga),
  [maqām](research:Maqām) and other traditions in which characteristic melodic gestures and
  performance practice belong to a mode's identity.
- **Harmonic mode** is more complicated. Western theory recognizes modal harmony, but that
  usually means harmony working within an established pitch mode. Harmonic mode here is broader:
  a recognizable manner of organizing harmonic relationships in its own right, such as
  functional tonal progression, parallel chord movement, harmonic stasis, or transformations
  among sonorities (see [Harmony beyond the West](research:Harmony%20beyond%20the%20West)). Not every
  harmonic technique is a separate mode, so the theory needs criteria for telling a harmonic
  mode from a harmonic style, a procedure or a single composition.

### Mode and material

A mode is more than a list of permitted materials. It describes how those materials relate and
behave. Each mode can be described at four analytical levels, which apply the same way to all
three dimensions:

1. **Material**: what musical elements are available?
2. **Organization**: what relationships and hierarchies exist among them?
3. **Behavior**: how do those elements move, interact, develop or transform?
4. **Realization**: how are those relationships expressed through composition, improvisation and
   performance?

### A formal model

A musical configuration can be written as **M** = (*M*m, *M*r, *M*h): its melodic, rhythmic and
harmonic modes. Each component holds its own materials, organizational rules, behavioral
tendencies and performance practices, and a composition is a particular realization of these
dimensions and their interactions. This is an initial model. It still has to account for
variable combinations, ambiguity, and traditions in which the dimensions are not understood as
independent.

### Mode and form

A mode is not a musical form. Sonata form describes a large-scale organization of material,
while a melodic, rhythmic or harmonic mode may work locally, throughout a composition, or across
a whole tradition. In the same way, meter is not rhythmic mode, and a scale is not melodic mode:
a rhythmic mode can involve timing, duration, grouping, accent and gesture without conventional
meter.

### A refined definition

> **Musical modality** is the systematic organization and realization of musical relationships
> through distinguishable manners of melodic, rhythmic and harmonic behavior. Each modal
> dimension may establish characteristic materials, hierarchies, movements, gestures, temporal
> relationships and conventions of performance. These dimensions may operate independently or
> interact to produce the distinctive musical identity of a composition, an improvisation or a
> musical tradition.

The theory's strongest contribution is to make modal *behavior*, not just modal content, the
defining consideration. That separates a scale from a melodic mode, a rhythmic pattern from a
rhythmic mode, and a chord vocabulary from a harmonic mode, and it supports composing with
melodic, rhythmic and harmonic modes that are combined, transformed or developed independently,
as PHI's Mode menu lets you do.

One foundational question remains open: is a mode defined mainly by its organizational rules,
or by the recognizable musical behaviors that emerge from them? The answer could set the
direction of the whole theory.

## Is Western theory harmony-centered?

Research gathered for the theory of mode asks whether Western music theory treats melody as
"broken harmony", a horizontal unfolding of chords. If it does, melody stops being a dimension
of its own, which is the opposite of what PHI's Mode menu assumes.

The evidence says that harmony, meaning chord structure, harmonic progression and tonal
function, did become the dominant organizing framework of Western theory. There is also a long
tradition of questioning whether melody should be understood as subordinate to it.

### The Ramellian paradigm

Peter van der Merwe, in *Roots of the Classical: The Popular Origins of Western Music* (Oxford
University Press, 2004), names the assumption that melody is fundamentally based on harmony the
**Ramellian paradigm**, after Jean-Philippe Rameau. He argues that the supposed priority of
harmony over melody is mistaken: "If melody is 'arpeggiated' harmony, then harmony is
consolidated melody." He separates a harmonic tonality from a possibly independent melodic
tonality, and calls their interplay *tonal counterpoint*.

The book's larger argument is that Western classical music cannot be understood apart from the
folk, dance, vernacular and popular music whose melodic and harmonic patterns kept feeding it.
It treats folk, popular and classical music, especially from about 1770 to 1910, as one
connected ecosystem, and follows recurring patterns (melodic, harmonic, rhythmic and formal)
across social and stylistic lines rather than from masterpiece to masterpiece. It moves in three
stages: melodic foundations, from children's chants to pentatonicism; a harmonic revolution, the
rise of tonality, dissonance and competing tonal systems, traced from the simple tonic and
dominant patterns of Italian dance music around 1500 to the chromatic music of around 1900; and a
melodic counter-revolution, through Eastern influences, Central European dances, nineteenth
century vernacular music, Romanticism, Modernism, the blues and early jazz. It is broad and at
times speculative. Richard Taruskin, reviewing it in *Music & Letters*, faulted its overstatement
and confirmation bias, yet called it "marvellously stimulating and important" for making
musicians hear familiar music differently.

### Rameau and Rousseau

Rameau's *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722) gave the first systematic account of chords, their
roots and their relationships, and he argued that melody draws its expressive force from
harmony. Jean-Jacques Rousseau held the opposite, that melody comes first, and the two argued it
out in print in 1753–1754, in the years of the Querelle des Bouffons. Joel Lester's "Rameau and
eighteenth-century harmonic theory" (in *The Cambridge History of Western Music Theory*, 2002)
shows how Rameau gathered contrapuntal motion, the bass, root relationships and cadential
direction into a single harmonic idea of tonality. The dominance of harmony in theory was not
inevitable; it came out of this history.

### Melody classified by chords

The plainest sign of harmony-centered thinking is the split between chord tones and non-chord
tones. In the melody C–D–E–G–E–C over a C major chord, C, E and G belong to the chord and D is
a passing tone between C and E: the harmony is the frame, and the melody is sorted into notes
that belong to it and notes that decorate it. Standard teaching, from the College Board's AP
Music Theory course to open textbooks such as [Introduction to Non-Chord Tones](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/NonChordTonesIntroduction.html), classifies melodic notes this way. It is useful for tonal
music, but explaining a melody by an underlying harmony is not the same as showing that the
melody comes from it. C–D–E is also simply a rising scale gesture, with its own rhythm,
articulation and meaning, that needs no chord.

### Schenker

Heinrich Schenker (1868–1935) can seem to subordinate melody to harmony, but the deepest level
of his analysis, the *Ursatz*, joins a melodic line (the *Urlinie*) with an arpeggiated bass
(the *Bassbrechung*). He rejected music understood as a mere succession of surface chords, and
treated melody, voice leading, counterpoint and harmony as one connected structure. The theory
of mode may share more with him than a plain opposition of melody and harmony suggests.

### What is taught

The usual American undergraduate core is about three semesters of tonal harmony and one of
post-tonal theory, often from Aldwell and Schachter's *Harmony and Voice Leading*. A 2019 study
at Ball State University found that common-practice harmony holds the center while other scale
systems get little attention, and the open textbook *Music Theory for the 21st-Century
Classroom* criticizes traditional texts for neglecting how coherent melodies are built. Music
theory as a field is wider (rhythm, meter, counterpoint, form, timbre, popular and non-Western
music), but harmony is usually taught as a developed system of relationships and functions,
while melody and rhythm come as lists of elements and techniques.

| Dimension | What introductory theory stresses | What modal theory can study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melody | Chord tones, embellishments, scale degrees, phrases | Melodic syntax, contour, gesture, movement, intonation, ornament |
| Rhythm | Notated durations, meter, subdivision, syncopation | Temporal hierarchy, cycles, microtiming, duration patterns, rhythmic gesture |
| Harmony | Chords, inversions, functions, progressions, cadences | Sonority, harmonic hierarchy, voice relationships, transformations, harmonic movement |

### Scholars who challenge the imbalance

- **Richard Cohn**, "Why We Don't Teach Meter, and Why We Should" (2015): theory teaching gives
  far more to tonality than to meter, though meter applies across many more of the world's
  musics. Support for rhythm as a dimension of its own.
- **Justin London**, "A Bevy of Biases" (2022): theories of harmony, form and pedagogy rest on a
  narrow, unrepresentative sample of European common-practice works.
- **Philip Ewell**, "Music Theory and the White Racial Frame" (2020): in seven widely used
  American textbooks, the examples are overwhelmingly by white European composers; it asks which
  traditions have been raised into supposedly general theories.

### Melody in the world's scales

John M. McBride and colleagues, "Melody predominates over harmony in the evolution of musical
scales across 96 countries" (2024), compared theories of how scales form, across 1,314 scales
from 96 countries. Melodic explanations, above all the preference for small steps, did better
than harmonic interval ratios; harmonic models fitted some theoretically documented Eurasian
scales better than scales measured from recordings. It does not prove melody always matters more,
but it challenges harmonic consonance as the universal explanation of pitch systems.

### Counterpoint

Counterpoint is the Western tradition that gives melodic lines their own structure: vertical
sounds come from the meeting of horizontal lines, so harmony is partly a result of melody, not
only its foundation. Counterpoint also rules consonance and dissonance, and tonal counterpoint
takes in harmonic constraints, so the history is mixed. A harmony-centered reading goes from
chord progression to melodic realization; a contrapuntal one from voices to the vertical
relationships they make. Both are ways of analysing, not of how music must be composed.

### Modal independence

Three propositions follow. Western common-practice theory took on a strongly harmony-centered
orientation, above all after the eighteenth century's consolidation of harmonic theory. That
orientation shaped how melodies are classified, analysed and taught. And harmony's prominence in
Western teaching does not show that it has universal priority over melody or rhythm.

> **The principle of modal independence**: melodic, rhythmic and harmonic modes are analytically
> distinguishable dimensions of musical organization. None must be defined solely in terms of
> another, although their relationships and interactions may be fundamental to the identity of
> particular works or traditions.

This does not deny harmony or replace tonal theory. It treats harmony as one dimension rather
than the frame for all the others. What would theory look like if melody, rhythm and harmony had
equal standing from the start, three distinct but interacting systems? An open question for the
melodic side: has anyone built a theory of *melodic syntax* as far-reaching as Western harmonic
syntax?
