# Modes / West Asia

## Arabic maqām

A maqām is a melodic mode of Arabic music: a scale built from smaller units (ajnās, groups of
three to five notes), a starting note, a dominant, and a typical path through the scale and its
modulations. Its intervals include neutral seconds, between a semitone and a whole tone, written
as quarter tones in modern notation.

The theory grew from the Abbasid court of Baghdad (8th–10th centuries), with al-Kindī, al-Fārābī's
*Kitāb al-mūsīqī al-kabīr* and Ṣafī al-Dīn's systems. The Cairo Congress of Arab Music (1932)
recorded the traditions of the region and debated a 24-tone equal scale. Today's practice is
learned by ear from masters as much as from theory.

- [Arabic maqam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_maqam), Wikipedia.
- [Maqam World](https://www.maqamworld.com), every maqām and jins with examples.
- Scott Marcus, *Music in Egypt* (Oxford University Press, 2007).

## Iran (dastgāh)

Persian classical music is organized into seven dastgāhs (Shur, Homāyun, Segāh, Chahārgāh,
Māhur, Rāst-Panjgāh, Navā) and five āvāz derived from Shur and Homāyun. Each holds a sequence of
short pieces (gushehs), together the radif, learned by heart and used as the ground of
improvisation.

The twelve maqāms of medieval Persian and Arabic theory were regrouped into the dastgāh system
at the Qajar court in the nineteenth century, credited to Mirzā ʿAbdollāh and Mirzā Hoseyn-Qoli.
Ali-Naqi Vaziri wrote it in Western notation with signs for the koron and sori (1920s).

- [Persian traditional music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_traditional_music) and
  [Dastgah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dastgah), Wikipedia.
- Bruno Nettl, *The Radif of Persian Music* (Elephant & Cat, 1987).
- Jean During, Zia Mirabdolbaghi and Dariush Safvat, *The Art of Persian Music* (Mage, 1991).

## Ottoman Empire (makam)

Turkish makam music, the art music of the Ottoman court, the Mevlevi lodges and the cities of the
empire, uses hundreds of makams, each defined by its scale, its seyir (melodic path: ascending,
descending or both) and its finalis and dominant, with intervals measured in commas.

It took shape from the fifteenth century, drawing on Persian, Arabic and Byzantine music;
Dimitrie Cantemir wrote it down around 1700. After the Republic (1923) it was at times banished
from radio; the Arel-Ezgi-Uzdilek system, with 24 unequal notes from 53 commas to the octave,
became the official theory.

A makam is a melody type with rules for both composing and improvising. Composed forms (peşrev,
beste, şarkı, âyin) follow it, and so do free ones such as the taksim, the gazel and the recited
Qurʾān and Mevlid; its rhythmic partner is the usul. Its relatives are the Arab maqām, the
Byzantine echos, Uyghur muqam and Uzbek shashmaqom, and some trace a debt to Mosul, through the
Iraqi musician Mulla Othman al-Musili at the Ottoman court.

The building blocks are four- and five-note segments, the dörtlü and beşli. There are six basic
ones, Çârgâh, Bûselik, Kürdî, Uşşâk, Hicaz and Rast, and each beşli is its dörtlü plus a whole
tone. They are spelled in named steps counted in commas:
- koma 1; eksik bakiye 3; bakiye 4;
- küçük mücenneb 5; büyük mücenneb 8;
- tanîni (whole tone) 9; artık ikili (augmented second) 12 or 13.

Any segment can be moved to another note and keeps its shape. A makam joins a dörtlü and a beşli,
one above the other, and often a genişleme, an extension above or below. Three notes matter
most:
- the **durak**, the tonic and final, where every piece ends;
- the **güçlü**, the first note of the upper segment, a resting point in mid-piece, usually the
  fifth but often the fourth and sometimes the third;
- the **yeden**, the note below the durak that leads into it.

Makams are simple (most of them rising in seyir), transposed (şed), or compound, and the
compound ones number in the hundreds. Some simple ones:
- **Rast:** Rast beşli on G with a Rast dörtlü on D. Coming down, F♯ falls four commas to F,
  making a Bûselik dörtlü.
- **Uşşâk:** an Uşşâk dörtlü on A with a Bûselik beşli on D, so its güçlü is the fourth.
- **Bûselik:** on A, like the minor or the harmonic minor, with F♯ and a lowered E possible
  coming down.
- **Çârgâh:** on C, looks like the major scale, but is not best thought of that way.

The notes have names of their own in each octave, from Kaba Çârgâh (C4) through Rast (G4),
Dügâh (A4) and Nevâ (D5) to Tîz Çârgâh (C6), rather than letters repeated. Rast is common for the
afternoon and night calls to prayer, and Uşşâk for the noon call.

- [Turkish makam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_makam), Wikipedia.
- Walter Feldman, *Music of the Ottoman Court* (VWB, 1996).
- Karl Signell, *Makam: Modal Practice in Turkish Art Music* (Asian Music Publications, 1977;
  Usul Editions, 2004).
- Münir Beken and Karl Signell, "Confirming, delaying, and deceptive elements in Turkish
  improvisation", in *Maqām Traditions of Turkic Peoples*, ed. Jürgen Elsner and Gisa Jähnichen
  (trafo, 2006).

## Iraq (maqām ʿirāqī)

The Iraqi maqām is a vocal art of Baghdad and the north: a suite of sung pieces on classical and
colloquial poetry, in a fixed melodic order for each maqām, accompanied by the chalghi ensemble
of santur, jowza, tabla and daff, and ending in a light song (pesteh).

It holds old layers of Arabic, Persian and Turkish music, sung in coffee houses and gatherings.
Muhammad al-Qubbanchi represented it at the Cairo Congress (1932); Yusuf Omar and Munir Bashir
carried it on. UNESCO listed it as intangible heritage in 2003 (inscribed 2008).

- [Iraqi maqam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_maqam), Wikipedia.
- [UNESCO: Iraqi Maqam](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/iraqi-maqam-00076).

## Azerbaijan (mugam)

Azerbaijani mugam is a modal system and a suite form: each mugam (Rast, Shur, Segah, Chahargah,
Bayati-Shiraz, Shushtar, Humayun and others) moves through sections (shöbes) up its scale,
sung by a khanende with tar and kamancheh.

It shares roots with the Persian dastgāh and grew around Shusha, Baku and Shamakhi. Üzeyir
Hacıbəyli described its scales as chains of tetrachords and built the first opera of the
Muslim world on it (*Leyli and Majnun*, 1908). UNESCO proclaimed it in 2003.

- [Mugham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mugham), Wikipedia.
- [UNESCO: Azerbaijani Mugham](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/azerbaijani-mugham-00039).

## Zalzal

Manṣūr Zalzal (8th century), a lute player at the Abbasid court of Baghdad, gave his name to the
neutral third, about 355 cents, between the minor and the major: the wusṭā Zalzal fret of the
ʿūd, the interval that marks Arabic music to this day.

He was the teacher of Isḥāq al-Mawṣilī, the greatest musician of the court. The fret is known
from al-Fārābī's and later accounts of the ʿūd.

- [Neutral third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_third), Wikipedia.
- Owen Wright, "Arab Music", *Grove Music Online*.

## Ṣafī al-Dīn al-Urmawī

Ṣafī al-Dīn al-Urmawī (d. 1294), musician to the last Abbasid caliph and then to the Mongol
rulers of Baghdad, divided the octave into 17 unequal steps, Pythagorean in origin, and set
out the twelve maqāms (shudūd) and six āvāz of the medieval system.

His *Kitāb al-adwār* and *Risāla al-sharafiyya* were the foundation of the "Systematist" school
of Arabic, Persian and Turkish theory for centuries. He also wrote early rhythmic cycles in a
notation of his own.

- [Safi al-Din al-Urmawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safi_al-Din_al-Urmawi), Wikipedia.
- Owen Wright, *The Modal System of Arab and Persian Music, A.D. 1250–1300* (Oxford University
  Press, 1978).

## Dimitrie Cantemir

Prince of Moldavia (1673–1723) who lived for over twenty years in Istanbul and played the
tanbur. His *Kitāb-ı ʿilmüʾl-mūsīkī ʿalā vechiʾl-hurūfāt* (c. 1700) set out a letter notation and
wrote down some 350 instrumental pieces of the Ottoman repertoire.

It is the chief source for Ottoman music of the seventeenth century. Cantemir later served
Peter the Great and wrote a history of the Ottoman Empire.

- [Dimitrie Cantemir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitrie_Cantemir), Wikipedia.
- Owen Wright, *Demetrius Cantemir: The Collection of Notations*, 2 vols (SOAS, 1992–2000).

## Mīkhāʾīl Mushāqa

A Lebanese physician and scholar (1800–1888) whose *Al-risāla al-shihābiyya* (c. 1840) first
described dividing the octave into 24 quarter tones, the basis of modern Arabic notation.

He did not claim the 24 steps were equal; the equal 24-tone scale was debated at the Cairo
Congress of 1932 and taught after it, though musicians still tune by ear.

- [Mikhail Mishaqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Mishaqa), Wikipedia.
- Eli Smith, "A Treatise on Arab Music", *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 1 (1847), a
  translation of Mushāqa's treatise.

## Rauf Yekta

A Turkish musicologist and composer (1871–1935) who first applied the Pythagorean comma theory to
Turkish makam, describing its intervals in commas (a ninth of a tone). He wrote the article on
Turkish music for Lavignac's *Encyclopédie de la musique* (1922).

He led the collection of Turkish music at the Darülelhan conservatory. His work led to the
Arel-Ezgi-Uzdilek theory.

- [Rauf Yekta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rauf_Yekta), Wikipedia.

## Rodolphe d'Erlanger

A French-British painter and musicologist (1872–1932) who settled at Sidi Bou Said in Tunisia
and wrote *La musique arabe* (6 vols, 1930–59), translating the medieval treatises and describing
the maqāms and rhythms of his time.

He was a moving force behind the Cairo Congress of Arab Music (1932), and his palace, Ennejma
Ezzahra, is now the Centre for Arab and Mediterranean Music.

- [Rodolphe d'Erlanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodolphe_d%27Erlanger), Wikipedia.

## Arel, Ezgi and Uzdilek

Hüseyin Saadettin Arel (1880–1955), Suphi Ezgi (1869–1962) and Salih Murat Uzdilek (1891–1967)
made the standard theory of Turkish makam: 24 notes in the octave, chosen from the 53 equal
commas of Holdrian theory, with accidentals for each.

The system has been taught in Turkish conservatories since the mid-twentieth century. Musicians
know that the notes it writes are not the ones played (the segâh, for one, is sung lower), and
other theories (Yarman, Karadeniz) have been proposed.

- [Arel–Ezgi–Uzdilek system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arel%E2%80%93Ezgi%E2%80%93Uzdilek_system),
  Wikipedia.

## Üzeyir Hacıbəyli

An Azerbaijani composer (1885–1948), founder of Azerbaijani art music. His *Leyli and Majnun*
(1908) was the first opera of the Muslim East, built on mugam, and his *Principles of
Azerbaijani Folk Music* (1945) described the modes as chains of tetrachords that repeat at the
fourth.

He wrote the national anthem and founded the Baku Conservatory. His birthday, 18 September, is
Music Day in Azerbaijan.

- [Uzeyir Hajibeyov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzeyir_Hajibeyov), Wikipedia.
- Üzeyir Hacıbəyli, *Principles of Azerbaijani Folk Music* (1945; English ed. 1985).
