# Composition / South and West Asia

## Rāga development

A rāga (Sanskrit, "colouring" or "dyeing", for its power to colour the listener's mind) is the
melodic framework of Indian classical music, shared by the Hindustani north and the Carnatic
south. It is more than a scale, since several rāgas can share one, and less than a tune, since
one rāga yields endless tunes: Joep Bor calls it a tonal framework for composition and
improvisation, and Nazir Jairazbhoy tells rāgas apart by scale, ascent and descent, the notes
they stress, register, intonation and ornament. Each has a strongest note (vādī) and a second
one consonant with it (samvādī), in opposite halves of the octave, and a catch phrase (pakaḍ or
chalan) by which listeners know it even when another rāga has the same notes. Rāgas may use
five (auḍava), six (ṣāḍava) or seven (sampūrṇa) notes, a different number going up and down,
as Bhimpalasi does, or a zigzag (vakra) path. Hindustani rāgas belong to times of day and
seasons; Bhatkhande sorted them into ten parent scales (thāṭ), while Carnatic rāgas derive from
72 melakarta scales. The modern sense of the word first appears in Mataṅga's *Bṛhaddeśī* (8th or
9th century), and Śārṅgadeva's *Saṅgītaratnākara* (13th century) discusses 253 rāgas. Hundreds
are known; a performer's working repertoire is perhaps forty or fifty. Rāgas also carry Sikh
scripture, qawwali and much film song.

A Hindustani performance unfolds a rāga in stages, from free to strict time and from slow to
fast. The ālāp opens it: in dhrupad it is unmetred and unaccompanied but for the tānpūrā, and
slow, setting out the rāga's mood and which notes lead and which serve. Many musicians build
it methodically (vistār), bringing in the notes one at a time so that no phrase goes more than
a step past what has already been heard, dwelling on the vādī, samvādī and pakaḍ; the first
reach into a new octave then lands with great force. Some shape the ālāp like a four-part
composition, sthāyī, antarā, sañchārī and ābhog, and Bimalakanta Roychaudhuri's dictionary
lists thirteen stages, the last long extinct. In instrumental music the joṛ adds a steady pulse
and the jhālā quickens it until rhythm overtakes melody, with rapid strokes on the drone
strings (chikārī); in dhrupad singing the fast part is called nomtom, and an ālāp sung on
syllables or words is called sākṣar, one without them anakṣar. Then the composition, a gat for instruments or a bandish for voice, enters with the
tablā in a tāla such as tīntāl (16 beats), alternating the fixed tune with improvisation, and
sections often close with a tihāī, a phrase played three times to land on sam, the first beat
of the cycle. The tānpūrā's drone sounds throughout.

jhālā and a gat in tīntāl ending in a tihāī on sam, over the tānpūrā.

## Maqām: taqsīm and waṣla

A taqsīm (Turkish taksim, Greek taximi) is an unmetred improvisation that usually comes before
a composed piece in Arab, Turkish, Kurdish, Greek, Iranian and Azerbaijani music, the nearest
Western relative being the cadenza. It is played solo, or over a drone on the tonic or a
percussionist's pulse. It begins on the maqām's tonic (qarār) and stays at first in its lowest
jins (a tetrachord or pentachord), so that the listener learns the maqām; then the player may
range freely, climbing through its ajnās to the dominant (ghammāz) and the upper octave
(jawāb), and modulate to other maqāms, as long as the end, a cadence (qafla), comes home.

The word means "division". The ʿūd is its chief instrument, with the violin, nāy and qānūn,
and even Arabised instruments such as the accordion. A taqsīm is not free fantasy: Scott Marcus stresses the conventions
it follows, and Mondher Ayari and Stephen McAdams hear phases, an introduction, a presentation
of the maqām's basic elements, an exposition, a re-exposition and a confirmation, whose order
and length vary with the player. It is prized as the place where players show their own
invention rather than someone else's composition, and as a sign of good musicianship (Ali Jihad
Racy). Jonathan Shannon links it to ṭarab, the heightened, enraptured state that Arab listeners
and performers prize, and to salṭana, the player's immersion in the maqām, a melodic flow that
seems to suspend time. Zeina Alkaei and Mats Küssner's interviews with three Syrian oud players
in Berlin (2021) found them describing it as composing on the spot, built from the tetrachords
and from phrases borrowed from songs and masters' taqāsīm, with two jobs: to introduce a piece,
preparing players and listeners for its mood, and to give a solo within it. They argue it fits
neither end of the old composition-improvisation scale, a scale Bruno Nettl himself later
doubted.

Nettl and Ronald Riddle's close study (1973) of sixteen taqāsīm in maqām Nahawand that Racy
recorded alone in 1971–72, fourteen on the buzuq and two on the nāy, shows the patterns under
the freedom. Each ran roughly five to eight minutes in five to twelve sections, each section
closed by a cadential figure, most often three falling notes. Groups of very short sections
alternate with long ones, and the long ones, which come mostly in the second half, carry the
climaxes, the widest range and every modulation, to Rast, Bayātī, Ḥijāz, Ṣabā and others,
always after Nahawand is firmly set. The central note of each section generally climbs, in a
zigzag, while the line itself moves by step around neighbour notes; melodic sequences, a figure
repeated at successive pitches, fill much of the long sections, and on the buzuq the lower
strings give an interrupted drone on the tonic or fifth, mostly early on. The playing grows less
metrical and more ornamented as it goes. No two performances were alike, yet they shared one
range of shapes.

Scott Marcus's guide to recordings (1993) fills in the practice. Most taqāsīm last three to five
minutes, though one may end within a minute or run ten, depending on the time allowed and the
player's mood. Silences divide the sections, each section dwelling on one feature of the maqām,
so that the whole unfolds the mode, usually rising from the bottom of its scale toward the upper
octave; listeners relish the modulations, the obligatory return and above all the stock cadences
(qaflāt) that close the sections, which draw shouts of approval: a good qafla is said to rescue a
weak taqsīm, and a bad one to spoil a strong one. Listeners call out praise, or the player's name,
and so spur the player on. Young players learn by imitating elders and memorising the
masters' recordings, then must find their own style. In the early 20th century taqāsīm opened
and punctuated the waṣla; when the waṣla gave way from the 1930s to the long solo song
(ughniyya), taqāsīm retreated to informal gatherings and dance, until Farīd al-Aṭrash wrote long
ʿūd taqāsīm into the instrumental introductions (muqaddima) of his own songs and became the most
celebrated ʿūd player of the Arab world, famous for the right-hand virtuosity with which he ended them. Musicians rate Riyāḍ
al-Sunbāṭī's slower, deeper taqāsīm higher, with their echoes of single notes an octave below;
Marcus also recommends the violinist Aḥmad al-Ḥifnāwī, the Iraqi ʿūd player Munīr Bashīr, Simon
Shaheen and Racy.

In Ottoman and Turkish art music the taksim is the improvised form of a makam ("place", the word
for the mode and for its degrees). A makam is a hierarchy of degrees in an untempered scale,
learned from existing works through its seyir, the characteristic path of its phrases; one scale
can carry several makams, which is why the music passes by ear and resists transcription. The
notes to Kudsi Erguner's UNESCO recording *The Turkish Ney* divide the taksim into four stages:
the makam's most typical phrases first (the notes call this stage zaman; it is usually called
zemin), then further phrases bringing out the relations of its degrees, then a modulation from
one of its degrees (meyan), and last a gradual return to the main degree (karar). A taksim
introduces a composition or links two, such as the measured instrumental prelude (peşrev) and
the saz semaisi in ten beats, and the ney's taksim, rooted in the Mevlevi (whirling dervish)
ceremony, is the favourite instrumental form of the tradition. Halil Altınköprü (2023) names its kinds by place and
purpose: the opening (baş or giriş) taksim, those between pieces (ara) and at the end (son), the
transitional (geçiş) taksim, the fihrist taksim that runs through many makams, the
question-and-answer taksim between two players (karabatak) and the meyan taksim. What sets a
taksim apart from free improvisation, he argues, is the makam, which it prepares for the works
that follow; it has been learned in the meşk tradition by listening, watching and repeating, and
he proposes written template phrases, shown for Rast and Uşşak, as a guide for students.

Güniz Alkaç's study (2025) of a Hicaz taksim on the kanun by Hasan Ferid Alnar, recorded in
Berlin on a 1926–27 tour and about three and a half minutes long, gathers the history. The word,
from Arabic *qism* (a part), appears in Ottoman writing from the early 17th century, according
to Walter Feldman; Suphi Ezgi traced the form to gazels sung without metre; Cantemir has the
singer open the vocal fasıl with a taksim; and only in the late 19th and early 20th centuries,
helped by recording, did the word come to mean chiefly an instrumental improvisation, the vocal
kind being called gazel instead. Recording also brought a new virtuosity, heard in Tanburi Cemil
Bey, Alnar, Şerif Muhittin Targan and Yorgo Bacanos. Writers classify taksims as traditional,
in three parts that keep to the makam's seyir, or free and modern, built around its central
notes rather than its customary path; İhsan Özgen calls the latter "romantic", composed around
an idea and changing mood, rhythm and dynamics within one taksim. Alnar's Hicaz taksim falls
into four sections, an introduction ending on the tonic, two developments and a conclusion, and
Alkaç notates its kanun techniques: flicks of the left hand, vibrato from the tuning levers,
unmeasured tremolo on one string or in octaves, appoggiaturas, accents, glissandi and trills.

A waṣla (plural waṣalāt) is a suite of eight or more pieces, usually in one maqām, that joins
improvisation with composed forms. Instrumental pieces such as the bashraf, the dūlāb and the
samāʿī open it; the samāʿī has four sections (khānāt) with a recurring refrain (taslīm), in a
ten-beat rhythm, and a closing section in another metre. Then come taqsīm and the vocal
improvisations, each a way of tracing the maqām's sayr, its customary path: the layālī, sung on
the stock words *yā layl yā ʿayn* ("O night, O eye"), long since a vehicle for ornament rather than
meaning, usually as the singer's warm-up before the next vocal piece; the mawwāl, improvised on a
colloquial poem in Egyptian, Levantine or Iraqi dialect, such as the seven-line Baghdadi mawwāl
(sabʿāwī, rhyming AAABBBA) loved in Aleppo; and the improvised qaṣīda, on a metred poem in
classical Arabic. Composed songs follow: muwashshaḥāt, the qaṣīda set to music, the Egyptian dawr,
Aleppo's qudūd and popular songs. Ṣāliḥ ʿAbd al-Ḥayy's recording of *Leh yā banafsag* runs in
Rast through an ʿūd taqsīm, a samāʿī, a qānūn taqsīm, the samāʿī's taslīm again and a layālī
before the song itself, a late example of the form. Ali Jihad Racy has described it as a compound
form, a principle of linking pieces rather than a fixed order, and the word also names a section
of a Sufi ceremony. A typical Egyptian order, never a fixed one, moves from composed instrumental
music through freer improvisation to a long vocal close: an ʿūd taqsīm setting out the maqām, a
dūlāb or samāʿī, a second taqsīm on violin or nāy, muwashshaḥāt, a qānūn taqsīm, the singer's
layālī or mawwāl, and a dawr as the climax. Unity comes from the maqām, explored, left for nearby
modes and regained, and from the contrast of free rhythm with the measured rhythmic cycles
(īqāʿāt), not from themes or harmony. A waṣla could last about an hour, and an evening held
several. The AMAR Foundation's *Durūb al-Nagham*, following Nidāʾ Abū Murād, reads it in three
broad stages: the maqām presented in composed, metred pieces, then explored with growing
improvisational freedom, then gathered up in an extended vocal performance. In Aleppo the waṣla
leans on muwashshaḥāt and the lighter qudūd ḥalabiyya, which Jonathan Shannon has studied. It is the Egyptian and Levantine cousin of the Ottoman fasıl and the
Andalusian nūba. From the 1930s the long solo song (ughniyya) displaced it in Egyptian
concert life (Marcus 1993).

## Radif and dastgāh

Persian classical music is learned as the radif ("order"): an ordered body of short melodic
models (gushe-hā) handed down from master to pupil by ear and memorised whole, grouped into
modal systems standardised in the 19th century as Persian music left the older maqām system.
Most writers count seven dastgāh-hā, Shur, Segāh, Navā, Homāyun, Chahārgāh, Māhur and
Rāst-Panjgāh, with Shur and Māhur called the mothers, and treat Abu ʿAtā, Dashti, Afshāri and
Bayāt-e Tork (under Shur) and Bayāt-e Esfahān (under Homāyun) as āvāz-hā, offshoots; Hormoz
Farhat and others count these, sometimes with Bayāt-e Kord, as dastgāhs, making twelve. The
word is said to mean the hand's (dast) position (gāh) on the instrument, and it names both a
group of modes and the opening mode the group returns to, so it is not quite a "mode". Each
dastgāh has seven basic notes plus variable ones (moteghayyer) that appear in two forms, and
assigns its notes roles: a finalis for closing cadences, a starting note (āghāz), a stopping
note for other phrases (ist) and a prominent note (shāhed). A dastgāh holds perhaps ten to
thirty gushe-hā and follows a set course (sayr). It opens with its darāmad, usually the lowest
in range, which sets the mode, then moves through gushe-hā in rising order, each centred on a
higher shāhed with its own ist, to the climax (owj), and returns to the opening mode by a
forud. Some gushe-hā recur across dastgāhs, kereshme in all of
them; others, such as Delkash in Māhur or Hesār in Chahārgāh, alter degrees of the scale and
let the player modulate to another dastgāh, and a few, Bayāt-e Kord among them, are becoming
dastgāhs of their own. Most gushe-hā have no fixed metre: rhythm is regular, irregular (lang)
or free, shaped by the metre of the poetry. The voice (āvāz) sings classical poetry, answered
phrase by phrase by an instrument such as the tār or santur; metric pieces frame it, the
pishdarāmad before, the chahārmezrāb and the dance-like reng at the end, the last two
instrumental only.

The radif as we have it is attributed to ʿAli Akbar Farāhāni, a tār player at the court of
Nāser al-Din Shāh, and was passed on through his sons Mirzā ʿAbdollāh (d. 1918), whose version
is the most widely taught, and Āqā Hosseinqoli. When the systematic ordering began is disputed:
Bruno Nettl traces it to at least the 18th century, Dariush Talāʾi only to the 19th, and the
word radif appears only in late Qajar sources, after the older maqām system had given way to
dastgāhs. Dervish Khān took it out of the court into public concerts. Ali-Naqi Vaziri made the
first transcription in the late 1910s, Musā Maʿrufi's government edition of 1963 lists 470
gushe-hā, Jean During published Mirzā ʿAbdollāh's radif as Nur-ʿAli Borumand played it, and
Dariush Pirniākān Āqā Hosseinqoli's in 2001. Each master's radif differs, and there are
regional ones, of Isfahan, Shiraz, Qazvin and Tehran; Nettl found the music changed little
between recordings while the names of gushe-hā changed more. Critics have called it an
incoherent collection (Farhat) or the music of one region, Farāhān, frozen (Kayhan Kalhor), and
Mohammad-Reza Shajarian compared confining music to the radif to forbidding poetry because Hafez
had already written the best of it. UNESCO inscribed it as intangible heritage in 2009. The
Wikipedia article draws largely on Persian sources and several of its footnotes cite unrelated
papers, so its details are best checked against Nettl, Farhat and During.

# Composition / East and Southeast Asia

## Gamelan colotomic form

Gamelan, from the Javanese *gamel*, to strike, is the gong-chime ensemble of Java, Sunda and Bali,
inscribed by UNESCO in 2021. Musicians much like it already appear on the 8th-century reliefs of
Borobudur, and the instruments took their present form under the Majapahit kingdom. Each
gamelan is tuned as a set, to sléndro or pélog, and Colin McPhee remarked that tunings differ so
much that there are as many scales as gamelans; in Bali paired instruments are tuned slightly
apart so that their beating (ombak, "wave") shimmers. The music passed by ear; the courts of
Yogyakarta and Surakarta wrote it down in the 19th century only to preserve it, and kepatihan
cipher notation replaced their grid notation around 1900. Central Javanese gamelan pieces (gendhing) are built in cycles marked off by gongs, a structure
called colotomic, a word Jaap Kunst coined from the Greek for a rhythmic unit and for cutting; it
has since been applied to Japanese gagaku and Thai piphat too. The skeletal melody, the balungan, is grouped in gatra of
four beats whose last note, the seleh, is the goal. The large gong ends each cycle (gongan);
kenong, kempul and kethuk divide it at fixed points, so that a ladrang of 32 beats, for
example, has four kenong strokes and kempul between them. Faster instruments elaborate the
balungan at multiples of its density, saron peking and bonang among them, and the soft
instruments (gendèr, rebab, voice) weave around it. The drummer (kendhang) leads changes of
tempo and of irama, the density level: in each slower irama the elaborating parts play twice
as many notes per balungan beat. The gongs' Javanese names
imitate their sounds, and in kepatihan notation their strokes are marks over the balungan
numbers. Each structure (bentuk) nests its divisions, mostly in fours, so that the bigger gongs
fall on the stressed ends of the groups. A lancaran is 16 beats: the gong once, the kenong four
times, the kempul between (silent at the first point, the wela) and the kethuk between those; a
ketawang is 16 beats with two kenong strokes, and a ladrang 32 with four, both adding the
kempyang between every beat. The long gendhing proper has a slow merong and a minggah, each one
gong cycle of four kenong phrases, with no kempul, the kethuk strokes sparse (arang) or frequent
(kerep) and a single merong cycle possibly lasting 256 beats and many minutes. The shadow-play
(wayang) forms, ayak-ayakan, srepeg and the fast sampak, have gong cycles of varying length,
with kenong and kempul on every goal note, and may stop wherever the drummer signals. A piece
starts with a solo introduction (buka) and ends with a slowing (suwuk). Each piece belongs to a tuning, sléndro or pélog, and a pathet.

*In PHI*: a balungan in gatra of four in a ladrang's gong cycle (default sléndro pathet
manyura), kenong, kempul, kethuk and gong at its points, peking and bonang elaborating it twice

## Jo-ha-kyū

Jo-ha-kyū ("beginning, break, rapid") is a Japanese principle of tempo and form: everything
should begin slowly, gather speed and end swiftly. It came from the way gagaku, the court music,
was described, where a suite opens with an unmetred prelude (netori) and pieces accelerate, and
spread to the tea ceremony, the martial arts, linked verse (Nijō Yoshimoto required it for renga
in 1356) and above all the theatre. Zeami (c. 1363–1443) treated it as the pattern of all motion
and made it the rule of nō, applied to a day's programme, to a play and to each action within it,
so that every part also quickens within itself. His ideal play in *Sandō* has five sections
(dan): an auspicious, slow jo; three of ha building tension, the climax in the third; and a
quick kyū returning to calm. Jōruri and kabuki kept the five-act plan, which the chanter
Takemoto Gidayū described as love, battle, a tragic climax, a journey of song and dance (michiyuki)
and a swift, happy end. Gagaku ("elegant music"), named for the court music bureau founded in 701 and settled in
form by the 10th century, joins native song and dance with music from Tang China and from Korea,
grouped as music of the left (tōgaku) and of the right (komagaku, which survives only as dance).
It is played as kangen, concert music, as bugaku, dance music without strings, and as sung
poetry, mostly on the pentatonic yo scale. Every player plays the one melody from memory, each in
an instrument's own version, in eight distinct parts: the hichiriki oboe leads, bending the line
with microtones; the ryūteki flute varies it higher up; the shō mouth organ simplifies it under
chords of five or six notes (aitake); the biwa adds single notes and chords and the koto
repeated patterns; the taiko gives each phrase its main accent, the shōko gong supports, and the
kakko drum leads the ensemble and sets the changes of tempo. Henry Cowell, Messiaen (*Sept
haïkaï*, 1962), Britten (*Curlew River*, 1964), Takemitsu and La Monte Young's drones all drew
on it.

*In PHI*: jo, an unmetred netori; ha, metred and quickening; kyū, fast to a sudden close; with the
gagaku layers (default mode Hyōjō).

## Sanjo

Sanjo ("scattered melodies") is a Korean solo instrumental form, played with janggu drum
accompaniment, that runs through a series of rhythmic cycles (jangdan) from slow to fast:
jinyangjo, jungmori, jungjungmori, jajinmori and hwimori, sometimes ending in danmori. It grew
out of pansori, shamanic music and sinawi improvisation, and counts as folk music (minsogak).
Kim Chang-jo is credited with the first gayageum sanjo around 1890 (sources give his death as
1919 or 1920); sanjo for geomungo, haegeum, ajaeng, piri and others followed, and daegeum sanjo,
from the 1920s, became one of the most popular. As in pansori, the drummer and the listeners
call out encouragement (chuimsae), and a loud response marks a good performance and lifts the
player. Sanjo is not fixed: each master's version is a school (ryu), and players
keep adding their own variations. Its phrases build tension and release it,
in a principle of binding and loosening (maeggo, pulgo). When several instruments play
together each keeps its own version of the line, a sanjo hapju.

*In PHI*: a solo over the janggu through the jangdan from jinyangjo to hwimori (default mode
gyemyeonjo), phrases tied and released; more instruments make a hapju.

## Sǎn-màn-zhōng-kuài-sǎn

Sǎn, màn, zhōng, kuài, sǎn ("free, slow, moderate, fast, free") names the overall shape of much
Chinese instrumental music: one tune (qupai) played first in free rhythm, then in a series of
metrical versions from slow to fast, and free again at the end. The versions are made by banshi
variation, which expands or contracts the tune's metre and fills or thins its notes, as in the
Jiangnan sizhu ("silk and bamboo", strings and winds) music of the lower Yangtze. Its ensembles
date from the Ming and Qing dynasties; by the early 20th century Shanghai's amateur clubs were its
centre, playing for pleasure in teahouses, and their music became the basis of the modern Chinese
orchestra. The instruments are not fixed, usually one of each, with erhu, dizi or xiao, pipa and
yangqin at the core and sheng, sanxian, qinqin and clapper and drum (guban) as available. At the
heart of the repertoire stand the Eight Great Pieces, many grown from one old tune, *Lao liuban*
("Old Six Beats"), whose most important offshoot is *Zhonghua liuban* ("Moderately ornamented Six
Beats"). A tune's phrases often follow the
four-part rhetoric of qǐ, chéng, zhuǎn, hé (opening, continuing, turning, closing), and in the
linking technique yú yǎo wěi ("fish biting tail") each phrase begins on the note the last one
ended on.

*In PHI*: one qupai of four phrases, qǐ, chéng, zhuǎn, hé, linked fish-biting-tail, played free,
slow, moderate, fast and free again.
