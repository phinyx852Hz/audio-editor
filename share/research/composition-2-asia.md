# Composition / South and West Asia

## Rāga development

A Hindustani performance unfolds a rāga in stages, from free to strict time and from slow to
fast. The ālāp, unmetred, introduces the rāga note by note from the lower Sa upward, dwelling
on its strongest notes, the vādī and samvādī, and on its characteristic phrases (pakaḍ), a
gradual expansion called baṛhat; it reaches the upper Sa and returns. In instrumental music the
joṛ adds a steady pulse and the jhālā quickens it with rapid strokes on the drone strings
(chikārī). Then the composition, a gat for instruments or a bandish for voice, enters with the
tablā in a tāla such as tīntāl (16 beats), alternating the fixed tune with improvisation, and
sections often close with a tihāī, a phrase played three times to land on sam, the first beat
of the cycle. The tānpūrā's drone sounds throughout.

*In PHI*: Compose > South and West Asia > Rāga Development (default rāga Yaman) runs ālāp, joṛ,
jhālā and a gat in tīntāl ending in a tihāī on sam, over the tānpūrā.

## Maqām: taqsīm and waṣla

In Arab music a taqsīm is an unmetred solo improvisation that sets out a maqām. It begins on
the maqām's tonic (qarār) in its lowest jins (a tetrachord or pentachord), climbs through its
ajnās to the dominant (ghammāz) and the upper octave (jawāb), often modulates to a neighbouring
maqām, and ends with a cadence (qafla) home. A waṣla is a suite of pieces in one maqām, joining
taqsīm with composed forms: an instrumental samāʿī in four sections (khānāt) with a recurring
refrain (taslīm), in a ten-beat rhythm and a closing section in another metre; vocal
muwashshaḥāt; and further improvisation. It is the Levantine and Egyptian cousin of the
Ottoman fasıl and the Andalusian nūba.

*In PHI*: a taqsīm through the maqām's ajnās (default Rast) to the jawāb, a modulation and a
qafla, then a waṣla of a samāʿī with its taslīm and a muwashshaḥ.

## Radif and dastgāh

Persian classical music is learned as the radif, a body of several hundred short melodic
models (gushe-hā) handed down from master to pupil and grouped into twelve modal systems, seven
dastgāh-hā and five āvāz-hā. A performance of a dastgāh opens with its darāmad, which sets the
mode, then moves through gushe-hā in rising order, each centred on a higher note (shāhed) with
its own resting note (ist), to the climax (owj), and returns to the opening mode by a forud. The
voice (āvāz) sings classical poetry in free rhythm, answered phrase by phrase by an instrument
such as the tār or santur; metric pieces frame it, the pishdarāmad before, the chahārmezrāb and
the dance-like reng at the end. The radif of Mirzā ʿAbdollāh (d. 1918) is the most widely
taught, and UNESCO inscribed the radif as intangible heritage in 2009.

*In PHI*: a dastgāh's gushe-hā in radif order (default Shur) to the owj and the forud, the
voice answered by the tār, a chahārmezrāb and a reng to close.

# Composition / East and Southeast Asia

## Gamelan colotomic form

Central Javanese gamelan pieces (gendhing) are built in cycles marked off by gongs, a structure
called colotomic, after Jaap Kunst. The skeletal melody, the balungan, is grouped in gatra of
four beats whose last note, the seleh, is the goal. The large gong ends each cycle (gongan);
kenong, kempul and kethuk divide it at fixed points, so that a ladrang of 32 beats, for
example, has four kenong strokes and kempul between them. Faster instruments elaborate the
balungan at multiples of its density, saron peking and bonang among them, and the soft
instruments (gendèr, rebab, voice) weave around it. The drummer (kendhang) leads changes of
tempo and of irama, the density level: in each slower irama the elaborating parts play twice
as many notes per balungan beat. A piece starts with a solo introduction (buka) and ends with a
slowing (suwuk). Each piece belongs to a tuning, sléndro or pélog, and a pathet.

*In PHI*: a balungan in gatra of four in a ladrang's gong cycle (default sléndro pathet
manyura), kenong, kempul, kethuk and gong at its points, peking and bonang elaborating it twice
as densely in irama II, with a buka and a suwuk.

## Jo-ha-kyū

Jo-ha-kyū is a Japanese principle of tempo and form: jo, a slow beginning; ha, a "breaking"
that develops and quickens; kyū, a rapid close. It comes from gagaku, the court music, where a
suite opens with an unmetred prelude (netori) and pieces accelerate, and Zeami (c. 1363–1443)
made it the rule of nō, applying it to a day's programme, to a play and to each phrase within
it, so that every part also quickens within itself. In the gagaku ensemble the ryūteki flute
and hichiriki oboe play the melody in heterophony, the shō mouth organ holds tone clusters
(aitake), the koto and biwa mark the beats with patterns, and the kakko, taiko and shōko drums
keep the cycle.

*In PHI*: jo, an unmetred netori; ha, metred and quickening; kyū, fast to a sudden close; with the
gagaku layers (default mode Hyōjō).

## Sanjo

Sanjo ("scattered melodies") is a Korean solo instrumental form, played with janggu drum
accompaniment, that runs through a series of rhythmic cycles (jangdan) from slow to fast:
jinyangjo, jungmori, jungjungmori, jajinmori and hwimori, sometimes ending in danmori. It grew
out of shamanic music and sinawi improvisation, and Kim Chang-jo (1865–1919) is credited with
the first gayageum sanjo in the 1890s; sanjo for geomungo, daegeum, haegeum, ajaeng and piri
followed. Each master's version is a school (ryu). Its phrases build tension and release it,
in a principle of binding and loosening (maeggo, pulgo). When several instruments play
together each keeps its own version of the line, a sanjo hapju.

*In PHI*: a solo over the janggu through the jangdan from jinyangjo to hwimori (default mode
gyemyeonjo), phrases tied and released; more instruments make a hapju.

- [Sanjo (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjo_(music)),
  [Jangdan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jangdan) and
  [Gayageum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gayageum), Wikipedia.
- Keith Howard, *Korean Music: A Listening Guide* (Academy of Korean Studies, 2006).
- Robert C. Provine, "Sanjo", *Grove Music Online*.

## Sǎn-màn-zhōng-kuài-sǎn

Sǎn, màn, zhōng, kuài, sǎn ("free, slow, moderate, fast, free") names the overall shape of much
Chinese instrumental music: one tune (qupai) played first in free rhythm, then in a series of
metrical versions from slow to fast, and free again at the end. The versions are made by banshi
variation, which expands or contracts the tune's metre and fills or thins its notes, as in the
Jiangnan sizhu ("silk and bamboo") music of Shanghai, where *Lao liuban* ("Old Six Beats") is
the source of slower versions such as *Zhonghua liuban*. A tune's phrases often follow the
four-part rhetoric of qǐ, chéng, zhuǎn, hé (opening, continuing, turning, closing), and in the
linking technique yú yǎo wěi ("fish biting tail") each phrase begins on the note the last one
ended on.

*In PHI*: one qupai of four phrases, qǐ, chéng, zhuǎn, hé, linked fish-biting-tail, played free,
slow, moderate, fast and free again.
