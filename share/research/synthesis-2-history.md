# Synthesis / History

## From the telegraph to the Telharmonium

Electrical sound began as a by-product of the telephone. In 1874–76 Elisha Gray found that a
self-vibrating electromagnetic circuit made a steady tone, and built the **musical telegraph**,
with an oscillator for each key sending tones down a telegraph line: the first electrical
instrument. Thaddeus Cahill's **Telharmonium** (patented 1897, played from 1906) was the first
additive synthesizer: huge dynamos, tonewheels turning at the frequency of each harmonic, mixed
to make a timbre and sent by telephone to subscribers in New York. It weighed some 200 tonnes,
interfered with the phone lines and went out of business by 1914, but its principle returned in
the Hammond organ.

## Vacuum tubes and the first electronic instruments (1920–1950)

The vacuum tube made oscillators small. Léon Theremin's **theremin** (about 1920), played
without touch by moving the hands near two antennas, was the first to be heard widely. Maurice
Martenot's **ondes Martenot** (1928), with its ribbon and keyboard, is still written for, in
Messiaen's *Turangalîla* and many film scores. Friedrich Trautwein's **Trautonium** (1930),
played by Oskar Sala, used subharmonics and gave Hitchcock's *The Birds* its sound. Laurens
Hammond's tonewheel **organ** (1935) made additive synthesis a fixture of churches, jazz and rock;
his **Novachord** (1939) was a polyphonic, subtractive synthesizer in all but name. Hugh Le
Caine's **Electronic Sackbut** (1945–48) had touch-sensitive keys that changed pitch, timbre
and loudness: the first voltage-controlled synthesizer.

## The studios and the first computers (1950s)

After the war electronic music was made in studios, with oscillators, filters and tape. Pierre
Schaeffer's **musique concrète** in Paris (1948) worked from recordings; the West German radio
studio in Cologne (1951), with Herbert Eimert and Karlheinz Stockhausen, built sounds from sine
waves; the BBC Radiophonic Workshop (1958), with Daphne Oram and Delia Derbyshire, made the
*Doctor Who* theme. RCA's **Mark II Sound Synthesizer** (1957), at the Columbia-Princeton
Electronic Music Center, was programmed with punched paper and gave the synthesizer its name.
Evgeny Murzin's **ANS** (1958) in Moscow played spectra drawn on glass, and Daphne Oram's
**Oramics** (1960s) played drawn shapes on film.

Computers joined in. Australia's CSIRAC played tunes in 1951; in 1957 Max Mathews at Bell Labs
wrote **MUSIC I** for the IBM 704, the first program to synthesize sound digitally, and the
ancestor of every synthesis language since.

## Voltage control and the modular synthesizer (1960s)

Robert Moog's paper "Voltage-Controlled Electronic Music Modules" (1964) set out the idea that
made the synthesizer an instrument: every module controlled by a voltage, one volt per octave, so
a keyboard, an envelope or another oscillator could play anything. His **modular systems** went
to studios, universities and bands; Wendy Carlos's ***Switched-On Bach*** (1968), played on one,
was a best seller and made the Moog a household word. In California Don Buchla built his
**Buchla 100** (from 1963) for Morton Subotnick and the San Francisco Tape Music Center, the
start of the West Coast school. In London, EMS's **VCS 3** (1969) was small enough for a desk.

## The portable and polyphonic synthesizer (1970s)

The **Minimoog** (1970) put the modular's best path, three oscillators, the ladder filter and two
envelopes, in a box with a keyboard and no cables, and took the synthesizer on stage. ARP's 2600
and Odyssey, Korg's MS-20 and Roland's SH series followed. Polyphony came next: Oberheim's
Four-Voice, Yamaha's CS-80 (1977, the sound of *Blade Runner*) and the **Prophet-5** (1978), whose
microprocessor stored every setting of every patch. Meanwhile digital synthesis left the
mainframe: John Chowning published **FM** (1973), and the Synclavier (1977) and the **Fairlight
CMI** (1979) became the first digital synthesizers and samplers a studio could buy.

## Digital synthesis and MIDI (1980s)

In 1983 Dave Smith of Sequential and Ikutaro Kakehashi of Roland led the industry to **MIDI**, a
common language that let any keyboard play any synthesizer and, soon, any computer sequence
them. The same year Yamaha's **DX7** made digital FM affordable and sold in the hundreds of
thousands. Digital methods multiplied: the PPG Wave's wavetables (1981), Casio's phase
distortion (CZ-101, 1984), Sequential's vector synthesis (Prophet VS, 1986), Roland's linear
arithmetic (D-50, 1987). Sampling became affordable with the E-mu Emulator (1981), the Ensoniq
Mirage (1984) and the Akai MPC (1988); drum machines, Roland's TR-808 and TR-909 and the TB-303,
gave birth to hip hop, house and techno. The Korg M1 (1988), samples, effects and a sequencer in
one keyboard, became the best-selling synth of all and the model of the workstation. In homes,
the sound chips of the Commodore 64, the NES and the Amiga made chiptune and trackers.

## Workstations, physical models and virtual analogue (1990s)

The 1990s refined digital hardware and began to model what came before. The Korg Wavestation
(1990) sequenced waves; General MIDI (1991) and SoundFont put a standard set of sampled sounds in
every PC; Yamaha's **VL1** (1994) was the first commercial physical modelling synth, from Julius
Smith's waveguides at Stanford; Clavia's **Nord Lead** (1995) modelled analogue circuits in
software on a DSP and named virtual analogue, followed by the Access Virus and the Roland
JP-8000. The Doepfer A-100 (1996) began **Eurorack**. On the computer, Csound, Max and the new
Pure Data and SuperCollider (both 1996) made real-time synthesis on a desktop machine possible.

## Software synthesizers and plugins (1996–2010)

When personal computers grew fast enough to synthesize in real time, the synthesizer became
software. Steinberg's **VST** (1996) let effects run inside Cubase, and VST 2.0 (1999) added
instruments; Apple's Audio Units, and on Linux LADSPA, DSSI and **LV2**, followed. Propellerhead's
**ReBirth** (1997) put a TB-303 and a TR-808 on screen, and its **Reason** (2000) a whole rack.
Native Instruments, from Generator (1996, later **Reaktor**), made modular software and then
virtual instruments of every kind: the B4 organ, the Pro-52 Prophet, FM7, Absynth, Kontakt and
**Massive** (2007). Arturia's emulations of the Minimoog and the Moog modular (from 2003) began
the virtual vintage collection. Free plugins such as Synth1, modelled on the Nord Lead 2, put a
synthesizer on every computer.

## The modular revival and open source (2010–2020)

Two movements shaped the 2010s. Hardware came back: Eurorack grew to thousands of modules,
Moog, Korg and Behringer reissued their classics, and small, cheap synths (Korg's Volca,
Teenage Engineering's OP-1) were everywhere. And software opened up: **Xfer Serum** (2014) made
the drawn wavetable the sound of dance music; VCV **Rack** (2017) brought Eurorack to the computer
as open source; Vember Audio's **Surge** became open source (2018) as Surge XT; Matt Tytel released
**Helm** and then **Vital** (2020) free; and MPE (2018) gave each note its own pitch bend and
pressure, for controllers like the ROLI Seaboard and the LinnStrument.

PHI ships several of these open source instruments: Helm, Cardinal (VCV Rack), Vaporizer2,
OctaSine and others; the built-in Synth plays MPE.

## Today's virtual synths (2020 onward)

Today nearly every synthesis method of the last century runs as software, often on the same
computer at once: faithful models of the Minimoog, the Prophet-5, the DX7 and the Jupiter-8
beside hybrid synths that combine wavetable, FM, granular and physical modelling in one patch.
The **CLAP** plugin format (Bitwig and u-he, 2022) added per-note modulation and better use of
many cores; **MIDI 2.0** (2020) finer resolution and two-way conversation between instruments.
Open source synths, Surge XT, Vital, Dexed, Cardinal, Odin 2, stand beside commercial ones.
Neural synthesis, from DDSP and RAVE to models that turn text into music, is the newest
addition, and the oldest ideas, additive and modular, are back in new forms.

PHI is one of these: its Synth menu loads open source instruments as CLAP and VST3 plugins, and
any VST3, LV2 or CLAP instrument can play a mixer track.
