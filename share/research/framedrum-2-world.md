# Frame Drums / Around the world

## West Asia and North Africa

The frame drum's oldest home is still its richest. The **tar** is the plain frame drum of the
Arab world and North Africa: a single goat skin on a wooden hoop, held upright in one hand and
struck with the fingers of both. The **bendir** of the Maghreb is the same drum with snares of
gut stretched across the inside of the head, so that every stroke buzzes; the player's thumb
hooks through a hole in the frame, and it is the drum of Sufi ceremonies as well as of village
music. The **daf** of Iran and Kurdistan, also **dap**, **dayereh** or **dâyere**, is larger,
often with metal rings hung inside the frame, and played with the finger snaps that give it its
sharp, rapid accents; Sufi orders play it in their ceremonies of remembrance, and it has become
a concert instrument of Persian classical music. The smaller **doira** of Uzbekistan and
Tajikistan and the **ghaval** of Azerbaijan are its Central Asian and Caucasian cousins.

The **riq** (riqq) is the Arab tambourine and the timekeeper of the Egyptian and Syrian *takht*
ensemble: a small frame, about 20 cm across, with five pairs of heavy brass jingles set in
slots in the frame and a head of fish skin, now often plastic. The riq player often leads the
ensemble. The **mazhar** is its large, heavy-headed relative, with big brass jingles shaken for
a roar of sound; the Egyptian percussionist Hossam Ramzy was one of its masters. The **tof** of
the Hebrew Bible, the drum of Miriam, is a frame drum of the same family, and the **duff** or
**def** gives its name to the whole family in Arabic, Turkish and the languages of the Balkans.

## Europe

The frame drum came to medieval Europe through Muslim Spain and the Crusades, and stayed in its
folk music. The **adufe** of Portugal and Spain is square, of pine, with a goat skin sewn over
both faces and seeds or pebbles inside that rattle as it is struck; in the Beira region of
Portugal it was traditionally a women's drum, played in religious processions and at festivals.
The **pandero cuadrado** of Galicia and León is its Spanish twin. In southern Italy the
**tamburello** and the larger **tammorra**, hung with jingles once cut from tin cans, drive the
tarantella and the *pizzica* of Apulia, the dance once said to cure the bite of the tarantula,
and the *tammurriata* of Campania; Alessandra Belloni has taught them in America since the 1980s.

The **bodhrán** of Ireland is held upright by a hand inside the frame, which presses the skin to
change its pitch, and struck with a double-ended wooden beater, the tipper or cipín. Its origins
are disputed: a ritual drum of the wren boys and mummers of the southwest, a tray or sieve for
grain made into a drum, or a tambourine that lost its jingles in the 19th century. It became
the drum of Irish traditional music only in the revival of the 1960s, when Seán Ó Riada made it
part of Ceoltóirí Chualann and the Chieftains carried it abroad. Johnny "Ringo" McDonagh of De
Dannan introduced brush beaters and rim shots. The jingled **tambourine** itself, the *buben* of
Russia and Ukraine, entered the Western orchestra in the 18th century.

## South Asia

The **kanjira** of South India is a small frame drum of jackfruit wood, 7 to 9 inches across,
with a head of monitor lizard skin and a single pair of jingles. As a folk and devotional drum it
is centuries old; its place on the classical stage, beside the mridangam in Carnatic concerts, is
credited to Manpoondia Pillai, a temple lantern-bearer who in the 1880s took up drumming and
reduced it to its single pair of jingles. The player wets the skin from inside to bring the
pitch down and bends it with the fingers of the holding hand; its split-hand fingerings were
among the techniques Glen Velez carried into the modern lap style. The lizard is now protected,
and modern drums use goat skin or plastic. The **daf** and smaller **dafli** of North India, and
the **dappu** of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, are the frame drums of the north and of popular
festivals.

## East and Southeast Asia

In the Malay world the **rebana**, a frame drum of the Islamic tradition, accompanies devotional
singing and the *zikir*, and is played in ensembles of interlocking drums of several sizes. In
Japan the **uchiwa-daiko**, a fan-shaped drum with a handle, is beaten by followers of Nichiren
Buddhism as they chant; in Korea the **sogo** is the small handled drum of the farmers' bands of
*pungmul*, played by dancers. Chinese tradition has the **bajiaogu**, an octagonal tambourine
of the narrative singing of the north.

- [Rebana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebana), Wikipedia.

## The Americas

The **pandeiro** of Brazil is a tambourine with a tunable head and jingles, rocked between the
thumb and the fingertips and heel of the hand so that a single drum plays bass, tone and jingle
together; it is the drum of samba, choro and capoeira, and the singer Jackson do Pandeiro took
his name from it. The **tamborim**, small and without jingles, is struck with a
stick in the samba schools. The octagonal **pandero jarocho** belongs to the son jarocho of
Veracruz, and the **pandereta** of Puerto Rico, played in sets of three sizes, to the *plena*.

The hand drums of Native North America, a skin laced over a hoop and struck with a padded beater,
belong to song, dance and ceremony, and in the far north to the shamans; the **qilaut** of the
Inuit is among them.
