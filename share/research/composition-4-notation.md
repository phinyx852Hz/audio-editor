# Composition / Notation

## Staff notation and PHI's microtonal staff

The five-line staff grew from the neumes written over chant in the 9th century; Guido of Arezzo
(c. 1025) set them on lines a third apart, with clefs, and mensural notation (13th to 16th
centuries) gave the notes measured lengths, the ancestor of today's whole, half and quarter
notes. The staff was made for seven notes to the octave, with sharps and flats between, so
music in other tunings needs more: quarter-tone accidentals (used since the early 20th century
by Alois Hába and others), arrows and numbers of cents for just intonation, as in the Extended
Helmholtz-Ellis accidentals of Marc Sabat and Wolfgang von Schweinitz, and the Sagittal system
of George Secor and Dave Keenan for any tuning.

*In PHI*: Compose > New Microtonal Score opens a blank score on PHI's own microtonal staff in the
Mode menu's tuning; New Score In picks any of the notations below, and Each Tradition's Own
chooses the one that belongs to the mode.

  [Mensural notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mensural_notation) and
  [Microtonal music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microtonal_music), Wikipedia.
- [Sagittal](https://sagittal.org), the notation's own site.

## Hindustani sargam (Bhatkhande)

Indian music names its seven degrees by syllables, the sargam: Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni. Vishnu
Narayan Bhatkhande (1860–1936) set down a notation for Hindustani music in Devanagari or Roman
letters: a line under a letter for a flat (komal) note, a vertical stroke above Ma for the
sharp (tivra) Ma, a dot below for the lower octave and above for the upper, notes grouped by
beat, and the tāla's divisions marked with sam (×), khālī (0) and numbered tālīs. Vishnu
Digambar Paluskar devised a rival system. Bhatkhande's is the one used in his anthology
*Kramik Pustak Malika* and in most printed collections since.

David Courtney traces how it came about. Notation in India (swar lipi) is old:
- **Vedic chant:** Rigveda texts mark three pitches, one raised by a stroke above, one plain and
  one by a line below. Scholars disagree, sometimes bitterly, over which of udātta, anudātta and
  svarita is which. The Sāmaveda, a song book, needed more pitches and in some texts uses numbers.
- **Treatises:** the *Nāṭyaśāstra*, Matanga's *Bṛhaddeśī* (said to be the first to name notes by
  something like the present sargam) and Śārṅgadeva's *Saṅgītaratnākara* (13th century) all
  needed some notation, though verse hides how it worked in practice.
- **Under the sultanates and Mughals:** musicians mostly guarded their compositions and did not
  write them down.
- **After 1858:** the old courts lost their power to patronise, musicians turned to the new middle
  class, and books on music multiplied, each with a notation of its own:
  - Sadiq Ali Khan's *Sarmaye Ishrat* (1874) wrote sitar music by its frets;
  - Pannalal Goswami's *Nad Vinod* (1896) put duration marks over sargam;
  - Paluskar's system, around 1900, fixed every note's length exactly. A pupil, B. R. Deodhar,
    links that to Paluskar learning Western staff notation from a bandleader.

Paluskar's system spread through his Gandharva Mahavidyalaya schools (Lahore 1901, Bombay 1908),
but it was cluttered. From the second quarter of the century, Bhatkhande's took its place, and
by 2000 Paluskar's was all but gone. Bhatkhande's change was rhythmic. Instead of writing each
note's length, he fitted everything into the beat (mātrā), which made the page cleaner. The cost
is tālas with half beats, such as 8½ or 9½ mātrās, which are awkward to write; Paluskar's system
handled them better. In Courtney's summary:
- a dash marks a rest;
- an arc beneath joins notes that share a beat;
- grace notes (kan) sit above the beat;
- the tāla marks may go above or below the line.

The system names no script, so it is written in Devanagari, Roman, Kannada, Urdu and others.
Ustad Mahfooz Khokhar's *Rag Swaroop* (Pakistan, 1998) writes it in Urdu, right to left, and puts
an X on every clap, X1 X2 0 X3 for tīntāl.

The dates of *Kramik Pustak Malika* vary by source. Courtney gives 1920 for the first volume;
bibliographies give 1919 or 1920, then 1921 and 1922 for the next two, with the six volumes
complete by 1937, the year after Bhatkhande died. It was written in Marathi, and the Hindi
editions most students use came later.

In his second part Courtney weighs ways of carrying the notation abroad:
- **Staff notation** reaches the most readers but misleads. It implies a fixed key, where Indian
  musicians transpose freely; the habit of writing everything in C is seldom explained. It also
  implies equal temperament.
- **Bhatkhande in Roman letters** keeps the music intact, as in Ravi Shankar's 1968 sitar
  examples, which add signs for sitar technique and leave the inner claps implied. It still asks
  the reader to know the theory.
- **Sargam alongside staff** gives both at once.
- **Braille:** there is also an Indian notation for blind readers.

Typewriters and computers brought other versions. The Ali Akbar College of Music, from the late
1960s, used case to show the note's form: S r R g G m M P d D n N, lower case for komal Re, Ga,
Dha and Ni and for shuddha Ma, upper case for shuddha notes and tivra Ma. Its students carried
it onto the early internet, where it is still common, though Courtney thinks Unicode has made
it unnecessary. Others changed the software instead. Systems that showed Bhatkhande notation on
screen and played it date from 1987, and Courtney's own from about 1990 ran on a Commodore 64;
today a few redrawn font characters or SVG do the job.

Ornaments have signs of their own. An arc under a group of notes (chhand) means they all fall
within one beat, as in fast tāns. Meend, a smooth glide from one note to another touching those
between, is marked by an arc over the notes, so that a slide from komal Dha down to komal Re
through tivra Ma and Ga, as heard in Rāg Shree, is written as one gesture. A kan, a quick touch
of another note before the main one, is written small and raised before it: Ma begun with a
touch of Ga. A murki, a short, quick turn of three or four neighbouring notes, is shown by
brackets around a note, and which notes it uses is left to the rāga and the performer.

A Bhatkhande page sets out a composition (bandish) in rows of three for each line: the notes, the
syllables of the text beneath them, and the tāla marks at the bottom. Vertical lines divide the
page into the tāla's sections, four for tīntāl. Writing starts at the left edge even when the song
begins mid-cycle, and the marks show where the cycle falls. A hyphen holds the previous note for
another beat, and a sign like a large S (the avagraha, ऽ) holds the syllable. The reader is
expected to know the tāla already, so the marks are few. Notation of this kind is for teaching
and remembering, not for performing from: a performance is improvised, and printed compositions
give a bare outline of the melody and leave ornaments to be learnt by ear. Websites and teachers
adapt the system for typing, each in their own way. On Raag Hindustani, for instance, a
spreadsheet column stands for each beat, a tilde stretches a vowel, and brackets mark grace notes.
Staff versions usually put the tonic on C and use a key signature for the rāga's altered notes.
Rāg Yaman, with its sharp Ma, takes one sharp, though it is not G major.

## Carnatic swaras

South Indian (Carnatic) music uses the same seven syllables, sa ri ga ma pa da ni, but names
twelve-plus pitch positions with numbered variants: ri1, ri2, ri3, ga1, ga2, ga3 and so on, where
some share a pitch (ri2 and ga1, for example), as the 72 melakarta rāgas require. Octaves are
dotted as in Hindustani notation, and the tāla's divisions (laghu, drutam, anudrutam) are marked
with bars. Written notation is a guide; gamakas, the ornaments that make a rāga, are learned by
ear.

- [Svara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svara) and
  [Melakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melakarta), Wikipedia.

## Jianpu (numbered notation)

Jianpu writes the degrees of a scale as numbers, 1 to 7 for do to ti, with 0 for a rest, dots
above or below for octaves, underlines to halve a note's length and dashes to extend it. It
descends from the cipher notation Jean-Jacques Rousseau proposed in 1742 and the Galin-Paris-Chevé
system of 19th-century France, came to Japan and China around 1900, and is now the everyday
notation for Chinese music, folk song and much popular music in East and Southeast Asia.

- [Numbered musical notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_musical_notation) and
  [Galin-Paris-Chevé system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galin-Paris-Chev%C3%A9_system),
  Wikipedia.

## Kepatihan (gamelan)

Kepatihan is the cipher notation of Javanese gamelan, devised around 1900 at the Kepatihan, the
chief minister's palace in Surakarta. The pitches of a gamelan's sléndro (1 2 3 5 6) and pélog
(1 to 7) tunings are written as numbers, with dots for octaves, and the balungan is written in
groups of four (gatra) with symbols over them for the gong, kenong, kempul and kethuk strokes.
It records only the skeleton; the elaborating parts are improvised from it within each
instrument's idiom.

It grew from the Galin-Paris-Chevé number system that missionaries brought for hymns, and it
displaced several Javanese notations of the same years, which were first meant for archives
rather than for playing. A dot above a number raises it an octave, and two dots raise it two; a
dot below lowers it. The numbers are names, not Western degrees. Sléndro can sound roughly like
do re mi sol la, but pélog is just numbered from low to high, and its 4 and 7 act like
accidentals: 4 as a raised 3 or a lowered 5, 7 as a lowered 1, depending on the pathet.

Every number lasts one beat unless marked. A line over notes halves them, though this is often
written loosely. A dot standing in for a number holds the previous note; it is not a rest. Only
vocal parts write rests, as 0, since the instruments play on through. Gong strokes have marks
that vary from book to book, and one common set is:
- a circle for gong ageng, brackets for gong suwukan;
- ^ for kenong, ˇ for kempul;
- + for kethuk, – for kempyang.

They are often left out, since the form implies them. Sundanese notation runs the other way: 1
is the highest note, and a dot above lowers the octave.

There are older scripts too. Surakarta's set the balungan on a horizontal staff, with a wavy
line for the singer's free rhythm, and Yogyakarta's "chequered" notation used a ladder-like
vertical staff with dots for the balungan and signs for the main drum strokes. Staff
transcriptions are common in scholarship. They fit poorly, because the heaviest beat in
gamelan is the last of each group of four, not the first. In the 1920s Walter Spies arranged
gamelan pieces for piano at the Yogyakarta kraton.

The notation moved onto computers through fonts that map signs to a US keyboard, such as a dot
below on the key row below the numbers:
- Kepatihan, by Carter Scholz (1987);
- Matthew Arciniega's expanded font (1994), which Raymond Weisling made into KepatihanPro for
  Windows (2000), now the standard;
- the Yogyakarta kraton's Kridhamardawa (2020).

SIL's Doulos SIL Cipher (2014) puts such signs at standard Unicode points and also covers
jianpu.

## Jeongganbo (Korean)

Jeongganbo is the Korean mensural notation invented under King Sejong in the 15th century, the
first East Asian notation to show rhythm exactly. The page is ruled into columns of squares
(jeonggan), each square one unit of time, read top to bottom and right to left; the pitch names
written in the squares are taken from the Chinese twelve-pitch system (yullyeo), and subdividing
a square divides its time. The pitch names are yulmyeong, such as hwang for hwangjong. Sejong's
annals record pieces in it. A page can hold a whole ensemble score, part beside part, with the
words and ornament signs in their own columns. It is still used for court music (jeongak) and
classical music.

*In PHI*: the staff can also show sigimsae, Korean ornament signs, from the MIDI editor's menu.

## Arabic, Turkish and Persian staffs

Arab, Turkish and Persian art music are written on the Western staff with extra accidentals for
their intervals. Arab notation uses the half-flat and half-sharp, a quarter tone each, which
were agreed at the Cairo Congress of Arab Music in 1932. Turkish makam music is written in the
Arel–Ezgi–Uzdilek (AEU) system, which divides the whole tone into nine commas and gives
accidentals for 1, 4, 5 and 8 commas, sharp and flat: six signs beyond the Western four. Of the
53 commas in the octave, 24 are used. AEU borrows the shapes of the Arab half-flat and half-sharp
but gives them comma values, not quarter tones. So Rast looks the same on paper in both
traditions, though its Turkish third is a just major third and the Arab one is neutral. Persian music uses the koron (a lowered note, about a
quarter tone flat) and the sori (raised), both introduced by Ali-Naqi Vaziri in the 1920s.
Each staff is a convention: players bend the written pitch toward the maqām's intonation.

- [Arabic maqam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_maqam),
  [Turkish makam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_makam) and
  [Ali-Naqi Vaziri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali-Naqi_Vaziri), Wikipedia.
- Karl Signell, *Makam: Modal Practice in Turkish Art Music* (Asian Music Publications, 1977).

## Degree numbers

Numbering the scale degrees, 1 for the tonic up to the last degree, makes a notation independent
of key and tuning, closely related to jianpu and to the movable-do solfège of Galin, Paris and
Chevé and of John Curwen's tonic sol-fa. It suits modes with more or fewer than seven notes, and
a tuning in which the same number can be any size of step.

- [Scale degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_(music)) and
  [Tonic sol-fa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_sol-fa), Wikipedia.
