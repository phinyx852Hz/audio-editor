# Modes / The Americas

## Southern Appalachia

The ballads and fiddle tunes of the Southern Appalachian mountains keep the modes of their
British and Irish sources (Mixolydian, Dorian, Aeolian and gapped pentatonic scales) with
drones on banjo and dulcimer, and the shape-note hymnody of *The Sacred Harp* (1844).

Scots-Irish, English and German settlers brought the songs in the eighteenth century, and
African American musicians shaped the banjo and fiddle styles. Cecil Sharp and Maud Karpeles
collected over 1,600 tunes there (1916–18); the Lomaxes recorded it for the Library of Congress,
and it fed old-time, bluegrass and country music.

- [Appalachian music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_music), Wikipedia.
- Cecil J. Sharp, *English Folk Songs from the Southern Appalachians*, ed. Maud Karpeles (1932).

## Jazz (United States)

Jazz harmony uses the seven modes of the major scale and those of the melodic and harmonic minor
(Lydian dominant, altered, half-diminished), with blue notes between major and minor that come
from African American song, and chords extended to ninths, elevenths and thirteenths.

The blues and ragtime of the late nineteenth century and New Orleans jazz of the 1910s grew into
swing and bebop. George Russell's *Lydian Chromatic Concept* (1953) and Miles Davis's *Kind of
Blue* (1959) began modal jazz, which John Coltrane carried further.

- [Jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) and [Modal jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_jazz),
  Wikipedia.
- Paul F. Berliner, *Thinking in Jazz* (University of Chicago Press, 1994).
- Mark Levine, *The Jazz Theory Book* (Sher Music, 1995).

## Charles Ives

Ives (1874–1954), an insurance man from Connecticut, wrote music with several keys at once,
clusters, and quarter tones, learned from his father George, a bandmaster who experimented with
tunings. His *Three Quarter-Tone Pieces* for two pianos (1923–24) are a landmark.

His essay "Some 'Quarter-Tone' Impressions" (1925) and the *Concord Sonata* (1915) were little
known until the 1930s and 40s; he won the Pulitzer Prize in 1947.

- [Charles Ives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Ives), Wikipedia.

## Julián Carrillo

A Mexican composer (1875–1965) who in 1895 divided a violin string into sixteenths of a tone
and called it Sonido 13, the thirteenth sound beyond the twelve. He wrote in quarter, eighth and
sixteenth tones, and had fifteen "metamorphoser" pianos built (1950s), each tuned to a different
division.

His *Preludio a Colón* (1922) was the first published microtonal piece of its kind. The
metamorphoser pianos won a medal at the Brussels World's Fair (1958).

- [Julián Carrillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juli%C3%A1n_Carrillo), Wikipedia.

## Joseph Yasser

A Polish-born organist and theorist in New York (1893–1981) whose *A Theory of Evolving
Tonality* (1932) argued that scales grow from five notes to seven to twelve, and that the next
step would be nineteen, with twelve "diatonic" notes and seven "chromatic" ones.

His nineteen-tone theory influenced later microtonal composers. He was also a historian of
Russian music and a founding member of the American Musicological Society.

- [Joseph Yasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Yasser) and
  [19 equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19_equal_temperament), Wikipedia.

## Henry Cowell

Cowell (1897–1965), a Californian, wrote *New Musical Resources* (1930), tying rhythm to the
ratios of the overtones, and with Léon Theremin built the Rhythmicon (1931), the first drum
machine. He played tone clusters on the piano and studied Persian, Indian, Japanese and Irish
music.

He taught world music at the New School in New York and taught John Cage and Lou Harrison. His
*Persian Set* (1957) and the *Ongaku* works come from his travels.

- [Henry Cowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Cowell), Wikipedia.
- Henry Cowell, *New Musical Resources* (1930; ed. David Nicholls, Cambridge University Press, 1996).

## Roy Harris

An American symphonist (1898–1979) who ranked the church modes from bright to dark, from Lydian
to Phrygian, and used them for their colour, as in his Third Symphony (1939), the most played
American symphony of its time.

He studied with Nadia Boulanger in Paris and taught at Juilliard and elsewhere; William Schuman
was his pupil.

- [Roy Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Harris), Wikipedia.

## Harry Partch

Partch (1901–1974) rejected equal temperament for just intonation, built on the ratios of the
first eleven harmonics: a 43-note scale to the octave, the tonality diamond, and instruments he
built himself to play it (the Chromelodeon, the Diamond Marimba, the Cloud-Chamber Bowls).

He set out his theory in *Genesis of a Music* (1949) and wrote music-theatre such as *Delusion of
the Fury* (1966). Hobo, sign painter and outsider, he is the patron of American microtonality.

- [Harry Partch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Partch), Wikipedia.
- Harry Partch, *Genesis of a Music* (1949; 2nd ed. Da Capo, 1974).

## John R. Pierce

An engineer at Bell Labs (1910–2002) who named the transistor and led the first
communications satellites (Echo, Telstar), and with Max Mathews made computer music possible. In 1984 he
described the Bohlen–Pierce scale independently of Bohlen.

He wrote *The Science of Musical Sound* (1983) and later worked at Stanford's CCRMA.

- [John R. Pierce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Pierce) and
  [Bohlen–Pierce scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohlen%E2%80%93Pierce_scale), Wikipedia.

## Lou Harrison

Harrison (1917–2003), a Californian composer, wrote in just intonation and built American
gamelans with his partner Bill Colvig, tuned in pure ratios, joining Javanese and Western music
in works such as the *Concerto for Violin and Javanese Gamelan* (1959, revised later).

A pupil of Cowell and Schoenberg, he championed Ives and wrote *Lou Harrison's Music Primer*
(1971). He gave many works Esperanto titles and wrote for percussion of found objects.

- [Lou Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lou_Harrison), Wikipedia.

## Ivor Darreg

An American composer and instrument builder (1917–1994) who explored every equal temperament from
5 to 53 steps, coined the word "xenharmonic" for music in tunings other than twelve, and built electric guitars and keyboards for them.

His "megalyra" family of instruments and his writings, many in Xenharmonikôn, the journal of the
movement, inspired a generation of microtonal musicians.

- [Ivor Darreg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivor_Darreg), Wikipedia.

## George Russell

A jazz composer and theorist (1923–2009) whose *Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization*
(1953) made the Lydian, not the Ionian, the natural scale of a chord, and gave jazz a theory of
modes. Miles Davis and Bill Evans drew on it for *Kind of Blue* (1959).

He wrote "Cubana Be, Cubana Bop" for Dizzy Gillespie (1947), led his own bands and taught at the
New England Conservatory.

- [George Russell (composer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Russell_(composer)), Wikipedia.

## Ben Johnston

Johnston (1926–2019), a student of Partch, wrote extended just intonation with a notation of his
own, accidentals for each prime ratio, in ten string quartets; the Kepler Quartet recorded them
all. His Fourth Quartet ("Amazing Grace", 1973) is the best known.

He taught at the University of Illinois for thirty years.

- [Ben Johnston (composer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Johnston_(composer)), Wikipedia.

## Joe Maneri and Ezra Sims

Maneri (1927–2009), a jazz saxophonist and composer, and Sims (1928–2015), a Boston composer,
both worked in 72 equal steps to the octave (twelfth tones), which comes close to just intervals
up to the eleventh harmonic. Maneri taught it at the New England Conservatory.

Sims also devised a notation for it.

- [Joe Maneri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Maneri) and
  [Ezra Sims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Sims), Wikipedia.
- [72 equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/72_equal_temperament), Wikipedia.

## Erv Wilson

A Mexican-born American theorist (1928–2016) who mapped tunings as lattices and found new
structures in them: combination product sets (hexany, eikosany), moments of symmetry and the
Wilson keyboard, which plays any temperament in the same fingering.

He published little in his lifetime; his papers are online in the Wilson Archives.

- [Erv Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erv_Wilson), Wikipedia.
- [The Wilson Archives](https://www.anaphoria.com/wilson.html).

## Easley Blackwood

An American composer and theorist (1933–2023) at the University of Chicago who wrote a set of
*Twelve Microtonal Etudes* for synthesizer (1980), one for each equal temperament from 13 to 24,
and *The Structure of Recognizable Diatonic Tunings* (1985).

He was also a concert pianist and wrote symphonies in a traditional idiom.

- [Easley Blackwood Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easley_Blackwood_Jr.), Wikipedia.

## James Tenney

Tenney (1934–2006), composer, pianist and theorist, worked at Bell Labs on early computer music
and wrote on harmonic space, a geometry of pitch by prime ratios. His *Critical Band* (1988) and
*Spectral CANON for CONLON Nancarrow* (1974) explore the harmonic series.

He taught at CalArts and York University; *A History of "Consonance" and "Dissonance"* (1988) is
his historical study.

- [James Tenney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Tenney), Wikipedia.

## La Monte Young

Young (b. 1935) is the father of drone music and an elder of minimalism: *The Well-Tuned Piano*
(from 1964), in a just intonation of his own on the seventh harmonic, lasts over six hours, and
the *Dream House* (with Marian Zazeela, in New York since 1993) holds sustained just intervals
in light for years.

He studied Hindustani singing with Pandit Pran Nath from 1970, with Terry Riley.

- [La Monte Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Monte_Young), Wikipedia.
- [MELA Foundation](https://www.melafoundation.org), the Dream House.

## Terry Riley

Riley (b. 1935) wrote *In C* (1964), 53 short figures played by any group, each player moving on
in their own time, the work that began minimalism. He later tuned keyboards in just intonation
(*The Harp of New Albion*, 1984) and studied Hindustani singing with Pran Nath.

He wrote much for the Kronos Quartet from 1980.

- [Terry Riley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Riley), Wikipedia.

## Wendy Carlos

Carlos (b. 1939), whose *Switched-On Bach* (1968) made the Moog synthesizer famous, explored new
tunings in *Beauty in the Beast* (1986): the alpha, beta and gamma scales, which do not repeat at
the octave but divide the pure fifth and third into equal steps, and many just and world tunings.

She also scored *A Clockwork Orange* and *Tron*.

- [Wendy Carlos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Carlos) and
  [Alpha scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_scale), Wikipedia.

## Glenn Branca

Branca (1948–2018) wrote symphonies for massed electric guitars, from the 1980s, tuned in unisons
and harmonics so that the overtones of the mass are heard; his later works used guitars tuned to
the harmonic series.

He came from the New York no wave scene; Sonic Youth's Thurston Moore and Lee Ranaldo played in
his ensembles.

- [Glenn Branca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Branca), Wikipedia.

# Modes / Oceania

## Percy Grainger

A composer and pianist born in Melbourne (1882–1961) who collected English folk song on wax
cylinders (from 1906), noting its free rhythms and inflections exactly, and dreamed of "free
music", gliding tones without fixed steps, which he built machines to play with Burnett Cross
(1940s–50s).

His *Lincolnshire Posy* (1937) sets folk song for wind band. He settled in the United States and
founded a museum of himself in Melbourne.

- [Percy Grainger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Grainger), Wikipedia.
- [Grainger Museum](https://grainger.unimelb.edu.au), University of Melbourne.
