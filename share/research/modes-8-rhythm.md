# Modes / Rhythm

## Euclidean rhythms

Spreading a number of hits as evenly as possible over a number of steps (3 in 8, 5 in 8, 7 in
12...) gives many of the world's traditional timelines: the tresillo, the cinquillo, the bembé
bell.

Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common divisor (*Elements*, c. 300 BC) does it. Eric
Bjorklund found the same pattern for timing the Spallation Neutron Source (2003), and Godfried
Toussaint showed in 2005 that it gives the timelines of many musical traditions.

- [Euclidean rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_rhythm), Wikipedia.
- Godfried T. Toussaint, "The Euclidean Algorithm Generates Traditional Musical Rhythms"
  (Bridges, 2005), and *The Geometry of Musical Rhythm* (CRC Press, 2nd ed. 2019).

# Modes / Rhythm / Europe

## Medieval rhythmic modes (ars antiqua)

Six patterns of long and short notes, by analogy with the metrical feet of poetry, that ruled the
rhythm of Parisian polyphony around 1200: long-breve (trochee), breve-long (iamb), long-breve-breve
(dactyl), breve-breve-long (anapest), all longs (spondee) and all breves (tribrach).

They are read from the ligatures (note groups) of the Notre-Dame organa of Léonin and Pérotin.
Johannes de Garlandia described them (*De mensurabili musica*, c. 1240); Franco of Cologne's
mensural notation (c. 1280) gave each note its own length and made the modes unnecessary.

- [Rhythmic mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythmic_mode), Wikipedia.
- William G. Waite, *The Rhythm of Twelfth-Century Polyphony* (Yale University Press, 1954).
- Richard H. Hoppin, *Medieval Music* (Norton, 1978).

# Modes / Rhythm / West Asia

## Iqāʿāt

An iqāʿ (plural iqāʿāt) is a rhythmic mode of Arabic music: a cycle of low strokes (dum), high
strokes (tak) and rests, from the two-beat waḥda to cycles of 10 (samāʿī thaqīl), 16, 24 and more,
played on the darbuka, riqq and daff. Each has its character and its repertoire: maqsūm and
baladī for dance, samāʿī for instrumental forms, the long cycles for the muwashshaḥ.

Al-Kindī and al-Fārābī described the rhythmic cycles of the Abbasid court; Ṣafī al-Dīn wrote them
in a notation of strokes. The Turkish usûl and Persian and Andalusian cycles are related systems.

- [Maqam World, iqāʿāt](https://ftp.maqamworld.com/en/iqaa.php), every iqāʿ with its notation.
- A. J. Racy, *Making Music in the Arab World* (Cambridge University Press, 2003).
- [Arabic rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_rhythm), Wikipedia.

# Modes / Rhythm / South Asia

## North Indian tāl

A tāl is a rhythmic cycle of Hindustani music: a number of mātrās (beats) grouped into vibhāgs,
marked by claps (tālī) and waves (khālī), with sam, the first beat, where the cycle lands. Each
tāl has its ṭhekā, the basic pattern of tabla strokes (bols): tīntāl (16), jhaptāl (10), rūpak
(7), ektāl (12), dādrā (6), kehervā (8).

The old mārga tālas of the *Nāṭyaśāstra* gave way to the deśī tālas of the medieval treatises;
the tabla, which took over from the pakhāvaj in the eighteenth century, made the modern tāls.
Bhatkhande's notation (x for sam, 0 for khālī, numbers for the tālīs) is still used.

- [Tala (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tala_(music)), Wikipedia.
- Martin Clayton, *Time in Indian Music* (Oxford University Press, 2000).
- David R. Courtney, *Fundamentals of Tabla* (Sur Sangeet Services, 1995), and his site
  [chandrakantha.com](https://chandrakantha.com).

## South Indian tāḷam

The Karnatak tāḷa system: seven basic tāḷams (sūḷādi sapta tāḷas: dhruva, maṭhya, rūpaka,
jhampa, tripuṭa, aṭa, ēka), each made of aṅgas (laghu, drutam, anudrutam), and five jātis that
set the length of the laghu (3, 4, 5, 7 or 9), giving 35 tāḷams; ādi tāḷam (8 beats) is the most
used. The beats are counted with claps, waves and finger counts (kriyās), and rhythm is spoken in
solkaṭṭu syllables.

Purandara Dāsa (16th century) is credited with the sūḷādi system. Gati, the subdivision of the
beat, and the arithmetic of korvais and mōrās give the mṛdaṅgam its elaborate cadences.

- [Carnatic tala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tala_(music)#Carnatic_tala), Wikipedia.
- P. Sambamoorthy, *South Indian Music*, books I–III.
- David P. Nelson, *Solkattu Manual* (Wesleyan University Press, 2008).

# Modes / Rhythm / East Asia

## Korean jangdan

Jangdan ("long and short") are the rhythmic cycles of Korean music, played on the hourglass drum
janggu with its two heads: the deep gung and the sharp chae. They run from the slow jinyang (24
beats of three) of sanjo and pansori to the quick hwimori, with semachi, gutgeori, jungmori,
jajinmori and others.

They come from shaman ritual, farmers' bands (pungmul) and court music; the modern samul nori
quartet (1978) brought them to the concert stage and the world.

- [Traditional Korean rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Korean_rhythm), Wikipedia.
- Keith Howard, *Korean Musical Instruments* (Oxford University Press, 1995).

# Modes / Rhythm / Africa

## West African timelines

A timeline is a short asymmetric pattern, usually on a bell, that every part of an ensemble hears
itself against: the seven-stroke standard pattern of 12/8 (agbekɔ, bembé), the fume-fume, the
gahu and shiko bells. Djembe rhythms such as kuku layer drum parts over them, and the same
patterns run under soukous and Afrobeat.

The Ewe, Yoruba, Akan and Mande traditions have used them for centuries; they crossed the Atlantic
with the slave trade and became the claves of Cuba and Brazil. A. M. Jones (*Studies in African
Music*, 1959), J. H. Kwabena Nketia and Kofi Agawu shaped the study of them.

- [Bell pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_pattern), Wikipedia.
- Kofi Agawu, *The African Imagination in Music* (Oxford University Press, 2016).
- David Locke, *Drum Gahu* (White Cliffs Media, 1987).
- [Djembe Rhythm – Kuku](https://afrodrumming.com/djembe-rhythm-kuku/), afrodrumming.com.

## North African and Gnawa rhythms

The rhythms of Egypt and the Maghreb share the Arabic iqāʿāt (maqsūm, baladī, ṣaʿīdī, malfūf)
and add their own: the qarāqib (iron castanets) of the Gnawa, in a swung triple feel, and the
rhythms of Moroccan, Algerian and Tunisian popular music.

The Gnawa, descendants of West Africans brought to Morocco, hold all-night līla ceremonies of
music and trance with the guembri lute; Gnawa music was inscribed by UNESCO in 2019.

- [Gnawa music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnawa_music), Wikipedia.
- Deborah Kapchan, *Traveling Spirit Masters* (Wesleyan University Press, 2007).

# Modes / Rhythm / The Americas

## Caribbean rhythms

The clave patterns of Cuba (son and rumba clave, 3-2 and 2-3), the cáscara, the tresillo,
cinquillo and habanera, the bembé of 6/8, and the rhythms they gave rise to: son montuno, cha-cha-
chá, the one drop, rockers and steppers of reggae, ska, the dembow of reggaetón, soca and merengue.

They come from West and Central Africa through the Afro-Cuban religions and the music of the
islands. The habanera reached Europe and New Orleans in the nineteenth century, the "Spanish tinge"
of early jazz; Cuban son and mambo shaped salsa in New York in the 1960s and 70s.

- [Clave (rhythm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clave_(rhythm)) and
  [Tresillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tresillo_(rhythm)), Wikipedia.
- Rebeca Mauleón, *Salsa Guidebook* (Sher Music, 1993).
- Michael E. Veal, *Dub* (Wesleyan University Press, 2007).

## South American rhythms

Samba batucada and bossa nova from Brazil, the baião of the northeast, the huayno of the Andes,
the tango and milonga of the Río de la Plata, the candombe drums of Montevideo and the cumbia of
Colombia.

Each joins African, Indigenous and European music. Samba grew in Rio de Janeiro in the 1910s; bossa
nova (João Gilberto, Tom Jobim, 1958) slowed it into song. Candombe came with enslaved Africans to
Uruguay; tango grew in Buenos Aires in the 1880s from the habanera and the milonga.

- [Samba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samba), [Tango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tango) and
  [Candombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candombe), Wikipedia.
- Ed Uribe, *The Essence of Brazilian Percussion and Drum Set* (Warner Bros., 1993).

## Breakbeats

A break is the part of a funk or soul record where everything but the drums drops out. DJs looped
them, and producers sampled them: the Amen break, Funky Drummer, Think, Apache, Impeach the
President, When the Levee Breaks and others are the backbone of hip hop, jungle and drum and bass.

DJ Kool Herc began looping breaks on two turntables in the Bronx in 1973; samplers such as the
E-mu SP-1200 and the Akai MPC (1980s) made them the stuff of production. The Winstons' drummer
Gregory C. Coleman, who played the Amen break (1969), was never paid for its use.

- [Break (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Break_(music)) and
  [Amen break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break), Wikipedia.
- Joseph G. Schloss, *Making Beats: The Art of Sample-Based Hip-Hop* (Wesleyan University Press,
  2004).

## Trap

Hip hop from Atlanta: half-time drums at a fast tempo (around 140 BPM, felt at 70), rolling hi-hats
in triplets and thirty-seconds, booming Roland TR-808 kicks and long tuned bass. Drill, from
Chicago and then London, shifts the snares and slides the bass.

The style grew in the early 2000s with T.I., Gucci Mane and Young Jeezy and producers such as
DJ Toomp and Shawty Redd; Lex Luger, Metro Boomin and others spread it in the 2010s.

- [Trap music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trap_music) and
  [Drill music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drill_music), Wikipedia.
