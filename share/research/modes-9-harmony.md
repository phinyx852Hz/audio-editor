# Modes / Harmony

## Harmony beyond the West

Harmony here is any way of sounding more than one pitch at a time on purpose: chords that move by rule, but also drones, parallel and diaphonic singing, heterophony, interlocking and hocket, overtone singing and stratified polyphony.

Western theory long treated harmony as a stack of consonances that move by rule. Studies of
multipart music elsewhere describe other priorities:

- **Seconds can be consonant.** In Shope diaphony and Lithuanian sutartinės, the beating major
  second is the sound that is sought (Messner; Račiūnaitė-Vyčinienė).
- **The texture can be many versions of one line.** Heterophony, as in the Arab takht, Jiangnan
  sizhu, Korean sinawi and Russian podgoloski, makes harmony from the places where those versions
  part.
- **Harmony can come from the sum of parts no one plays alone.** Examples are hocket and
  interlocking: Banda-Linda horns, Balinese kotekan, Andean sikuri and Shona mbira (Arom; Tenzer;
  Turino; Berliner).
- **Harmony can come from one sound's partials.** Examples are xöömei, the Xhosa bows' two roots
  and Bunun pasibutbut (Levin; Dargie).
- **Harmony can come in layers of density over a core.** The gamelan's stratified polyphony is
  the example (Sumarsam; Hood).
- **Harmony can be mapped by zones.** Nketia sorted Africa's multipart singing into areas of
  thirds, of fourths and fifths, and of octaves.

Jordania (*Who Asked the First Question?*, 2006) surveys vocal polyphony worldwide. Ahmedaja and
Haid's *European Voices* (2008–) covers Europe's multipart singing.

- [Polyphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphony) and [Heterophony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterophony), Wikipedia.
- Joseph Jordania, *Who Asked the First Question? The Origins of Human Choral Singing, Intelligence, Language and Speech* (Logos, 2006).
- Ardian Ahmedaja and Gerlinde Haid (eds.), *European Voices* (Böhlau, 2008–).

# Modes / Harmony / Western art music / Middle Ages

## parallel organum

The first written polyphony: a chant (vox principalis) doubled a fourth or fifth below by the vox organalis, the doubling bent at the edges of the phrase to avoid the tritone, and both voices may be doubled at the octave.

**From:** Musica enchiriadis, c. 900. **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** perfect fourths, fifths and octaves in parallel; unison at the start and end.

- [Search Wikipedia for “parallel organum”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=parallel%20organum)
- Musica enchiriadis and Scolica enchiriadis (c. 900).
- Grout & Palisca.

## organum purum

Léonin's two-voice organum for the Magnus liber organi: the chant's notes stretched into a long held tenor, over which the duplum spins melismas, meeting it on fifths, octaves and unisons and passing through dissonance between.

**From:** Notre-Dame school, 1160s–1250. **Texture:** drone.

**Sonorities:** perfect consonances on the tenor's notes; seconds, thirds and sixths passing.

- [Search Wikipedia for “organum purum”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=organum%20purum)
- Magnus liber organi.
- Anonymous IV (c. 1275).
- Roesner, Le Magnus liber organi de Notre-Dame.

## english discant and gymel

The English taste for imperfect consonances: two voices moving in thirds (gymel, 'twin song') or three in chains of sixth-chords, heard on the Continent as the contenance angloise of Dunstable.

**From:** England, 13th–15th c. **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** parallel thirds and sixths, cadencing to the fifth and octave.

- [Search Wikipedia for “english discant and gymel”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=english%20discant%20and%20gymel)
- Sumer is icumen in (13th c.).
- Bukofzer, Studies in Medieval and Renaissance Music.
- Martin le Franc, Le champion des dames.

# Modes / Harmony / Western art music / Renaissance

## fauxbourdon

A chant in the top voice, a tenor a sixth below it, and an unwritten middle voice sung a fourth below the top: chains of 6/3 chords that open out to an octave and fifth at the cadence.

**From:** Guillaume Du Fay (1397–1474), c. 1430. **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** 6/3 chords in parallel; the double leading-tone cadence to 8/5.

- [Search Wikipedia for “fauxbourdon”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=fauxbourdon)
- Du Fay, Missa Sancti Jacobi (c. 1427).
- Trowell, 'Faburden and fauxbourdon'.

## modal counterpoint

Independent voices in a church mode, every dissonance prepared and resolved: the passing tone on the weak beat, the suspension on the strong one, the cadence on the octave or a full triad. Codified by Zarlino and, later, Fux's species.

**From:** Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina (1525–1594), 16th c. **Texture:** counterpoint.

**Sonorities:** triads and their inversions; the 4–3 and 7–6 suspension; no parallel fifths or octaves.

- [Search Wikipedia for “modal counterpoint”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=modal%20counterpoint)
- Zarlino, Le istitutioni harmoniche (1558).
- Fux, Gradus ad Parnassum (1725).
- Jeppesen, Counterpoint.

# Modes / Harmony / Western art music / Baroque

## basso continuo and figured bass

A bass line with figures, realized at sight by keyboard or lute as chords above it: harmony thought of from the bass up, the seed of functional harmony and of the chord as a thing in itself.

**From:** Italy, c. 1600–1750. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** 5/3 and 6/3 chords, 6/4 and 7 by figure; the cadential 6/4–5/3.

- [Search Wikipedia for “basso continuo and figured bass”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=basso%20continuo%20and%20figured%20bass)
- Viadana, Cento concerti ecclesiastici (1602).
- Heinichen, Der General-Bass (1728).
- Arnold, The Art of Accompaniment from a Thorough-Bass.

# Modes / Harmony / Western art music / Common practice

## functional harmony

Major and minor keys in which every chord has a function, tonic, subdominant or dominant, and the fundamental bass moves by fifths toward the tonic: the harmony of Bach's chorales, Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven.

**From:** Jean-Philippe Rameau (1683–1764), 1722. **Texture:** functional.

**Sonorities:** triads and the dominant seventh; I–IV–V–I; the perfect and the deceptive cadence.

- [Search Wikipedia for “functional harmony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=functional%20harmony)
- Rameau, Traité de l'harmonie (1722).
- Riemann, Vereinfachte Harmonielehre (1893).
- Aldwell & Schachter, Harmony and Voice Leading.

## neo-riemannian transformations

Triads joined not by function but by smooth voice leading: P (parallel), L (leading-tone exchange) and R (relative) each move one note, so chains like the hexatonic cycle drift through distant keys. Named after Hugo Riemann by Cohn and Lewin.

**From:** Franz Liszt (1811–1886), 19th c. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** major and minor triads a third apart; the hexatonic and octatonic cycles.

- [Search Wikipedia for “neo-riemannian transformations”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=neo-riemannian%20transformations)
- Cohn, 'Introduction to Neo-Riemannian Theory' (1998).
- Lewin, Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations (1987).

## romantic chromaticism

Keys stretched by chromatic voice leading until the tonic is implied more than stated: augmented sixths, half-diminished sevenths that do not resolve, and the Tristan chord.

**From:** Richard Wagner (1813–1883), 1859. **Texture:** functional.

**Sonorities:** the Tristan chord (F–B–D♯–G♯), the augmented sixth, the diminished seventh, chromatic mediants.

- [Search Wikipedia for “romantic chromaticism”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=romantic%20chromaticism)
- Wagner, Tristan und Isolde (1859).
- Kurth, Romantische Harmonik (1920).
- Bailey (ed.), Prelude and Transfiguration.

# Modes / Harmony / Western art music / Twentieth century

## impressionist planing

Chords moved in parallel as a single colour, the dominant ninth that does not resolve, whole-tone and pentatonic fields: harmony as timbre rather than progression.

**From:** Claude Debussy (1862–1918), c. 1890–1915. **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** parallel ninth chords; whole-tone and pentatonic sonorities.

- [Search Wikipedia for “impressionist planing”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=impressionist%20planing)
- Debussy, La cathédrale engloutie, Nuages.
- Lockspeiser, Debussy.
- DeVoto, Debussy and the Veil of Tonality.

## the mystic chord

A six-note chord of fourths (augmented, diminished and perfect) that stands as a centre in its own right, from which the melodies of Prometheus are drawn.

**From:** Alexander Scriabin (1872–1915), 1910. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** C–F♯–B♭–E–A–D, a subset of the acoustic scale.

- [Search Wikipedia for “the mystic chord”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=the%20mystic%20chord)
- Scriabin, Prometheus: The Poem of Fire, Op. 60 (1910).
- Perle, 'Scriabin's Self-Analyses'.

## free atonality and twelve-tone harmony

Harmony emancipated from tonal resolution: chords chosen by interval content (pitch-class sets), and in the twelve-tone method drawn from the segments of a row.

**From:** Arnold Schoenberg (1874–1951), 1908–1923. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** set classes such as 3-5 and 4-19; the all-interval tetrachords; row segments as chords.

- [Search Wikipedia for “free atonality and twelve-tone harmony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=free%20atonality%20and%20twelve-tone%20harmony)
- Schoenberg, Harmonielehre (1911).
- Forte, The Structure of Atonal Music (1973).

## polytonality

Two keys at once: the Petrushka chord sets C major against F♯ major, a tritone apart; Milhaud and Ives took it further. The octatonic scale often holds both.

**From:** Igor Stravinsky (1882–1971), 1911. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** two triads a tritone apart (C and F♯); octatonic collections.

- [Search Wikipedia for “polytonality”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=polytonality)
- Stravinsky, Petrushka (1911).
- Milhaud, 'Polytonalité et atonalité' (1923).
- van den Toorn, The Music of Igor Stravinsky.

## modal harmony

Chords built from a mode rather than a major or minor key, so the mode's own colour stays: the ear is held on its root by staying on the tonic chord or a pedal, the V–I cadence that would turn it back into major or minor is avoided, chords move by step rather than by fifth, and the chords chosen carry the mode's characteristic note (dorian's raised sixth, mixolydian's flat seventh, lydian's raised fourth, phrygian's flat second) without a tritone that would make them sound dominant. Quartal voicings keep it open. From Debussy, Fauré and Vaughan Williams's modal revival to Kind of Blue, My Favorite Things, Impressions and Footprints, and the ♭VII–I of rock and the dorian vamps of film scores. (Mode ▸ jazz has its quartal side; Renaissance modal counterpoint is the older, separate sense.) The example plays each mode on the same final over a pedal.

**From:** the modal revival, modal jazz and after, c. 1890–. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** dorian i–IV, mixolydian I–♭VII, lydian I–II, phrygian i–♭II; pedals; stacked fourths.

- [Search Wikipedia for “modal harmony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=modal%20harmony)
- Pease, Jazz Composition: Theory and Practice (Berklee Press, 2003).
- Persichetti, Twentieth-Century Harmony (1961).
- Miller, Modal Jazz Composition and Harmony (1996).
- Berklee Online, 'Modal Harmony in Jazz Composition'.
- My New Microphone, Hub Guitar, Chromatone and Audiofanzine guides to modal harmony.
- Davis, So What (1959).

## quartal harmony

Chords stacked in fourths instead of thirds, with no root by default: in Schoenberg's Chamber Symphony, Bartók, Hindemith's theory of chord values, and from there jazz.

**From:** Paul Hindemith (1895–1963), 1937. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** stacked perfect fourths; the fourth chord and its inversions.

- [Search Wikipedia for “quartal harmony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=quartal%20harmony)
- Schoenberg, Kammersymphonie Op. 9 (1906).
- Hindemith, Unterweisung im Tonsatz (1937).
- Persichetti, Twentieth-Century Harmony (1961).

## pandiatonicism

Every note of a diatonic scale free to sound with every other, without functional progression: the open, added-note sound of Copland's Appalachian Spring and Stravinsky's neoclassicism. Slonimsky's term.

**From:** Aaron Copland (1900–1990), 1930s–1940s. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** triads with added seconds, fourths and sixths; diatonic clusters.

- [Search Wikipedia for “pandiatonicism”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=pandiatonicism)
- Slonimsky, Music since 1900 (1937).
- Copland, Appalachian Spring (1944).

## minimalist harmony

Slow harmonic rhythm over pulsing patterns: a chord, or a cycle of a few, held while the texture changes inside it, as in Reich's Music for 18 Musicians and Glass's arpeggiated progressions.

**From:** Steve Reich (b. 1936), 1960s–. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** diatonic chords with added notes, changing slowly; static drones.

- [Search Wikipedia for “minimalist harmony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=minimalist%20harmony)
- Reich, Music for 18 Musicians (1976).
- Glass, Einstein on the Beach (1976).
- Potter, Four Musical Minimalists.

## spectral harmony

Chords made from the partials of a sound, its harmonic spectrum or an analysed one, heard as a single timbre; the music of L'Itinéraire, Grisey's Partiels and Murail's Gondwana.

**From:** Gérard Grisey (1946–1998), 1975. **Texture:** overtone.

**Sonorities:** the harmonic series, its seventh and eleventh partials rounded here to the nearest key.

- [Search Wikipedia for “spectral harmony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=spectral%20harmony)
- Grisey, Partiels (1975).
- Murail, 'The Revolution of Complex Sounds' (2005).
- Fineberg, 'Guide to the Basic Concepts of Spectral Music'.

# Modes / Harmony / Jazz

## twelve-bar blues

Twelve bars on I, IV and V, each a dominant seventh, the blue third and seventh sung against them: the dominant seventh as a tonic, the root of jazz harmony.

**From:** the blues, c. 1900–. **Texture:** functional.

**Sonorities:** I7, IV7, V7; the blue notes ♭3, ♭5, ♭7 against major chords.

- [Search Wikipedia for “twelve-bar blues”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=twelve-bar%20blues)
- Handy, St. Louis Blues (1914).
- Titon, Early Downhome Blues.
- Gioia, The History of Jazz.

## extended tertian chords

Sevenths as the norm and ninths, elevenths and thirteenths added above: the sixth and ninth chords of swing sections, voiced close in the saxophones and brass.

**From:** swing, 1930s. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** 6, maj7, 9, 11, 13 chords; the four-way close voicing.

- [Search Wikipedia for “extended tertian chords”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=extended%20tertian%20chords)
- Levine, The Jazz Theory Book (1995).
- Ellington, Mood Indigo (1930).

## ii–v–i and tritone substitution



**From:** bebop, 1940s; functional). The ii–V–I as the unit of a tune, and the dominant replaced by the one a tritone away, which shares its third and seventh, giving the chromatic bass of bebop: Dm7–D♭7–Cmaj7. *Sonorities:* m7–7–maj7; the tritone substitute ♭II7. **Texture:** altered dominants (♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13.

- [Search Wikipedia for “ii–v–i and tritone substitution”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=ii%E2%80%93v%E2%80%93i%20and%20tritone%20substitution)
- Levine, The Jazz Theory Book.
- Parker & Gillespie, Groovin' High (1945).
- Owens, Bird: The Life and Music of Charlie Parker.

## reharmonization and block chords

A melody harmonized note by note, the melody doubled an octave below with a chord in between ('locked hands'), passing notes taken as diminished sevenths.

**From:** George Shearing (1919–2011), 1949. **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** drop-2 and locked-hands voicings; the diminished-seventh passing chord.

- [Search Wikipedia for “reharmonization and block chords”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=reharmonization%20and%20block%20chords)
- Shearing, September in the Rain (1949).
- Levine, The Jazz Piano Book.

## rootless voicings

The left hand leaves the root to the bass and plays third, seventh and colour tones, in two shapes (A and B) that move by step through a ii–V–I.

**From:** Bill Evans (1929–1980), 1958–. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** 3–5–7–9 and 7–9–3–5; G13 as F–A–B–E.

- [Search Wikipedia for “rootless voicings”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=rootless%20voicings)
- Levine, The Jazz Piano Book (1989).
- Evans, Portrait in Jazz (1959).

## modal and quartal jazz

A mode held for many bars instead of changing chords, voiced in fourths: the 'So What' chord (three fourths and a major third) and McCoy Tyner's fourths.

**From:** Miles Davis and Bill Evans, 1959. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** the So What chord; stacked fourths moved in parallel within the dorian mode.

- [Search Wikipedia for “modal and quartal jazz”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=modal%20and%20quartal%20jazz)
- Davis, Kind of Blue (1959).
- Russell, The Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization (1953).

## coltrane changes

Three keys a major third apart, each reached by its own dominant, dividing the octave in three: Giant Steps' B–D7–G–B♭7–E♭.

**From:** John Coltrane (1926–1967), 1959. **Texture:** functional.

**Sonorities:** maj7 chords a major third apart, each with its V7.

- [Search Wikipedia for “coltrane changes”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=coltrane%20changes)
- Coltrane, Giant Steps (1960).
- Demsey, John Coltrane Plays Giant Steps.

## upper-structure triads

A triad played above a dominant's tritone so that its notes are the tensions: D major over C7 gives 9, ♯11 and 13; A♭ over C7 gives ♭13, root and ♯9.

**From:** post-bop, 1960s–. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** a major triad over the 3rd and 7th of a dominant; the altered scale.

- [Search Wikipedia for “upper-structure triads”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=upper-structure%20triads)
- Levine, The Jazz Piano Book.
- Mehegan, Jazz Improvisation (1959).

# Modes / Harmony / The world / Europe

## georgian polyphony

Three-voice singing, in the east over a held bass drone and in the west (Guria, Svaneti) in three independent parts, with sharp seconds, fourths and sevenths resolving to the fifth-and-octave, in a tuning of its own. UNESCO 2001.

**From:** Georgia, first written 11th c. **Texture:** polyphony.

**Sonorities:** fifth plus octave (or fourth) at cadences; seconds, fourths and sevenths in passing; a non-tempered scale.

- [Search Wikipedia for “georgian polyphony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=georgian%20polyphony)
- Jordania, Who Asked the First Question? (2006).
- Erkomaishvili archive.
- Scherbaum et al. on Georgian tuning.

## byzantine ison

The chant sung over the ison, a held note on the base of the echos, moving with the melody's centre.

**From:** Byzantine and Greek Orthodox chant, 15th c.–. **Texture:** drone.

**Sonorities:** the melody against its drone: unisons, fourths and fifths at rest, every interval of the echos in passing.

- [Search Wikipedia for “byzantine ison”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=byzantine%20ison)
- Levy & Troelsgård, 'Byzantine chant' (Grove).
- Conomos.

## albanian iso-polyphony

Two or three soloists, the 'taker' and the 'turner', in an anhemitonic pentatonic, over the iso, a drone sung by the group on a vowel. UNESCO 2005.

**From:** Albania (Tosk and Lab). **Texture:** drone.

**Sonorities:** seconds and fourths between soloists; the drone under both.

- [Search Wikipedia for “albanian iso-polyphony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=albanian%20iso-polyphony)
- Tole, Albanian Folk Iso-polyphony (2008).
- Ahmedaja, European Voices I.

## shope diaphony

Two-part singing in which one voice holds a drone and the other moves close above and below it, prizing the beating major second as the most beautiful sound.

**From:** Bulgaria (Shopluk). **Texture:** drone.

**Sonorities:** the major second against the drone; unisons at the ends.

- [Search Wikipedia for “shope diaphony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=shope%20diaphony)
- Messner, Die Schwebungsdiaphonie in Bistrica (1980).
- Rice, May It Fill Your Soul.

## paghjella

Three men's voices: the seconda gives the melody, the bassu supports it, the terza decorates high above with melismas; sung in a close huddle. UNESCO 2009.

**From:** Corsica. **Texture:** polyphony.

**Sonorities:** fifths and octaves at cadences, thirds and seconds between.

- [Search Wikipedia for “paghjella”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=paghjella)
- Bithell, Transported by Song: Corsican Voices (2007).

## sutartinės

Women's multipart songs in which voices meet in parallel and crossing seconds, the seconds heard as consonant; two-part, three-part canons, and four-part as two pairs. UNESCO 2010.

**From:** Lithuania. **Texture:** polyphony.

**Sonorities:** major seconds as the stable interval.

- [Search Wikipedia for “sutartinės”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=sutartin%C4%97s)
- Račiūnaitė-Vyčinienė, Sutartinės: Lithuanian Polyphonic Songs (2002).

## russian village polyphony

Protyazhnaya (drawn-out) songs where the voices branch from the tune as podgoloski, 'under-voices', each a variant of it, converging on the unison or octave at the ends of phrases.

**From:** Russia and Ukraine. **Texture:** heterophony.

**Sonorities:** unison and octave at cadences; thirds, fourths and fifths where the variants part.

- [Search Wikipedia for “russian village polyphony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=russian%20village%20polyphony)
- Lineva, Great Russian Songs in Folk Harmonization (1904).
- Mazo.
- Ivanytsky on Ukrainian polyphony.

## cantu a tenore

Four men: the boghe leads, the bassu and contra answer in guttural voices a fifth apart, the mesu boghe ornaments above; the chord is built on the major triad. UNESCO 2005.

**From:** Sardinia. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** root, fifth and octave from the bassu, contra and mesu boghe; the boghe's melody above.

- [Search Wikipedia for “cantu a tenore”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=cantu%20a%20tenore)
- Lortat-Jacob, Chants de Passion (1998).
- Macchiarella.

## seto leelo

A lead singer gives each line and the choir takes it up in two parts: the low torrõ, many voices, and the single high killõ above, meeting in seconds, fourths and fifths. UNESCO 2009.

**From:** Seto (Estonia). **Texture:** polyphony.

**Sonorities:** the killõ a second, fourth or fifth above the torrõ; unison endings.

- [Search Wikipedia for “seto leelo”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=seto%20leelo)
- Pärtlas, 'Seto Polyphonic Song' (2006).

## naturjodel



**From:** Alpine (Switzerland and Austria); chordal). Wordless yodels flipping between chest and head voice, sung in two to four parts in thirds and sixths over a bass on tonic and dominant, sometimes with the natural, sharpened fourth of the alphorn. *Sonorities:* thirds and sixths over I and V. **Texture:** the alphorn fa (an 11th partial.

- [Search Wikipedia for “naturjodel”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=naturjodel)
- Baumann, Das sogenannte Alphorn-Fa (1976).
- Wise, Yodel in Switzerland.

## klapa

Small groups of men (now women too) singing a cappella in close tertian harmony: first tenor leads, second tenor and baritone fill in, the bass holds the root, the chords lingering and swelling. UNESCO 2012.

**From:** Dalmatia (Croatia), 19th c.–. **Texture:** chordal.

**Sonorities:** close triads and dominant sevenths; the lead's melody over a sustained bass.

- [Search Wikipedia for “klapa”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=klapa)
- Ćaleta, 'Klapa singing' (1997).

# Modes / Harmony / The world / West Asia

## takht heterophony

The oud, qānūn, nāy and violin of a takht play one melody in a maqām each in their own way, at once, with the singer: harmony as many versions of one line.

**From:** Egypt and the Levant. **Texture:** heterophony.

**Sonorities:** the unison and octave, and the passing seconds where the versions differ.

- [Search Wikipedia for “takht heterophony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=takht%20heterophony)
- Racy, Making Music in the Arab World (2003).
- Marcus, Music in Egypt.

# Modes / Harmony / The world / Central Asia

## dombra küi

The two-stringed dombra tuned a fourth or fifth apart, the lower string a drone or moving in parallel with the upper: the harmony of the instrumental küi.

**From:** Kazakhs. **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** fourths and fifths between the strings.

- [Search Wikipedia for “dombra küi”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=dombra%20k%C3%BCi)
- Utegalieva.
- Levin, The Hundred Thousand Fools of God.

## urtyn duu with the morin khuur

The long song, free in time, and the horse-head fiddle that follows it a little behind or ahead, ornamenting the same line. UNESCO 2005.

**From:** Mongolia. **Texture:** heterophony.

**Sonorities:** unisons and octaves, seconds in passing.

- [Search Wikipedia for “urtyn duu with the morin khuur”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=urtyn%20duu%20with%20the%20morin%20khuur)

## xöömei

## gamelan layering

The free-reed mouth organ of the Lao, playing melody over its own held drones and in parallel octaves, fourths and fifths, in the lai modes. UNESCO 2017.

## shō clusters (aitake)

The mouth organ of gagaku holds a cluster of five or six pipes, an aitake named for its lowest note, and slides between them; the hichiriki and ryūteki play the melody heterophonically through it. (The cluster here is in the manner of an aitake, not a particular one.)

**Sonorities:** clusters of seconds, fourths and fifths.

## grand song

Kgal laox, sung without conductor or instruments: many voices hold and sway on a low part while one or two soloists sing the high part, imitating birds and cicadas. UNESCO 2009.

## upopo rounds

# Modes / Harmony / The world / Africa

## aka and baka polyphony

Contrapuntal singing in four parts on short ostinatos, each varied and yodelled, the main tune (mòtángòlè) and its response (ósêsê) and two more, overlapping into a dense pentatonic weave. UNESCO 2003.

**From:** Aka and Baka (Central Africa). **Texture:** polyphony.

**Sonorities:** pentatonic unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves in constantly changing combinations.

## horn hocket

An orchestra of horns, each playing one or two pitches at its own moment, so that melody and chords come only from their sum.

**From:** Banda-Linda (Central African Republic). **Texture:** interlocking.

**Sonorities:** the composite line; fourths and octaves where horns coincide.

- [Search Wikipedia for “horn hocket”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=horn%20hocket)
- Arom, African Polyphony and Polyrhythm (1991).

## akan thirds

Call and response with the chorus in parallel thirds; Nketia mapped Africa's harmony in zones, here the thirds, elsewhere fourths, fifths and octaves.

**From:** Akan (Ghana). **Texture:** parallel.

**Sonorities:** parallel thirds, at cadences the unison or octave.

## dorze polyphony

Multipart singing in up to six parts, each with its name and role, on a pentatonic base, overlapping and hocketing, unlike the monophony around it in Ethiopia.

**From:** Dorze (Ethiopia). **Texture:** polyphony.

**Sonorities:** pentatonic fourths, fifths and seconds between interwoven parts.

- [Search Wikipedia for “dorze polyphony”](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=dorze%20polyphony)

## mbira interlocking

Two mbira dzavadzimu: kushaura leads and kutsinhira interlocks, each part itself in two hands, in a cycle of four phrases of twelve pulses whose dyads give a harmonic progression.

**From:** Shona (Zimbabwe). **Texture:** interlocking.

**Sonorities:** dyads of fourths, fifths and thirds, cycling.

## two-root harmony

From the musical bows, uhadi and umrhubhe: two fundamentals a whole tone apart, each with its own overtones, alternate as two 'chords'; the songs, and split-tone umngqokolo singing, follow them.

**From:** Xhosa (South Africa). **Texture:** overtone.
