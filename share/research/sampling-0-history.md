# Sampling

## Sampling

Sampling is making music from recordings: a sound is captured, then played back as an instrument,
at new pitches, in new rhythms and in new places. The recording may be a single note of a violin
meant to stand in for the violin, or two bars of someone else's record meant to be heard as
exactly that. A **sampler** is the instrument that does it: it records or loads sounds, maps them
to keys or pads, and plays them back shaped by envelopes, filters and loops.

The word covers two traditions that grew up together. One is **emulation**: recording real
instruments so a keyboard can play them, from the Mellotron's flutes to today's orchestral
libraries of hundreds of gigabytes. The other is **collage**: taking pieces of existing recordings
and rearranging them, from musique concrète to hip hop, jungle and plunderphonics. The same
machines served both.

PHI has three samplers: **Simplest**, one sound across the keyboard after Ableton's Simpler; the
**Slicer**, a drum machine of 128 pads that cuts a sound into slices; and the **Tracker**, which
plays sample-based modules in the Amiga tradition.

## Tape and musique concrète

Sampling began before the sampler, with recorded sound cut up and rearranged by hand. In Paris in
1948 Pierre Schaeffer made his *Étude aux chemins de fer* from recordings of trains, first on
lacquer discs with locked grooves that repeated a fragment, then on magnetic tape. He called it
**musique concrète**: music made from concrete, recorded sounds rather than from notes on paper.

Tape made the techniques that every sampler later automated: the **loop** (a length of tape
spliced into a ring), playing back at another speed to change pitch and length together, playing
backwards, and cutting and joining fragments. The BBC Radiophonic Workshop (from 1958) built
sound effects and music this way, and Delia Derbyshire realised Ron Grainer's *Doctor Who* theme
(1963) from tape loops and oscillators. The Beatles' "Tomorrow Never Knows" (1966) layers tape
loops played live from several machines; Steve Reich's *It's Gonna Rain* (1965) lets two loops of
one voice drift out of phase.

## The Chamberlin and the Mellotron

The first sampling keyboards played tape. Harry Chamberlin in California built the **Chamberlin**
from the late 1940s: under each key a strip of tape held a recording of a real instrument at that
pitch, and pressing the key pulled the tape across a playback head. A Chamberlin salesman took
the design to Britain, where the Bradley brothers' Streetly Electronics built the **Mellotron**
from 1963.

Each Mellotron key played about eight seconds of tape, then had to be released to rewind: the
first instrument with a fixed sample length, and the reason its players learned to change chords
before the tape ran out. Its flutes, strings and choirs became the sound of late-1960s rock: the
flute intro of the Beatles' "Strawberry Fields Forever" (1967), the strings of the Moody Blues'
"Nights in White Satin" (1967), King Crimson's *In the Court of the Crimson King* (1969).

## The Fairlight and the Synclavier

Digital sampling, sound turned into numbers in memory, arrived in the late 1970s. The
**Fairlight CMI** (Computer Musical Instrument), built in Sydney by Peter Vogel and Kim Ryrie and
released in 1979, was the first commercial sampler a musician could buy, though at the price of a
house. It recorded short sounds at 8 bits, showed their waveforms on a screen drawn with a light
pen, and added a sequencer, Page R, in the Series II. Its factory sound ORCH5, an orchestra hit
from a recording of Stravinsky's *Firebird*, was heard on Afrika Bambaataa's "Planet Rock" (1982)
and Yes's "Owner of a Lonely Heart" (1983), and became a cliché of the decade. Peter Gabriel's
third album and Kate Bush's *Never for Ever* (both 1980) were among the first records made with
it.

New England Digital's **Synclavier**, a digital synthesizer from the late 1970s, added sampling
from the early 1980s and later recording straight to hard disk; it was the even dearer studio
choice of Michael Jackson's producers, Depeche Mode and film composers.

## Samplers for everyone: Emulator, Mirage and Akai

Through the 1980s the price fell from a house to a car to a guitar. E-mu Systems' **Emulator**
(1981) was a keyboard sampler alone, without the Fairlight's computer; the **Emulator II** (1984)
gave 8-bit samples a companding trick that made them sound much better and was all over the
decade's pop and film music. Kurzweil's **K250** (1984) set out to make a piano sound like a
piano. Ensoniq's **Mirage** (1984), under 1,700 dollars, put sampling within reach of working
musicians, and Casio's toy **SK-1** (1985) within reach of children.

Then came the rack samplers that filled studios: Sequential's Prophet 2000, Roland's S-50 and
S-550, and above all Akai's S-series, the **S900** (1986, 12 bits) and the 16-bit **S1000**
(1988), whose disk format became a standard for sample libraries.

## Drum machines and the MPC

Samples made drum machines sound like drums. Roger Linn's **LM-1** (1980) was the first drum
machine with digitally recorded drums, and its successor the LinnDrum was heard on countless
records. E-mu's **SP-12** (1985) and **SP-1200** (1987) let the user sample their own sounds, at
12 bits and about 26 kHz for 10 seconds in all; producers sampled records at 45 rpm and tuned them
down to fit more in, and the grainy, punchy result is the sound of late-1980s and early-1990s hip
hop. Akai and Roger Linn's **MPC60** (1988) put sampling and sequencing behind 16 pads that
responded to how hard they were hit, and the MPC line became the producer's instrument, used for
example on DJ Shadow's *Endtroducing.....* (1996), made almost entirely from samples, and J Dilla's
*Donuts* (2006).

In PHI the Slicer is a pad sampler of this kind, with an MPC-style grid of pads and a step
sequencer. Simplest's Vintage quality plays at 12 bits and 26 kHz as the SP-1200 did, and its
Turntable 45 and 78 settings imitate a record sampled fast and tuned down.

- [E-mu SP-1200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mu_SP-1200) and [Akai MPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akai_MPC), Wikipedia.
- [Drum machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_machine) and [Hip hop production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hip_hop_production), Wikipedia.

## Trackers and the home computer

The Commodore **Amiga** (1985) could play four channels of 8-bit samples in hardware, and Karsten
Obarski's **Ultimate Soundtracker** (1987) used them for music: samples were triggered from a
grid of notes and effects that scrolled down the screen. Its **MOD** file kept the samples and
the patterns together, so a song could be shared as one small file, and the format spread through
the demoscene and early video games. ProTracker, Scream Tracker, FastTracker 2 and Impulse
Tracker followed, with more channels and 16-bit samples, in the S3M, XM and IT formats; Renoise
and MilkyTracker carry the tradition on today.

In PHI the Tracker plays these modules (MOD, XM, IT, S3M and the others MilkyTracker reads)
through MilkyTracker's player.

## Software samplers

From the mid-1990s computers were fast enough to be the sampler. Propellerhead's **ReCycle**
(1994) cut drum loops into slices that followed any tempo. **GigaSampler** (1998) streamed samples
from hard disk instead of loading them into memory, so a single piano could take gigabytes, and
made the deep orchestral library possible. Steinberg's HALion, Emagic's EXS24 (later in Logic),
and Native Instruments' **Kontakt** (2002) followed; Kontakt, with its scripting, became the
format most commercial libraries ship in, from the Vienna Symphonic Library to Spitfire Audio.
FL Studio and Ableton Live put sampling at the centre of the workstation: Live's **Simpler**
(2005), **Sampler** (2006) and **Drum Rack** (2007) made dragging a clip onto an instrument the normal
way to start.

Today a sampler is both a vast library player and a sketchpad, and sample packs sold online
(Splice from 2013) supply loops and one-shots cleared for use. The hardware has come back too,
in standalone MPCs and small samplers such as Elektron's Digitakt and Teenage Engineering's
pocket ones.

In PHI, Simplest follows Live's Simpler and the Slicer Ninjas2, Cubase's Drum Machine and Live's
Drum Rack.

## Sampling and the law

A sample of someone else's record uses two copyrights: the recording (owned by the label) and the
composition (owned by the writers and publishers). In the 1980s hip hop sampled freely; then the
courts caught up. In *Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros.* (1991) Biz Markie was found to have
infringed by sampling Gilbert O'Sullivan, and the Turtles' claim against De La Soul over
*3 Feet High and Rising* was settled. In the United States *Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films*
(2005) held that any sample of a recording, however short, needs a licence ("get a license or do
not sample"), though a later court (*VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone*, 2016) disagreed. In the European
Union the Kraftwerk case *Pelham v. Hütter* (2019) allowed a sample so changed that it cannot be
recognised.

Clearing samples became a business, which is why dense collages like *Paul's Boutique* (1989) are
rarely made now, and why royalty-free sample packs and interpolation (re-recording the passage
instead of sampling it) are common.
