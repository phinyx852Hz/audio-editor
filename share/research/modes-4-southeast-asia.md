# Modes / Southeast Asia

## Central Java

Javanese gamelan has two tunings (laras): sléndro, five notes to the octave in nearly equal
steps, and pélog, seven unequal notes from which five are chosen. Each is divided into three
pathet, modes that fix which notes are strong, how the music cadences and when in a night's
performance it is played. No two gamelans are tuned alike; each has its own embat.

The courts of Yogyakarta and Surakarta, after the division of Mataram (1755), shaped the
classical repertoire. Gamelan came to the West at the Paris Exposition of 1889, where Debussy
heard it; Jaap Kunst's *Music in Java* (1934) and Mantle Hood's programme at UCLA (from 1954)
brought it into scholarship and university ensembles.

- [Gamelan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan), [Slendro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slendro)
  and [Pelog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelog), Wikipedia.
- Sumarsam, *Gamelan: Cultural Interaction and Musical Development in Central Java* (University
  of Chicago Press, 1995).
- [Gamelan.org](https://www.gamelan.org) (American Gamelan Institute).

## Bali

Balinese gamelan uses five-note modes of pélog (selisir above all) and sléndro, played fast,
with interlocking parts (kotekan) split between pairs of players, and with paired instruments
tuned slightly apart so that they beat (ombak, the wave).

The modern gong kebyar, brilliant and sudden, appeared in North Bali around 1915. Colin McPhee,
who lived in Bali in the 1930s, wrote *Music in Bali* (1966); his transcriptions influenced
Britten, and the interlocking technique influenced Reich and Glass.

- [Gamelan of Bali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_gamelan) and
  [Gong kebyar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gong_kebyar), Wikipedia.
- Michael Tenzer, *Gamelan Gong Kebyar: The Art of Twentieth-Century Balinese Music* (University
  of Chicago Press, 2000).

## Sunda (West Java)

Sundanese music uses its own tunings (laras): pélog degung, madenda, sorog and salendro, with
intervals that shift between them, played on gamelan degung, kecapi suling (zither and flute)
and in the tembang Sunda Cianjuran song.

Degung was the gamelan of the regents' courts of the Priangan highlands in the nineteenth
century. Raden Machjar Angga Koesoemadinata described the Sundanese tunings in a 17-tone theory
(1950s).

- [Music of Sunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Sunda) and
  [Degung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degung), Wikipedia.
- Simon Cook, *Sundanese Gamelan Degung* (2008).

## Thailand

Thai classical music is tuned in seven nearly equal steps to the octave (about 171 cents),
from which pentatonic modes are drawn, played by the piphat ensemble of xylophones, gong circles,
oboe and drums, or the mahori and khrueang sai.

It comes from the courts of Sukhothai and Ayutthaya (from the 14th century) and the Bangkok
court after 1782, sharing roots with Khmer music. King Vajiravudh and the Fine Arts Department
(20th century) standardized and recorded it; David Morton's *The Traditional Music of Thailand*
(1976) brought it to Western scholarship.

- [Music of Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Thailand) and
  [Piphat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piphat), Wikipedia.
- David Morton, *The Traditional Music of Thailand* (University of California Press, 1976).

## Cambodia (Khmer)

Khmer classical music uses the same nearly equal seven-step tuning as Thai music, with the
pinpeat ensemble accompanying court dance and ceremony, and the mahori for entertainment.

The reliefs of Angkor Wat (12th century) show its instruments; Ayutthaya took Khmer musicians
in the fifteenth century. Under the Khmer Rouge (1975–79) most musicians were killed, and the
tradition was rebuilt from the few who survived. The Royal Ballet of Cambodia is on UNESCO's list
(2003).

- [Music of Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Cambodia) and
  [Pinpeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinpeat), Wikipedia.
- Sam-Ang Sam, *The Pin Peat Ensemble: Its History, Music and Context* (PhD, Wesleyan, 1988).

## Laos

Lao music centres on the khaen, a free-reed mouth organ of bamboo pipes, played in five modes
(lai) on two pentatonic scales, with drones, accompanying the sung poetry of mor lam.

The khaen is among the oldest free-reed instruments; Chinese and European visitors described it
from the sixteenth century, and it may have inspired the harmonica and the harmonium in Europe.
Khaen music was inscribed by UNESCO in 2017.

- [Khaen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khene) and [Mor lam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor_lam), Wikipedia.
- Terry E. Miller, *Traditional Music of the Lao* (Greenwood, 1985).

## Vietnam

Vietnamese traditional music uses pentatonic modes (điệu) with ornaments and pitch bends that
define them, in court music (nhã nhạc), chamber music (đờn ca tài tử of the south, ca Huế of
the centre), and ca trù, the sung poetry of the north.

Nhã nhạc grew at the courts from the Lý and Trần dynasties and was formalized at the Nguyễn court
of Huế (1802–1945). Trần Văn Khê's research in Paris (from the 1950s) brought the traditions to
international scholarship. Nhã nhạc (2003), ca trù (2009) and đờn ca tài tử (2013) are on
UNESCO's lists.

- [Music of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Vietnam), Wikipedia.
- Phong T. Nguyễn, "Vietnam", *The Garland Encyclopedia of World Music*, vol. 4 (1998).

## Martopangrawit

Raden Lurah Martopangrawit (1914–1986), a musician at the Surakarta court and teacher at the
conservatory (ASKI), wrote the central Javanese theory of gamelan: *Pengetahuan karawitan*
(1969–72), on pathet, cadences (sèlèh) and the inner melody, and many gendhing.

His idea of a melody behind all the parts, sung or imagined, has been central to Javanese and
Western study of gamelan since.

- R. L. Martopangrawit, "Catatan-catatan Pengetahuan Karawitan", trans. Martin Hatch, in Judith
  Becker and Alan Feinstein (eds.), *Karawitan: Source Readings in Javanese Gamelan and Vocal
  Music*, vol. 1 (University of Michigan, 1984).
