# Acoustic Ecology / Noise music

## Noise music

Noise music makes music of what music usually excludes: unpitched sound, distortion,
feedback, overload, extreme loudness and density, with little or no melody, harmony or
regular rhythm. It runs from the Futurists' noise machines through tape music and Fluxus to
the industrial music of the 1970s and the harsh noise of the 1980s onward, and today is an
international underground of small labels, cassettes, CD-Rs and live performance.

Its history is usually told in three strands: art music that admitted noise (Russolo, Varèse,
Schaeffer, Cage); rock and its feedback, from the Velvet Underground to Lou Reed's *Metal
Machine Music* (1975), a double album of guitar feedback; and the noise scenes proper, in
Britain and Europe, the United States and above all Japan, whose noise became so distinctive
that it has its own name, Japanoise.

- [Noise music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_music) and
  [Metal Machine Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_Machine_Music), Wikipedia.
- Paul Hegarty, *Noise/Music: A History* (Continuum, 2007).
- Torben Sangild, *The Aesthetics of Noise* (Datanom, 2002).

## Noise and acoustic ecology

Noise music belongs in acoustic ecology because the field's subject is the whole of what we
hear: wanted sound, which we call music or signal, and unwanted sound, which we call noise,
both within conscious human experience. Schafer himself defined noise as unwanted sound, a
judgement of the listener rather than a property of the sound, and that makes the line
between music and noise a matter of attention. Noise music moves that line: it takes what a
culture has decided not to hear and asks that it be listened to as fully as music.

The two meet and part on this ground. For Schafer, the noise of the industrial soundscape
was a lo-fi drone that masks the voices of a place, and acoustic design its cure; for the
noise musicians, the same sound was material and freedom, an experience of overload, release
or resistance. Jacques Attali's *Noise: The Political Economy of Music* (*Bruits*, 1977;
English 1985) gave the argument its theory: music is the ordering of noise, every order of
music a figure of a social order, and noise the disorder from which a new one comes. Cage's
"wherever we are, what we hear is mostly noise; when we ignore it, it disturbs us; when we
listen to it, we find it fascinating" (1937) states the common ground: listening closely to
noise is already the attention acoustic ecology asks for.

## Industrial music

Industrial music began in Britain in 1975–76 with Throbbing Gristle (Genesis P-Orridge, Cosey
Fanni Tutti, Peter Christopherson and Chris Carter), whose label Industrial Records gave the
genre its name and slogan, "industrial music for industrial people". Their tapes, synthesisers
and treated instruments, played loud with confrontational imagery, set out from the
performance art of COUM Transmissions. Cabaret Voltaire in Sheffield, SPK in Sydney and
Nurse With Wound (Steven Stapleton, from 1978), whose album sleeve listed hundreds of
experimental artists and became a map of the underground, followed.

In West Berlin, Einstürzende Neubauten (from 1980) played scrap metal, power tools and
building sites; in Slovenia, Laibach (from 1980) made industrial music a political theatre.
The industrial scene's tape-trading network of the 1980s carried noise worldwide.

- V. Vale and Andrea Juno (eds), *RE/Search #6/7: Industrial Culture Handbook* (RE/Search, 1983).

## Power electronics and harsh noise

Power electronics is the term William Bennett of Whitehouse (from 1980, London) coined for
their music: screamed vocals over electronic squall and feedback at the limit of loudness,
with extreme subject matter. Whitehouse, Ramleh, Consumer Electronics and the labels Come Org
and Broken Flag made Britain its home, and Sweden (Brighter Death Now, the label Cold
Meat Industry) and the US took it up.

Harsh noise is noise without the vocal or the song: walls of distortion, feedback and
crunch, often made with effects pedals, contact microphones and junk. Its most extreme form,
harsh noise wall, from the 2000s, holds a single dense static texture for a whole piece;
Vomir in France and The Rita in Canada are its best-known makers.

- [Power electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_electronics_(music)),
  [Whitehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehouse_(band)) and
  [Harsh noise wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harsh_noise_wall), Wikipedia.

## Noise in the United States

The American scene grew from the Los Angeles Free Music Society (from the early 1970s), with
Smegma and Le Forte Four, from the no wave of New York (Glenn Branca's guitar symphonies,
Sonic Youth, Swans) and from the noise rock that followed. Through the 1990s and 2000s it
became a nationwide network of basements, cassettes and touring: Wolf Eyes in Michigan
(from 1996, signed to Sub Pop in 2004), Prurient (Dominick Fernow, whose Hospital Productions
is a central label), Kevin Drumm in Chicago, Daniel Menche in Portland, Hair Police, Burning
Star Core and the No Fun Fest in Brooklyn (2004–09), which gathered the American and
Japanese scenes.

- [Noise rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_rock),
  [Los Angeles Free Music Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Free_Music_Society),
  [No wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_wave) and
  [Wolf Eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Eyes), Wikipedia.

## Japanoise

Japanese noise grew from the late 1970s into the most extreme and most influential noise
scene, one the West named Japanoise. Its roots lie in the improvising group Group Ongaku
(Takehisa Kosugi, Mieko Shiomi and others, from 1960), the Fluxus artists of Tokyo, and the
psychedelic free music of the Taj Mahal Travellers and Keiji Haino. Its scene was Osaka's
Kansai underground and Tokyo's, with small venues and labels such as Alchemy (Hijokaidan's
JOJO Hiroshige), and its music, often wordless and of unbroken intensity, was traded on
cassette with the industrial and noise underground abroad.

The ethnomusicologist David Novak argued that Japanoise was made in that exchange: a genre
heard and named across the Pacific, through tapes and tours, as much as in Japan, where it
was always marginal.

- [Japanoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanoise) and
  [Group Ongaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_Ongaku), Wikipedia.
- David Novak, *Japanoise: Music at the Edge of Circulation* (Duke University Press, 2013).

## Merzbow

Merzbow is Masami Akita (born 1956), the best-known noise musician in the world, named after
Kurt Schwitters's *Merzbau*. From 1979 he made tape collages and then, from the late 1980s,
harsh noise of overwhelming density, first with metal junk, contact microphones and effects,
later with laptops, more than four hundred releases in all. The *Merzbox* (2000), fifty CDs,
gathers his first two decades. An animal-rights activist and vegan, he has made many later
works about animals, *Bloody Sea* (2017) against whaling among them, a meeting of noise
music and acoustic ecology's concerns.

- [Merzbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merzbow), Wikipedia.
- [Kurt Schwitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Schwitters), Wikipedia.

## Hijokaidan, Incapacitants, Hanatarash and Masonna

Hijokaidan (from 1979, Osaka), led by JOJO Hiroshige, became notorious for live shows that
destroyed venues and is still active. Incapacitants (Toshiji Mikawa, also of Hijokaidan, and
Fumio Kosakai, from 1981) make some of the loudest noise of all. Hanatarash, Yamantaka Eye's
group of the mid-1980s, once drove a bulldozer through the back wall of a venue; Eye went on to
found Boredoms. Masonna (Maso Yamazaki, from 1987) plays short bursts of screaming noise with
violent physical performance. C.C.C.C. (Cosmic Coincidence Control Center), Aube (Akifumi
Nakajima), MSBR, Government Alpha and Pain Jerk widened the scene in the 1990s; Keiji Haino,
Otomo Yoshihide and KK Null work at its border with rock and improvisation.

## Noise elsewhere

Noise has strong scenes beyond the US, Britain and Japan. In Australia, SPK (from 1978) began
industrial music in Sydney. In Scandinavia, Norway's Lasse Marhaug and Jazkamer, Sweden's
power electronics around Cold Meat Industry, and Finland's Pan Sonic (Mika Vainio and Ilpo
Väisänen), whose minimal electronics border on noise, are prominent. Germany's
Einstürzende Neubauten and the Netherlands' and Belgium's tape and noise labels joined the
industrial network; Mexico, Brazil, Indonesia (Yogyakarta's noise scene, among the largest
in Asia after Japan's) and China (from the 2000s, with Torturing Nurse in Shanghai) have
active scenes of their own.
