# Acoustic Ecology / Noise music

## Noise music

Noise music (or simply noise) is experimental music that uses unwanted sound as its primary
material: distortion, feedback, static, hiss and hum, machine sounds, processed and field
recordings, often at high volume and in long continuous pieces, with little or no melody,
harmony or rhythm. It challenges the distinction between musical and non-musical sound.

Noise as music began with the Italian Futurist Luigi Russolo, whose manifesto *The Art of
Noises* (1913) called on musicians to "conquer the infinite variety of noise-sounds"; he
built noise machines, the intonarumori, and first performed *Risveglio di una città* with
them in 1914. Dada, Fluxus, musique concrète and electroacoustic music explored noise after
him; Edgard Varèse's *Ionisation* (1931), for thirteen players with sirens and a lion's roar,
was the first work organised entirely from unpitched sound, and Varèse asked "What is music
but organized noises?" Robert Ashley's *The Wolfman* (1964) and Pauline Oliveros's *A Little
Noise in the System* (1967) are among the earliest contemporary noise works; the free
improvisers AMM (London, 1965) and the Nihilist Spasm Band (London, Ontario, 1965, the
longest-running self-described noise band) presaged it; and Lou Reed's *Metal Machine Music*
(1975), a double album of guitar feedback, inspired later noise artists, Merzbow among them.

From the late 1970s industrial music, cheap synthesisers and the cassette underground let
non-musicians make noise their whole art; in Japan the scene became so distinctive that it
has its own name, Japanoise. Paul Hegarty dates "noise music proper" from Cage's *4′33″*,
whose incidental sounds stage the tension between desirable sound and undesirable noise.

## Noise and acoustic ecology

Noise music belongs in acoustic ecology because the field's subject is the whole of what we
hear: wanted sound, which we call music or signal, and unwanted sound, which we call noise,
both within conscious human experience. Schafer gave four meanings of noise in *The
Soundscape* (1977): unwanted sound, unmusical sound, any loud sound, and a disturbance in any
signalling system, such as static on a telephone. The first makes noise a judgement of the
listener rather than a property of the sound, and Torben Sangild's third definition says the
same: what is noise to one person can be meaningful to another, and what was unpleasant
yesterday may not be today. Beethoven's *Grosse Fuge* (1825) sounded like noise to its first
audience. Varèse: "to stubbornly conditioned ears, anything new in music has always been
called noise".

Noise music moves that line. For Schafer, the noise of the industrial soundscape was a lo-fi
drone that masks the voices of a place, and acoustic design its cure; for the noise
musicians, the same sound was material and freedom. Cage's "The Future of Music: Credo"
(1937) made no final distinction between noise and musical sound, rain, radio static, "a
truck at fifty miles per hour", and foresaw "a music produced through the aid of electrical
instruments which will make available for musical purposes any and all sounds that can be
heard". Jacques Attali's *Noise: The Political Economy of Music* (*Bruits*, 1977; English
1985) gave the argument its theory: noise in music prefigures social change, the
subconscious of a society testing new realities before they arrive.

## Industrial music

Industrial music, inspired by post-industrial society, emerged in the 1970s from musique
concrète, tape music, noise and sound collage. The term was coined in 1976 by Monte Cazazza
and Throbbing Gristle (Genesis P-Orridge, Cosey Fanni Tutti, Peter Christopherson and Chris
Carter) with the founding of their label Industrial Records, whose slogan was "industrial
music for industrial people". NON and Cabaret Voltaire were among its first artists; by the
late 1970s Clock DVA, Nocturnal Emissions, Einstürzende Neubauten (West Berlin), SPK
(Sydney), Nurse With Wound (Steven Stapleton) and Z'EV had followed.

Its tape-trading network, the cassette underground of the 1980s, carried most noise releases
of the decade around the world. Post-industrial styles grew from it: dark ambient, death
industrial, and power noise (also rhythmic noise), a European style of the 1990s drawing on
dance music and on the Spanish group Esplendor Geométrico.

## Power electronics and harsh noise

Power electronics is the term the English group Whitehouse (William Bennett, from 1980)
coined for their music, screamed vocals over electronic squall and feedback at the limit of
loudness; it became a key influence on contemporary noise.

Harsh noise, from the early 1980s, came out of the Japanese noise scene and European power
electronics: walls of distortion, feedback and crunch without the vocal or the song. Its most
extreme form, harsh noise wall, holds a single dense static texture for a whole piece; Japanese
artists and, in the 1990s, the Haters, Daniel Menche, Vomir (France) and Richard Ramirez
shaped both. Ambient noise wall is its softer, sparser offshoot.

  [Harsh noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harsh_noise) (with harsh noise wall), Wikipedia.

## Noise in the United States

American noise ran from the 1960s underground (the Velvet Underground's "Noise", 1966, and
John Cale and Tony Conrad's drone recordings, after La Monte Young) and the Los Angeles Free
Music Society with Smegma, through New York's no wave, to the Brooklyn noise scene of the
2000s, led by Black Dice and Wolf Eyes. From Brooklyn grew post-noise: in the 2000s and 2010s
the American underground took in kosmische Musik, ambient, drone and new age. Simon Reynolds
credited the Skaters (James Ferraro and Spencer Clark, from 2004) with catalysing an
"international post-noise network"; Oneohtrix Point Never (Daniel Lopatin), Emeralds, Yellow
Swans, Sun Araw, Pocahaunted, Zola Jesus and Laurel Halo followed, and the style fed
hypnagogic pop and vaporwave. The documentary *People Who Do Noise* (Adam Cornelius, 2008)
portrays the Portland scene, with Smegma and Daniel Menche.

## Japanoise

Japanese noise has, since the early 1980s, produced the most characteristically harsh noise
in the world, often called Japanoise. Its roots lie in Group Ongaku, formed in Tokyo on 8 May
1960 by six young musicians including Takehisa Kosugi and Yasunao Tone (later of Fluxus),
whose *Automatism* and *Object* mixed instruments with a vacuum cleaner, a radio, an oil drum
and dishes on speed-shifted tape. Its direct source was the Kansai no wave scene of Osaka,
Kobe and Kyoto in the late 1970s (Aunt Sally, Inu, Ultra Bide's Hide and JOJO Hiroshige, SS),
which drew on New York's no wave.

Hegarty writes that "with the vast growth of Japanese noise, finally, noise music becomes a
genre", mostly from the 1990s; *The Wire* counts the primacy of Merzbow, Hijokaidan and
Incapacitants among noise's main developments since 1990. The ethnomusicologist David Novak
argues that Japanoise was made in exchange across the Pacific, by tapes and tours, as much as
in Japan, where it was always marginal; the CD *The Japanese-American Noise Treaty* (Relapse,
1995) marks that exchange.

## Merzbow

Merzbow is Masami Akita, the best-known Japanese noise artist, who took his name from the
Dada artist Kurt Schwitters's *Merz* collages and his *Merzbau*. In the late 1970s and early
1980s he took *Metal Machine Music* as a point of departure and freed the noise aesthetic from
guitar feedback alone, working first with tape collage, then with metal junk, contact
microphones and effects, later with laptops, in some hundreds of releases. The *Merzbox*
(2000), fifty CDs, gathers his first two decades. A vegan and animal-rights advocate, he has
dedicated many later works to animals.

## Hijokaidan, Incapacitants, Hanatarash and Masonna

Hijokaidan (from 1979, Osaka), led by JOJO Hiroshige, became notorious for chaotic live shows
and later played with Canada's Nihilist Spasm Band. Incapacitants (Toshiji Mikawa, also of
Hijokaidan, and Fumio Kosakai) make some of the loudest noise of all. Hanatarash, Yamantaka
Eye's group of the mid-1980s, was known for destructive performances; Eye went on to found
Boredoms. Masonna (Maso Yamazaki) plays short bursts of screaming noise. C.C.C.C., Solmania,
K2, Government Alpha, Koji Tano, Alienlovers in Amagasaki and the Gerogerigegege widened the
scene, and KK Null and Keiji Haino work at its border with rock and improvisation.

## Noise elsewhere

Beyond the West and Japan, noise has strong scenes of its own. Indonesia's is among the
largest, from Yogyakarta to Samarinda; the Indonesian duo Senyawa joins traditional
instruments to harsh, abrasive sound, and Thailand's Mongoose and the Philippines' Children
of Cathode Ray are pioneers in their countries. India has the New Delhi harsh-noise band
SISTER. In Europe, Spain's Esplendor Geométrico shaped power noise, and Norway (Lasse
Marhaug, Jazkamer), Sweden (the label Cold Meat Industry) and Finland (Pan Sonic) are
prominent; Australia had SPK, and Canada the Nihilist Spasm Band. In the 2020s harsh noise
grew again in Japan, England, Canada, Indonesia and America, some of its artists finding
audiences through social media.
