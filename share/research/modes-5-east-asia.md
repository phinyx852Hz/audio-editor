# Modes / East Asia

## China

Chinese theory builds its twelve pitches, the lǜ, by the "method of adding and subtracting a
third" (sānfēn sǔnyì): pipes or strings two thirds and four thirds as long in turn, which gives
pure fifths and fourths, as in Pythagorean tuning. Five of the notes (gōng, shāng, jué, zhǐ, yǔ)
make the pentatonic scale, and each can be the final, giving the modes.

The method is in the *Guǎnzǐ* (4th–3rd century BCE) and the *Lǚshì chūnqiū* (239 BCE); the bells
of the Marquis Yi of Zeng (433 BCE), found in 1978, are inscribed with the names of pitches and
modes. Because twelve fifths do not close the circle, theorists from Jing Fang to Zhu Zaiyu
sought ways to make them meet.

- [Shí-èr-lǜ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi-er-lu) and
  [Chinese musicology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_musicology), Wikipedia.
- Kenneth Robinson, *A Critical Study of Chu Tsai-yü's Contribution to the Theory of Equal
  Temperament in Chinese Music* (Steiner, 1980).
- Lothar von Falkenhausen, *Suspended Music: Chime-Bells in the Culture of Bronze Age China*
  (University of California Press, 1993).

## Korea

Korean court music uses the twelve yul, taken from the Chinese lǜ, and pentatonic modes such
as pyeongjo and gyemyeonjo; folk music is ordered by regional tori, ways of singing a mode with
its ornaments, vibrato and bent notes, such as the namdo gyemyeon-jo of the southwest.

King Sejong (r. 1418–50) reformed the court music, invented mensural notation (jeongganbo) and
had the instruments retuned by Pak Yeon. The *Akhak gwebeom* (1493) is the great treatise.
Pansori, the sung epic, took form in the eighteenth century and is on UNESCO's list (2003).

- [Traditional music of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_music_of_Korea), Wikipedia.
- Keith Howard, *Perspectives on Korean Music*, 2 vols (Ashgate, 2006).
- Robert C. Provine, *Essays on Sino-Korean Musicology* (Il Ji Sa, 1988).

## Japan

Japanese court music (gagaku) uses six modes (chōshi) from Tang China; later traditions are
built on pentatonic scales with semitones, the miyako-bushi (in scale) of the city and the
koto, and the inaka-bushi (yō scale) of folk song, and the ritsu and ryo scales of Buddhist chant.

Gagaku came from China and Korea between the 7th and 9th centuries and is still played at the
Imperial Household. The koto music of Yatsuhashi (17th century), shakuhachi honkyoku and the
shamisen of kabuki grew in the Edo period. Uehara Rokushirō and Koizumi Fumio gave the modern
theories of the scales.

- [Gagaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gagaku) and
  [Japanese musical scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_musical_scales), Wikipedia.
- William P. Malm, *Traditional Japanese Music and Musical Instruments* (Kodansha, 2000).

## Okinawa (Ryūkyū Kingdom)

Okinawan music uses its own pentatonic scale, C, E, F, G, B (the ryūkyū scale, without the
second and sixth), sung with the sanshin, a three-stringed lute covered in snakeskin, ancestor
of the Japanese shamisen.

The Ryūkyū Kingdom (1429–1879) traded with China and Japan; its court music and dance (kumi
odori, 1719) took from both. The sanshin came from the Chinese sanxian in the fourteenth or
fifteenth century. Kunkunshī, its notation, was set down by Yakabi Chōki (18th century).

- [Music of Okinawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Okinawa) and
  [Sanshin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanshin), Wikipedia.

## Jing Fang

A Han dynasty scholar (78–37 BCE) of the *Book of Changes* who carried the cycle of fifths past
twelve to sixty notes, noting that the 53rd comes back almost exactly to the start (the
difference is the small "Jing Fang comma" of about 3.6 cents).

His sixty-note system tied pitch to the calendar and divination. The 53-note cycle he found is
the 53-tone equal temperament that Nicholas Mercator studied in Europe in the seventeenth century.

- [Jing Fang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jing_Fang) and
  [53 equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53_equal_temperament), Wikipedia.

## He Chengtian

An astronomer and mathematician of the Liu Song dynasty (370–447) who spread the Pythagorean comma
evenly over the twelve pipes, adding the difference in equal parts: an early near-equal
temperament, in lengths rather than ratios.

His method appears in the *Sòng shū* (History of the Song). He also reformed the calendar (443).

- [He Chengtian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_Chengtian), Wikipedia.

## Cai Yuanding

A Southern Song scholar (1135–1198), friend of Zhu Xi, whose *Lǜlǚ xīnshū* (New Book on Pitch)
extended the twelve pitches with six more ("changing pitches", biànlǜ), to let the modes be
transposed on all twelve notes without breaking the cycle of fifths.

His eighteen-note system was the standard Neo-Confucian theory of music until Zhu Zaiyu.

- [Cai Yuanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cai_Yuanding), Wikipedia.

## Zhu Zaiyu

A Ming prince (1536–1611) who calculated twelve-tone equal temperament exactly, to 25 digits,
with the twelfth root of two, in *Lǜlǚ jīngyì* (1596), from work he had finished by 1584, and
*Yuèlǜ quánshū* (1606), and built instruments to it.

He did it some years before Simon Stevin in Europe. His work was presented to the throne but not
adopted; it reached Europe through Jesuit accounts in the eighteenth century.

- [Zhu Zaiyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Zaiyu), Wikipedia.
- Kenneth Robinson, *A Critical Study of Chu Tsai-yü's Contribution to the Theory of Equal
  Temperament in Chinese Music* (Steiner, 1980).

## Yatsuhashi Kengyō

A blind koto master (1614–1685) of the Edo period, credited with the hirajōshi tuning of the
koto, a pentatonic in-scale with semitones, and with the danmono, the first solo koto pieces,
among them *Rokudan no shirabe*.

He took the koto from the court and temple into the music of the townspeople, founding the
zokusō tradition.

- [Yatsuhashi Kengyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yatsuhashi_Kengy%C5%8D) and
  [Hirajōshi scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiraj%C5%8Dshi_scale), Wikipedia.

## Uehara Rokushirō

A Japanese musicologist (1848–1913) whose *Zokugaku senritsu kō* (1895) divided Japanese scales
into two: the miyako-bushi (urban, with semitones, the in scale) and the inaka-bushi (rural,
without, the yō scale).

It was the first modern theory of Japanese scales, and remained the standard until Koizumi Fumio.

- [Japanese musical scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_musical_scales), Wikipedia.

## Yang Yinliu

A Chinese musicologist (1899–1984), the founder of modern Chinese music history. He recorded the
blind musician Abing in 1950, saving *Erquan yingyue* (The Moon Reflected in the Second Spring),
and wrote *Zhongguo gudai yinyue shigao* (Draft History of Ancient Chinese Music, 1981).

He studied the temperaments of the past and the Kunqu opera, and collected the music of temples
and villages.

- [Yang Yinliu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Yinliu), Wikipedia.

## Koizumi Fumio

A Japanese ethnomusicologist (1927–1983) who replaced Uehara's two scales with a theory of
tetrachords: four kinds (min'yō, miyako-bushi, ritsu and Ryūkyū), each a fourth with one note
inside, joined to make the scales of Japanese music (*Nihon dentō ongaku no kenkyū*, 1958).

He did fieldwork from India to Africa and the Arctic, and made world music known in Japan
through radio and television.

- [Fumio Koizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumio_Koizumi), Wikipedia.
